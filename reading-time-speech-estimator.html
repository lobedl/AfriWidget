What this estimator does

This tool takes a block of text and returns four counts and two time estimates. The counts are words, characters, sentences, and paragraphs. The time estimates are a silent reading time and a spoken delivery time. Both are calculated from the word count and a words-per-minute rate, and both rates are configurable so you can match the estimate to the context of the text you are working on.

Word count splits the text on whitespace. Character count includes every character typed, including spaces and punctuation. Sentence count looks for a full stop, question mark, or exclamation mark followed by a space or the end of the text. Paragraph count splits the text on double line breaks. These are the same simple rules that most content management systems, style guides, and word processors use, so the numbers should match what you see elsewhere.

Everything runs inside the browser. The counts and the estimates are calculated locally, and the text you paste never leaves your device. If the AI insight button is pressed, only anonymous counts are sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. The word count, character count, sentence count, paragraph count, and selected rates are the only pieces of information that leave the page.

How reading time is calculated

The silent reading time is the word count divided by the silent reading rate, expressed as minutes and seconds. If you paste a 900-word article and the reading rate is set to 225 words per minute, the estimate is 4 minutes. If you change the rate to 150, the estimate becomes 6 minutes, which better reflects careful reading of a technical document.

Reading time (minutes) = Word count ÷ Reading rate (wpm)

Silent reading time is useful for estimating how long a visitor will spend on an article, how long a document will take to review, or how much content fits a target length. It is the figure most editorial teams use when they plan a page or commission an article. It assumes a comfortable read of ordinary prose by an adult reader with reasonable familiarity with the subject.

How speech time is calculated

The spoken delivery time uses the same formula with a different rate. A typical conversational speaking rate for English falls between 120 and 150 words per minute, with 130 words per minute being a common average for presentations, lectures, and voice-over. A slower, more deliberate delivery sits around 110 words per minute, which is common for accessibility, language learners, and any context where the listener needs extra processing time.

Speech is slower than silent reading because the listener cannot re-read a sentence. In silent reading, a reader can pause, go back, and re-scan a paragraph, and the eye can jump ahead to skim. In speech, the words arrive in real time, and the listener has to hold each idea in working memory until the next one arrives. That is why the same word count produces a longer speech time than reading time, and why a script that reads comfortably on screen can feel rushed when spoken.

Reading speed research

The most widely cited figure for adult silent reading speed comes from a 2019 meta-analysis by Marc Brysbaert, published in the Journal of Memory and Language. The review pooled 190 studies covering around 18,000 participants and reported an average silent reading rate of about 238 words per minute for English non-fiction and about 260 words per minute for fiction.

Individual readers vary widely around that average. The same study reported that a typical range for silent reading of non-fiction runs from about 175 to about 300 words per minute, with slower readers below that range and faster readers above it. Technical material and unfamiliar vocabulary push the effective rate down, while familiar subject matter and light reading push it up. This is why most writing teams use a conservative default such as 200 to 225 words per minute rather than the average of 238, because the conservative figure accounts for the mix of readers a page will actually reach.

A practical rule. If you want the estimate to be safe rather than optimistic, use a rate below the average. A conservative estimate that a reader finishes in three minutes is more useful than an optimistic one that says two minutes and then disappoints the reader.

Choosing the right rate

The right rate depends on what the text is for and who is reading it. Five reading rates are offered here as a starting point.

For speech, three rates cover most contexts. Slow at 110 words per minute fits teaching, accessibility, and any context where the listener needs extra time. Average at 130 words per minute fits presentations, lectures, podcasts, and video voice-over. Fast at 160 words per minute fits familiar content, sports or news updates, and any delivery where the speaker has practised the material enough to move quickly without losing clarity.

Planning an article or a speech

If you are planning an article, decide the target reading time first, then multiply it by the reading rate to get a word count. A four-minute blog post at 225 words per minute is about 900 words. A three-minute news article at 250 is about 750 words. A six-minute technical guide at 150 is about 900 words as well, but the reader is spending more time per word, so the article will feel longer even though the word count is the same.

If you are planning a speech or a video script, decide the target running time, subtract a buffer for pauses, transitions, and any on-screen content, then multiply the remaining minutes by the speech rate to get a word count. A ten-minute presentation at 130 words per minute is about 1,300 words, but if you plan to spend two of those minutes on a demonstration, the script should be closer to 1,040 words. It is easier to trim a script than to pad one, so aim slightly under the target and add material during rehearsal if you need to.

Common mistakes

Assuming every reader reads at the same speed. The average hides a wide range. If your audience includes second-language readers, accessibility needs, or readers on small screens, use a slower rate and expect a longer session.

Confusing silent reading time with spoken delivery time. Speech is slower than silent reading by a wide margin, and a script that reads comfortably on screen may take considerably longer to say aloud. Use the speech estimate for anything that will be spoken, and add a buffer for pauses.

Forgetting about pauses and interaction. A speaker pauses for emphasis, waits for an audience response, and demonstrates a slide. A five-minute speech does not contain five minutes of words; it contains about four minutes of words and a minute of everything else. Build that margin into the plan.

Using words per minute for subtitles. Captioning standards use characters per second rather than words per minute, because subtitle reading speed is tied to the width of the caption and the time it stays on screen. Use the character count from this tool as an input to a proper captioning workflow and follow the accessibility standard for the platform.

Assuming the same rate for every language. Words-per-minute averages are language-dependent. Languages with long compound words show lower words per minute even when the characters-per-minute rate is similar. If you are estimating for a non-English audience, treat the default as a starting point and adjust.

Rounding too early. A 900-word article at 225 wpm is exactly four minutes. A 905-word article is 4 minutes and 1 second. Small differences in word count make small differences in reading time, but they add up over a series of related pages, and a series where every page is slightly over the target will feel long.

Where this tool stops

This estimator produces a planning figure from the text you paste and the rate you select. It does not know the difficulty of the material, the background knowledge of the reader, the device they are using, or the environment they are reading in. It does not model the effect of images, tables, charts, or interactive elements, all of which add time to a read. It does not replace a rehearsal with a stopwatch for any piece of content that will be delivered aloud.

For captions, subtitles, broadcast scripts, and accessibility compliance, use the appropriate standard for the platform rather than this estimate. For legal, medical, or regulated text, use the reading time as one input among several and check the content against the requirements of the audience and the regulator. The responsibility for the final decision belongs to the writer, the editor, or the presenter.

What the tool does give you is a fast, transparent, private way to see the size of your draft, compare reading time and speech time side by side, and check the effect of a different reading rate in a single click. Used as a first pass, it is a practical starting point for anyone who writes, presents, or produces content.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is a reading time estimate?

A reading time estimate is a planning figure, not a promise. It is based on an average words-per-minute rate for a typical adult reader of ordinary prose, and that average hides a wide range of individual speeds. Technical material, unfamiliar vocabulary, second-language reading, small screens, and the presence of charts, tables, or links will slow a reader down. Familiar material, skimming, and reading for pleasure will often be faster. Use the estimate as a starting point and adjust based on what you know about your specific audience and content.

What is the average reading speed for adults?

A large 2019 meta-analysis by Marc Brysbaert, published in the Journal of Memory and Language, pooled 190 studies covering around 18,000 participants and reported a silent reading rate of about 238 words per minute for English non-fiction and about 260 words per minute for fiction. Many writing tools use a round 200 to 225 words per minute as a conservative default because it accounts for the mix of readers a page will actually reach. This estimator defaults to 225 words per minute and lets you adjust the value up or down.

What is a normal speaking rate?

A typical conversational speaking rate for English falls between 120 and 150 words per minute, with 130 words per minute being a common average for presentations, lectures, and voice-over. A very slow, deliberate delivery for accessibility or language learners sits around 100 to 110 words per minute. A fast, familiar reading, such as a news update or a sports commentary, can reach 160 to 180 words per minute. This estimator offers 110, 130, and 160 as presets and lets you choose the one that matches your context.

Why is my real reading time different from the estimate?

Several factors affect real reading time. Longer sentences slow readers down, because the reader has to hold more information in working memory. Technical vocabulary, acronyms, and proper nouns slow the eye down because the reader has to decode them. Small screens and cluttered layouts increase scroll and re-scan time. Distractions, notifications, and multi-tasking split attention. And skim-reading, where the reader looks for a specific answer rather than reading for comprehension, can be much faster than the average. The estimate here describes a comfortable read of ordinary prose and is the figure most writing and product teams use when planning page length.

Is the words-per-minute count the same for every language?

No. Words-per-minute counts depend on the language, because some languages pack more meaning into a shorter string of characters and some use longer words on average. The reading speed research used to set the default here is based primarily on English. Spanish, Italian, and French readers tend to be close to English in word-level speed, while languages with very long compound words such as German and some Nordic languages show lower words-per-minute figures even though the reading rate in characters per minute is similar. Languages with a non-Latin script, such as Arabic, Mandarin, or Hindi, use different baselines altogether. If you are estimating for a non-English audience, treat the default as a rough starting point and adjust.

How do I estimate reading time for a video script or podcast?

For a video or audio script, use the speech time, not the silent reading time. Speech is slower than silent reading by design, because the listener has to process the words in real time and cannot re-read a sentence. Select the appropriate speech rate, then add a buffer for pauses, on-screen graphics, music, and time for the presenter to breathe. As a practical rule, add ten to fifteen per cent on top of the speech estimate to cover the natural pauses a good presenter will use, and add more if the script includes demonstrations, translations, or audience interaction.

Does the estimator count punctuation and numbers as words?

The counter splits the text on whitespace, so any group of characters between two spaces counts as one word. That means a number such as 1500 counts as one word, an email address counts as one word, and a URL counts as one word even though it contains multiple segments. This is the same method most content management systems, style guides, and word processors use for their word count, so the numbers should match what you see elsewhere. If you need to exclude URLs or code from the count, remove them from the text before pasting it in.

How do sentences and paragraphs get counted?

The sentence counter looks for the standard sentence-ending punctuation marks: a full stop, a question mark, or an exclamation mark, followed by a space or the end of the text. Abbreviations such as e.g. or Dr. will inflate the count slightly, because the counter cannot tell them apart from a real sentence end. The paragraph counter splits the text on double line breaks, which is the convention most writing platforms use to mark a new paragraph. If your source uses single line breaks between paragraphs, paste it in with a blank line between each paragraph so the counter can find them.

Is the text I paste sent to a server?

No. The counting and the time estimate run inside your browser, and the text never leaves your device. No request is sent when you type, when the estimator updates, or when you clear the box. The page itself is a static file, so nothing is uploaded and nothing is stored on any server. If you press the AI insight button, only anonymous counts are sent, such as the word count, the character count, the sentence count, the paragraph count, and the selected rates. The actual text is never included in that request.

Can I use the estimate for captions and subtitles?

Only as a rough starting point. Subtitle and caption standards set limits in characters per second rather than words per minute, because reading speed on screen is tied to the width of the caption and the amount of time the frame stays visible. The general guidance from most accessibility standards is a maximum of about seventeen to twenty characters per second for a comfortable read, with a minimum display time of about one second per caption. Use the word and character counts from this tool as inputs to a proper captioning workflow, and check the result with a captioning tool or a broadcast standard.
