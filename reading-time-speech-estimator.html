How reading time and speech duration are estimated

This free text estimator helps writers, teachers, students, presenters, podcasters, video creators, and public speakers understand the size of a draft. Paste text into the box and the page immediately counts words, characters, and sentences. It then shows two planning estimates: silent reading time and spoken speech time. The calculation is performed in the browser, so the text does not need to be uploaded or stored.

Word count is based on groups of characters separated by whitespace. Characters include spaces, punctuation, and line breaks. Sentence count looks for sentence-ending punctuation such as a full stop, question mark, or exclamation mark. These rules are deliberately simple and transparent. Different word processors and publishing platforms can count URLs, hyphenated words, numbers, emojis, and abbreviations differently, so use the method required by the destination when an exact limit matters.

Silent reading time

The silent reading estimate uses 225 words per minute, a useful general benchmark for ordinary adult prose. A 450-word article therefore takes about two minutes at this planning speed. Actual reading time varies widely. Technical writing, unfamiliar vocabulary, second-language reading, small screens, distractions, accessibility needs, and deliberate study can all make the real session longer. A short marketing article may be scanned faster, while a legal document or academic chapter may require rereading and note-taking.

Spoken speech time

Speech duration is calculated from the selected words-per-minute rate. Slow delivery is set to 110 words per minute, average delivery to 130 words per minute, and fast delivery to 160 words per minute. These are useful starting points for rehearsal. A calm keynote, teaching explanation, or voice-over with pauses may fit the slow estimate. A conversational presentation may be close to average, while a brisk reading of familiar material may approach the fast rate.

Remember that a script is not only words. A speaker may pause for emphasis, wait for an audience response, demonstrate a slide, pronounce names carefully, or allow translation. Add rehearsal time for introductions, questions, transitions, music, and visual demonstrations. If a speech needs to fit a strict slot, aim below the maximum and practise aloud with a timer.

Useful planning examples

Students can paste an essay to estimate how long a class presentation would take if read aloud. Teachers can check whether lesson notes fit a period. Content creators can compare a script with a target podcast or video length. Event speakers can test a welcome message before rehearsal. Editors can use the word count and reading estimate when writing a page title, article summary, newsletter, or long-form guide.

For a presentation, divide the total words across sections rather than assuming every slide receives equal attention. For a speech with audience interaction, use the average or slow rate and add a separate buffer. For captions or subtitles, this tool is only a first approximation because viewers need enough time to read each line and because subtitle standards use character-per-second rules as well as word counts.

Planning an exact event slot

If a speech must fit five, ten, or twenty minutes, calculate a safe word range rather than aiming at the last second. Multiply the minutes by the selected rate, then reserve space for pauses and audience interaction. Reading the script aloud with a stopwatch will reveal whether punctuation, slide transitions, names, or demonstrations add extra time. Rehearsal is especially important for multilingual audiences and accessible presentations where a deliberate pace improves understanding.

Privacy and limitations

The estimator is client-side. Text is read by JavaScript in the current tab and is not sent to a database. Do not paste confidential, legally protected, or unpublished material into any online tool unless you have permission. Close or clear the page when you finish if you are using a shared device. The results are estimates, not a guarantee of a reader’s or audience’s exact experience.

Frequently asked questions

What speech rate should I choose?

Use average for a normal presentation, slow for teaching or deliberate delivery, and fast for familiar text. Rehearsing aloud is the best way to refine the estimate.

Why is my real reading time different?

Reading speed changes with content difficulty, language familiarity, attention, accessibility needs, and whether you are studying instead of skimming.

Does the tool save my text?

No. The count and estimates are performed in your browser without an account or server upload.
