What a body fat percentage actually measures

Body fat percentage is the share of your total body weight that comes from fat tissue. It is not the same as body weight, and it is not the same as body mass index. Two people can weigh the same, be the same height, and have very different amounts of muscle and fat, and BMI cannot tell them apart. A body fat estimate adds a second dimension by trying to separate the fat component from the rest of the body, which includes muscle, bone, organs, and water.

That separation matters because fat and muscle play different roles in health. Fat tissue is not inert. It stores energy, insulates, cushions organs, and releases hormones that affect appetite, inflammation, and insulin sensitivity. Too little fat is a health problem, not a fitness goal, and too much fat in the wrong place, particularly around the abdomen, carries a higher risk of cardiovascular and metabolic disease than fat stored elsewhere. A body fat percentage is one way to look at that picture. It is not the only way, and it is not the most important way for everyone.

Whatever number you get from this tool, remember what it is: a screening estimate from a population equation. It is not a measurement of your specific body, and it is not a substitute for a proper clinical assessment. The tool is useful for tracking change over time, for comparing yourself against broad reference bands, and for starting a conversation with a professional. It is not useful as a target to chase or as a reason to change how you eat or train without proper advice.

Where the US Navy formula comes from

The equation this calculator uses was published in 1984 by J. A. Hodgdon and M. B. Beckett at the Naval Health Research Center in San Diego. The study measured hundreds of Navy personnel with both tape measurements and underwater weighing, which at the time was the most accurate field-available method for body composition. The researchers then fit a statistical equation that predicted the underwater-weighing result from a small set of easy-to-take circumferences. The result was two formulas, one for men and one for women, that could be applied in the field with nothing more than a tape measure.

The men's formula uses height, waist, and neck. The women's formula uses height, waist, neck, and hip, because the fat distribution between those sites differs between the two groups. Both equations use logarithms to model the relationship between circumference and body fat, which is why the numbers look unusual at first glance. The logarithm is not cosmetic. It reflects the fact that the relationship between, say, waist size and body fat is not linear: the same five-centimetre increase in waist has a different effect on body fat at a 70 centimetre waist than at a 100 centimetre waist.

In the original study, the men's equation correlated with underwater weighing at roughly 0.90 and the women's at roughly 0.85, with a standard error of about three to four percentage points. That is a good result for a field method, and it is why the Navy method has been used by military, fitness, and public health organisations for four decades. It is also why this calculator uses it rather than a made-up formula. But a standard error of three to four points means that a single reading can be off by that much in either direction, and for an individual, the true value could sit several points above or below what the formula reports.

How to take each measurement correctly

Most of the error in a body fat estimate comes from measurement technique, not from the equation. A few centimetres of tape placed in the wrong spot, a chest pulled in for a photo, or a tape tightened just a bit too much can move the result by one or two percentage points. Getting the technique right matters more than anything else you do with this tool.

Height. Stand without shoes, heels together, back straight, looking forward. Measure against a wall or a doorframe if you do not have a stadiometer. Take the reading at the top of your head, not where your hair sits above it.

Waist. This is the one most people get wrong. The Navy method wants the waist measured at the level of the navel, not at the narrowest point between the ribs and the hips. Keep the tape horizontal all the way around, breathe out normally, and let the tape sit against the skin without pressing in. Do not suck in your stomach or hold your breath. Measure the number where the tape meets itself at the front.

Neck. Measure just below the larynx, with the tape sloping slightly downward at the front. Do not compress the neck. Keep your shoulders relaxed and your head facing forward. If you have a beard, measure through it if necessary, but keep the technique consistent between readings.

Hips. Only required for the female equation. Stand with feet together and measure around the widest horizontal point of the hips and buttocks. Keep the tape level. Do not pull it tight against the body.

A practical tip. Take each measurement twice. If the two readings differ by more than half a centimetre, take a third and use the middle one. A rushed measurement is the single biggest source of error in a screening estimate like this one.

Reading the number without over-reading it

The category label that comes with the estimate is a broad reference band, not a personal verdict. It is derived from ranges that have appeared in body composition guidance from organisations such as the American Council on Exercise and the American College of Sports Medicine, and those ranges themselves are approximations. A person who sits right on the boundary between two bands is not meaningfully different from a person who sits just inside one of them.

Where the number is genuinely useful is in comparison to itself. If your reading six weeks ago was 27 per cent and today it is 24 per cent under the same conditions and the same tape, that is a real change, assuming the measurements were taken consistently. That is the case where the number earns its keep. It is a trend marker, not a score, and it is not worth comparing to a friend who used a different method or a different tape.

The bands are also not universal. Trained athletes often sit at the low end of the athletic range and remain perfectly healthy, while older adults tend to carry slightly more fat for the same level of health. Women carry more essential fat than men at every age because of hormonal and reproductive biology, which is why the reference bands split by sex. Pregnancy and adolescence are two more situations where these bands do not apply, and both are covered in the FAQ below.

A worked example

Take a man who is 178 centimetres tall, with a waist of 88 centimetres and a neck of 39 centimetres. The men's equation is:

Body fat % = 86.010 × log10(waist − neck) − 70.041 × log10(height) + 36.76

First, waist minus neck: 88 − 39 = 49. Then height in centimetres: 178. Now apply the logarithms:

  • log10(49) ≈ 1.6902
  • log10(178) ≈ 2.2504

Multiply: 86.010 × 1.6902 ≈ 145.37. And 70.041 × 2.2504 ≈ 157.61. Add the constant: 145.37 − 157.61 + 36.76 = 24.52. So the estimate is about 24.5 per cent body fat, which sits in the average range for men.

Now take a woman who is 165 centimetres tall, with a waist of 76 centimetres, a neck of 32 centimetres, and hips of 98 centimetres. The women's equation is:

Body fat % = 163.205 × log10(waist + hip − neck) − 97.684 × log10(height) − 78.387

Waist plus hip minus neck: 76 + 98 − 32 = 142. Height: 165. The logarithms:

  • log10(142) ≈ 2.1523
  • log10(165) ≈ 2.2175

Multiply: 163.205 × 2.1523 ≈ 351.27. And 97.684 × 2.2175 ≈ 216.63. Subtract the constant: 351.27 − 216.63 − 78.387 ≈ 56.25. That is well above the normal range and would typically indicate a wrong measurement, since the clamp in the tool caps the displayed value at 60 and flags anything over 50 as suspicious. In practice, a waist-plus-hip-minus-neck of 142 for someone 165 centimetres tall is unusual, and the most likely explanation is that one of the three measurements was taken at the wrong site. Rechecking the waist at the navel line and the hips at their widest point usually brings the result into a sensible range.

Tracking over time without obsessing over a figure

The best use of a body fat estimate is as one signal in a larger picture. If you are training, sleeping well, eating in a way that supports your goals, and feeling good, a body fat reading that moves slowly in the direction you want is a nice confirmation. If you are feeling tired, hungry, or unwell, a body fat reading that moves in the direction you thought you wanted is not a good reason to keep going. The number is not the goal. The number is a measure, and measures are only useful when the thing being measured is what you actually care about.

When you re-measure, do it the same way every time. Same tape, same time of day, same conditions, same sites, same posture. Measure on the same day of the week, ideally not right after a heavy meal or a sauna. Do not measure when you are stressed about the number, because stress changes breathing and posture and pulls the reading away from a neutral one. Three to four weeks is a reasonable interval. Daily measurement is dominated by noise and tends to feed anxiety without adding information.

Write the number down somewhere you will see it again, along with the date and any notes about conditions. A month from now, the pattern across several readings will be far more informative than any single measurement taken in isolation. If the pattern is moving and you feel well, you are probably on the right track. If the pattern is not moving or you are not feeling well, that is worth talking through with a coach, a dietitian, or a doctor.

Who should speak to a clinician first

This calculator is built for adults who are not pregnant, not in recovery from an eating disorder, and not managing a condition that affects body composition. If any of the following apply to you, please skip the tool and talk to a qualified professional first.

  • You are pregnant or breastfeeding. Body composition changes during and after pregnancy and the formula was not designed for it.
  • You are under 18. Adolescents are still growing, and body composition changes rapidly during puberty. Adult reference bands do not apply.
  • You are in recovery from an eating disorder. Any number-driven tracking can interfere with recovery. Follow the guidance from your treatment team instead.
  • You are managing a chronic condition. Diabetes, thyroid disease, kidney disease, and several other conditions affect body composition in ways the formula does not account for.
  • You have significant muscle mass or unusual body shape. Strength athletes, bodybuilders, and some people with unusual circumference distributions fall outside the range where the equation was validated.

If none of those apply, the calculator is a reasonable starting point. If one or more do, please treat the result here as background information and speak with a clinician before you act on it.

Where this calculator stops

This tool does not measure fat directly. It estimates body fat from a small set of circumferences and a population equation, and it does what a screening tool can do, which is to give a rough sense of where you sit and how you are trending. It cannot tell you your visceral fat, your muscle mass, your bone density, your fitness level, your metabolic health, or your risk of any specific disease. Those are questions for a clinical assessment, and often for laboratory tests.

It also cannot compensate for a bad measurement. If you measure in a hurry, or with a tape that has stretched, or at a site that does not match the method, the estimate will be off. The formula is only as good as the numbers that go into it. If your result looks implausible, the first thing to check is your measurements, not the equation. Take them again slowly, and compare against the notes you wrote down from last time.

Finally, it cannot tell you what to do. The reference bands describe where a population sits; they do not describe what your body needs or what goals are appropriate for you. If you want help changing your body composition safely, that is a conversation with a registered dietitian, a qualified coach, or a physician, not with a calculator. Use the tool for what it is, and let the professionals handle the parts it cannot.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the same as a DEXA scan?

No. A DEXA scan is a clinical imaging method that measures bone density, lean tissue, and fat tissue separately using a low-dose X-ray. The Navy circumference method used here estimates body fat percentage from tape measurements and a population equation. The two are not interchangeable. DEXA is more precise and more expensive, and this tool is a screening estimate you can repeat at home for free.

Can I use inches instead of centimetres?

Yes, but you need to convert to centimetres first. Multiply each inch value by 2.54 to get centimetres. For example, a 34-inch waist is about 86.4 cm. The Navy formula is defined in metric units, and mixing units inside the equation will give a wrong result. If you work in inches, convert every measurement before you type it in.

Why does the calculator ask for sex?

Because the original Navy study produced two separate equations, one for men and one for women, with different coefficients and different measurement sites. The men's equation uses height, waist, and neck. The women's equation adds hip measurement because the circumference distribution differs. Choosing a sex is choosing which equation to run, not making a statement about identity.

How accurate is the US Navy method?

In the original 1984 study the equation correlated with underwater weighing at roughly 0.90 for men and 0.85 for women, with a standard error of about 3 to 4 percentage points. That is reasonable for a screening tool but not precise enough for clinical decisions. The main sources of error are tape placement, posture, breathing, and the fact that the equation was fit to a specific military population that may not match your body shape.

Why do the male and female reference bands differ?

Because women carry more essential fat for hormonal and reproductive reasons. A body fat percentage of 22 per cent sits in the fit range for a woman and in the average range for a man. The bands used in this tool come from published body composition guidance and are approximate. They are reference points, not targets, and they do not apply equally to athletes, older adults, or people with particular medical conditions.

What if my waist measurement is smaller than my neck?

The men's formula uses the difference between waist and neck, and if the waist is not larger than the neck the logarithm becomes undefined and the estimate cannot be produced. This usually means one of the two measurements was taken at the wrong site. Recheck the waist at the navel line and the neck just below the larynx, without pulling the tape tight, and try again.

How often should I re-measure?

Every three to four weeks is enough for tracking trends. Daily measurement is dominated by noise from hydration, food, and posture, and it tends to create anxiety without adding information. Keep the technique consistent, measure at the same time of day, and compare results only against your own previous measurements taken the same way.

Is a lower body fat percentage always better?

No. Every body needs a minimum amount of fat to function. Below the essential fat threshold, hormone production, immune response, bone density, and menstrual cycles in women can all be affected. Very low body fat is not automatically healthy, and for many people the goal is a sustainable range rather than the lowest number they can reach. Extreme dieting to chase a number on a calculator is not a good idea.

Does the estimate work for athletes?

It can, but with more error than for the general population. The original equation was fit to a military sample, and athletes with dense muscle mass and unusual circumference distributions can fall outside the range where the formula was validated. Strength athletes often see an overestimate, and endurance athletes often see a closer match. Use the trend over time rather than the absolute number.

Should I use this if I am pregnant, under 18, or in recovery from an eating disorder?

No. The formula was not designed for pregnant bodies, growing adolescents, or anyone in recovery from an eating disorder, and using it in those situations can produce misleading numbers and unnecessary distress. Please speak with a qualified clinician before tracking body composition in any of those cases. They can advise on what measurements are appropriate, if any.

Does the tool send my measurements anywhere?

No. All the arithmetic runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the sex, the computed body fat percentage, the category band, and the rounded input measurements are sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. No name, no email, no identifying information.
