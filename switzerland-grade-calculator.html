What this calculator does

This tool does three jobs. In the first mode, it takes a set of Swiss university courses, weights each grade by the ECTS credits attached to that course, and returns a credit-weighted semester average together with its international equivalents. In the second mode, it converts a single Swiss mark to the US 4.0 GPA, German Notensystem, ECTS letter grade, French 0–20 scale, and UK honours classification. In the third mode, it audits a Gymnasiale Matura record against the two federal pass conditions.

The point of the tool is not to work out the arithmetic that a student could do by hand. It is to make the two things that Swiss students most often get wrong visible at the same time: the difference between an unweighted and a credit-weighted average, and the difference between the Swiss 1.0–6.0 scale and the German 1.0–5.0 scale that runs in the opposite direction. A student who assumes a Swiss 5.5 is a German 1.5, or who averages their grades without weighting them by ECTS, will be wrong on both counts.

Everything runs inside your browser. No grade, no course name, and no personal detail is uploaded. If the AI insight button is pressed, only an anonymous summary of the mode, the number of subjects, the weighted average, the ECTS total, and the pass status is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

The Swiss 1.0 to 6.0 scale

Switzerland grades on a 1.0 to 6.0 scale where 6.0 is the highest mark and 4.0 is the minimum passing mark. The scale is unusual in Europe because it runs in the same direction as the letter-grade systems used in the United States and the United Kingdom, and in the opposite direction to the German and Austrian systems. A Swiss 5.5 is a strong result; a German 5.5 is a failure.

Swiss universities award marks in quarter-point steps (5.25, 5.75) more often than in half-point steps, and the percentage bands shown here are the ones most often applied by credential evaluation services outside Switzerland. Inside Switzerland itself, the percentage equivalents are not used. What matters is the position of the mark on the 1.0–6.0 scale and, for ECTS letters, the position of the mark within the cohort.

ECTS credit-weighted averages

The European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System attaches a credit value to every course. At Swiss universities, a standard semester is worth around 30 ECTS credits, and a full academic year is worth 60. A single course typically carries between three and ten credits depending on its contact hours and its assessment load. The weighted average is what matters, because a strong mark in a ten-credit course moves the total four times further than the same mark in a two-and-a-half credit seminar.

Weighted Swiss average = Σ (course grade × ECTS credits) ÷ Σ (ECTS credits)

The difference between a weighted and an unweighted average is not academic. A student who takes four courses worth five credits each and one laboratory course worth ten credits will see the laboratory course dominate the weighted average. If the laboratory mark is weak, the weighted average falls sharply even though the four smaller courses are all strong. Running the calculation through the tool shows that outcome before the transcript is finalised, which is usually enough to change where the student puts their effort.

ETH Zurich and EPFL block compensation

The two Federal Institutes of Technology use a block examination system for the first year. Instead of assessing each subject separately, ETH Zurich and EPFL group first-year subjects into a Basisprüfung block, and the block is passed or failed as a whole.

The rule is straightforward. The block passes as long as the credit-weighted average of every subject in the block is at least 4.00. Individual marks below 4.00 do not automatically fail the block, provided the stronger subjects compensate. A 3.75 in Linear Algebra can sit inside a passing block if the credit-weighted average of the whole block is still above 4.00. The exact rules on how many below-4.0 marks can appear, and whether any subject has a hard minimum, are set by each institution's examination regulations and are worth reading carefully before the first examination session.

Compensation is not a licence to coast in the biggest course. Because the block average is credit-weighted, a low mark in a high-credit subject pulls the average down proportionally more than a low mark in a small one. A student who fails a ten-credit course needs substantially stronger results elsewhere to reach the 4.00 line than a student who fails a two-credit seminar.

Converting Swiss grades internationally

Four conversions matter most for Swiss students applying abroad.

German Notensystem. German universities use the Modified Bavarian Formula for Swiss applicants. The formula maps the Swiss 4.0–6.0 passing range onto the German 1.0–4.0 passing range, with the German value inverted: German grade = 1 + 1.5 × (6.0 − Swiss grade). A Swiss 6.0 becomes a German 1.0, a Swiss 5.0 becomes a German 2.50, and a Swiss 4.0 becomes a German 4.00. Anything below a Swiss 4.0 is treated as a failure on both scales.

US 4.0 GPA. There is no official federal formula for converting Swiss grades to a US GPA. Credential evaluation services publish their own tables, and the same Swiss mark can map to slightly different GPA values across services. The most commonly cited mapping treats a Swiss 5.75 to 6.00 as a 4.00, a Swiss 5.25 to 5.50 as roughly a 3.70 to 3.90, a Swiss 4.75 to 5.00 as roughly a 3.30 to 3.50, and a Swiss 4.00 as a 2.00. This calculator uses that published band.

ECTS letter grade. ECTS letters describe a student's rank within a cohort rather than an absolute mark. A is the top 10 per cent of passing students, B the next 25, C the next 30, D the next 25, and E the final 10. Because the letter is relative, the same Swiss mark can map to different letters at different institutions if the cohort is unusually strong or weak. The bands shown in this tool are the ones most commonly used at Swiss universities, but the transcript issued by your institution is the authority.

French 0–20 scale. French universities and the French Baccalauréat use a 0 to 20 scale where 10 is the passing mark and 20 is the maximum. The linear conversion most often applied to Swiss applicants is: French grade = 10 + 10 × ((Swiss grade − 4.0) ÷ 2.0), which maps a Swiss 6.0 to a French 20 and a Swiss 4.0 to a French 10.

The Swiss Matura pass conditions

The Swiss federal Matura certificate, the Maturitätsausweis, is awarded on two conditions that must both be met. The conditions are set out in the federal Matura regulations and applied by every canton.

The first condition is that the arithmetic average of every examined subject must be at least 4.00. This is a straightforward mean across all the Matura subjects, unweighted. The second condition is the double-deficit rule. The sum of all grade points above 4.0 must be at least twice the sum of all grade points below 4.0. In other words, a candidate whose strongest subjects carry twice the point surplus of their weakest subjects passes, even if one or two subjects are below the pass threshold.

Condition 1: mean of all Matura grades ≥ 4.00
Condition 2: Σ (grade − 4.0) for grades above 4.0 ≥ 2 × Σ (4.0 − grade) for grades below 4.0

The double-deficit rule is what stops a candidate with a strong average but a concentrated pattern of failures from slipping through. A student with four strong subjects and four weak subjects might still pass condition one on the strength of the average, but the double-deficit test forces a hard look at whether the surplus is large enough to cover the deficit twice over. The Matura pass audit mode in this tool runs both conditions and shows exactly where the arithmetic sits.

A worked example

Take a first-year mechanical engineering student at ETH Zurich sitting the Block 1 Basisprüfung across four subjects. The subjects, credits, and marks are: Analysis I and II at eight ECTS and a mark of 4.75; Physics I at six ECTS and a mark of 5.25; Linear Algebra at four ECTS and a mark of 3.75; Mechanics I at six ECTS and a mark of 5.00.

The weighted quality points are 4.75 × 8 = 38.0, 5.25 × 6 = 31.5, 3.75 × 4 = 15.0, and 5.00 × 6 = 30.0. The total is 114.5 across 24 ECTS credits, giving a credit-weighted Swiss average of 4.77. That is above the 4.00 pass line, so the block passes despite the 3.75 in Linear Algebra. The compensation mechanism does its job.

Converting the 4.77 average, the German Notensystem result is 1 + 1.5 × (6.0 − 4.77) = 2.85, which is a solid befriedigend. The US GPA equivalent sits in the 3.30 to 3.50 band, which is a B+ under most evaluation tables. The ECTS letter is a C, which is where the top 40 per cent of the passing cohort sits. The French 0–20 equivalent is 13.85.

Now suppose the same student had earned a 4.25 in Linear Algebra instead of a 3.75. The quality points become 4.25 × 4 = 17.0, the total becomes 116.5, and the weighted average moves to 4.85. The German grade improves to 2.73, the US GPA band moves closer to the top of the B range, and the ECTS letter stays at C. The difference is small but real, and it illustrates how a single mark in a mid-credit course moves the total by a fraction rather than by a step.

Common misunderstandings

Treating the Swiss and German scales as compatible. They are not. A Swiss 5.5 is a very good result; a German 5.5 is a failure. The two scales run in opposite directions, and they are also anchored differently: the Swiss minimum pass is 4.0 out of 6, the German minimum pass is 4.0 out of 5 with 1.0 at the top.

Using an unweighted average. Swiss transcripts report credit-weighted averages, and the difference can be a full quarter-point in either direction depending on how the credits are distributed. Averaging the raw marks gives a different number from the one on the transcript.

Assuming a low mark in one course fails the whole block. At ETH Zurich and EPFL, first-year blocks pass on the credit-weighted average, not on the individual marks. Whether a specific below-4.0 mark is compensable depends on the institution's examination regulations, but the general principle is that compensation is available.

Reading the Matura double-deficit rule as a soft preference. It is a hard condition. A candidate whose positive surplus is less than twice the deficit fails the Matura regardless of how strong the overall average is. The audit tool in this calculator shows the two sums side by side so the arithmetic is easy to check.

Assuming there is one official Swiss-to-US GPA conversion. There is not. Different credential evaluation services publish slightly different tables, and graduate admissions offices sometimes apply their own. The band shown in this tool is a reasonable reference point, not a guarantee of what a given programme will decide.

Confusing the ECTS letter with an absolute mark. The ECTS letter describes a student's position in a cohort. Two students with the same Swiss mark at different institutions may receive different ECTS letters if the cohorts are of different strengths. The letter is a relative measure, not an absolute one.

Where this calculator stops

This tool produces conversions and weighted averages from the numbers you enter. It does not know your institution's exact examination regulations, its specific ECTS-to-grade mapping, or the exact conversion table that a particular graduate admissions office will apply. It does not decide whether you have passed a block or been awarded the Matura. Those decisions belong to your university's examination board and to the cantonal education authority that issues the Matura certificate.

The conversions shown here are the ones most widely used for Swiss students applying abroad, and they are consistent with the Modified Bavarian Formula, the ECTS Users' Guide distribution bands, and the published tables from credential evaluation services. Where an institution publishes its own conversion table, that table governs, and the numbers here should be treated as a reference point rather than the final answer.

What the tool does give you is a fast, private way to see where a Swiss mark sits on the scales that matter outside Switzerland, and to see how a set of semester marks combines into a credit-weighted average. That view is useful for planning, for applications, and for understanding the transcript you already have in hand.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as a good grade at a Swiss university?

Swiss universities use a 1.0 to 6.0 scale where 6.0 is the highest mark and 4.0 is the minimum passing mark. A grade of 5.0 is a solidly good result, 5.5 is very good and typically sits in the top 10 to 15 per cent of a cohort, and marks between 5.75 and 6.0 are reserved for exceptional performance. Swiss grade distribution is narrower than in many other systems, so a 5.0 average is a strong outcome rather than an average one.

What is the minimum passing grade in Switzerland?

The minimum passing grade at every level of the Swiss education system is 4.00. This applies to the Gymnasiale Matura, to vocational qualifications, and to every university and Federal Institute of Technology. Any mark below 4.0 is classified as ungenügend, insuffisant, or insufficiente, which means failed.

How does the ETH Zurich and EPFL block compensation system work?

First-year subjects at ETH Zurich and EPFL are grouped into examination blocks, called the Basisprüfung. A student does not have to pass every subject individually at 4.0 or above. Instead, the block is passed as long as the credit-weighted average of every subject in the block equals or exceeds 4.00. One or two marks below 4.0 can be offset by stronger results elsewhere in the block. The exact compensation rules are set by each institution's examination regulations.

How do Swiss grades convert to the German grading system?

German universities use the Modified Bavarian Formula for Swiss applicants. The formula is: German grade = 1 + 1.5 × (6.0 − Swiss grade). A Swiss 6.0 becomes a German 1.0, a Swiss 5.5 becomes a 1.75, a Swiss 5.0 becomes a 2.50, a Swiss 4.5 becomes a 3.25, and a Swiss 4.0 becomes a 4.00, which is the minimum passing grade in Germany as well.

How do Swiss grades convert to a US 4.0 GPA?

There is no single official formula for converting Swiss grades to a US 4.0 GPA. Credential evaluation services such as WES and AACRAO apply conversion tables that map Swiss marks through percentage bands. A commonly published mapping treats a Swiss 5.75 to 6.00 as a 4.00, a Swiss 5.25 to 5.50 as roughly 3.70 to 3.90, a Swiss 4.75 to 5.00 as roughly 3.30 to 3.50, a Swiss 4.25 to 4.50 as roughly 2.70 to 3.00, and a Swiss 4.00 as a 2.00. Anything below 4.00 is treated as a failing grade and maps to 0.00.

What are the two Swiss Matura pass conditions?

The Swiss federal Matura certificate requires two conditions to be met. First, the arithmetic average of all examined subjects must be at least 4.00. Second, the sum of positive grade points above 4.0 must be at least twice the sum of the deficit points below 4.0. This second condition is often described as the double-deficit rule, and it is what stops a strong overall average from rescuing a candidate whose failures are concentrated in the wrong subjects.

How do Swiss grades map to ECTS letter grades?

ECTS letter grades describe a student's position in a cohort rather than an absolute mark. Grade A is the top 10 per cent of passing students, B is the next 25 per cent, C is the next 30 per cent, D is the next 25 per cent, and E is the final 10 per cent. At Swiss universities, an A usually corresponds to a mark of about 5.75 to 6.0, a B to about 5.25 to 5.5, a C to about 4.75 to 5.0, a D to about 4.25 to 4.5, and an E to 4.0. Anything below 4.0 is graded FX or F and must be retaken.

Does this calculator store my grades?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the mode, number of subjects, weighted average, ECTS total, and pass status is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Course names, individual marks, and any personal identifiers never leave the browser.
