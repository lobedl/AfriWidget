What this calculator does

This tool takes two numbers, your ICASS term mark and your national examination mark, and returns three things: the weighted composite score that DHET records on your statement of results, a pass or fail audit against the 40 per cent examination sub-minimum rule, and the classification band that determines whether the subject counts as a distinction, a standard pass, a condoned pass, or a rewrite. It applies the standard 40:60 ICASS and examination split used across Engineering Studies N1 to N6 and Business Studies N4 to N6 at public and private TVET colleges in South Africa.

The arithmetic is short, but the sub-minimum rule is what trips up a lot of students. A candidate with a very strong ICASS mark and a weak exam mark can end up with a weighted total well above 40 per cent and still be recorded as a fail, because the exam paper has its own threshold that cannot be waived. This calculator makes that second check explicit, so the outcome you see matches what DHET would record.

Every calculation runs inside your browser. No mark, no subject name, and no personal detail is uploaded. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the ICASS mark, exam mark, weighted composite, sub-minimum status, and classification band is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

How NATED Report 191 assessment works

The National Accredited Technical Education Diploma, known as NATED or Report 191, is the older vocational qualification stream offered at TVET colleges across South Africa. It is administered by the Department of Higher Education and Training and quality-assured by Umalusi. The qualification is modular: each subject is assessed independently, and the final mark for each subject is what determines whether you progress to the next N-level and eventually towards the National N Diploma.

Two components feed into that final mark. The first is ICASS, which stands for Internal Continuous Assessment, and it is built from the classroom tests, practical tasks, assignments, and internal trial examinations that your lecturer runs during the trimester or semester. The second is the national examination, which is set, administered, and moderated by DHET across the whole country. The two components are combined using a 40:60 weighting, with ICASS contributing 40 per cent and the national examination contributing 60 per cent.

That split is deliberate. It gives the college a meaningful say in the assessment through continuous classroom work, while still anchoring the qualification in a standardised national examination that can be compared across every TVET college in the country. A candidate who performs well in both components is in a strong position. A candidate who performs well in only one is limited by the other.

The ICASS component explained

ICASS is the internal continuous assessment mark that your college assembles across the trimester or semester. It is not a single test. It is a composite of everything you were assessed on during the period: class tests, practical vocational tasks, written assignments, oral presentations, projects, and internal trial examinations. The college weights those individual pieces according to the subject assessment plan published at the start of the term, and produces one final ICASS percentage out of 100.

The ICASS mark carries 40 per cent of the final NATED subject mark. That is a substantial weight, and it means the classroom work is not a formality. A student who treats the term as preparation for the exam alone and neglects assignments can arrive at the exam with an ICASS mark in the 40s, which caps the best possible final composite. A student who consistently performs in the classroom can arrive at the exam with an ICASS mark in the 70s or 80s and enter the examination with a real buffer.

There is also a gatekeeping function. DHET requires that a candidate hold a valid ICASS mark before being admitted to write the national examination. In most colleges the internal threshold is 40 per cent on the ICASS component, though some occupational and practical subjects use a higher baseline. If your ICASS mark falls below the college threshold, you may not be issued with an examination admission permit, and the national examination cannot be written until the internal requirement is satisfied.

Validity window for rewrites. An ICASS mark remains valid for one calendar year, which usually covers two consecutive national examination cycles. If you fail the exam on the first attempt, you can carry the same ICASS mark into the next sitting without repeating the classroom assignments. Once that window closes, the ICASS expires and a fresh internal mark has to be built by re-registering the subject.

The national examination component explained

The national examination is set and moderated by DHET and is written on the same date across every TVET college in South Africa. That uniformity is what makes the NATED qualification portable: a pass obtained at one college carries the same weight as a pass obtained at another, because the examination paper is the same. The paper is written under examination conditions at a registered centre, and the scripts are marked externally or under external moderation depending on the subject and level.

The national examination carries 60 per cent of the final subject mark. That weighting is significantly higher than the ICASS component, and it reflects the fact that the examination is the only truly comparable instrument across the whole country. A strong exam performance can lift a mediocre ICASS mark into a pass. A weak exam performance can drag a strong ICASS mark into a fail, especially when the sub-minimum rule applies.

The examination is also the component that determines your distinction standing. DHET awards a distinction when the weighted composite reaches 75 per cent or higher and the exam sub-minimum is met, but in practice the composite is heavily driven by the examination because of the 60 per cent weighting. A candidate aiming for a distinction typically needs an exam mark in the mid-80s to comfortably clear the 75 per cent composite threshold, even with a strong ICASS mark behind them.

The 40% exam sub-minimum rule

Every NATED subject has two pass conditions, and both must be satisfied for the subject to be recorded as a pass. The first is the weighted composite reaching 40 per cent or higher. The second is the national examination mark itself reaching 40 per cent or higher. The second condition is called the sub-minimum rule, and it is the single most common reason why a student with a respectable weighted total still ends up with a fail on the statement of results.

The rule exists because DHET wants to protect the credibility of the qualification. A candidate who performed very strongly in continuous assessment but wrote a weak national examination paper has not demonstrated the same level of competence as a candidate who wrote a strong paper, and the sub-minimum rule keeps the examination as a meaningful gate. It means that no matter how well the internal mark is assembled, the external examination remains the final check.

In practical terms, the rule usually affects candidates in one of two situations. The first is the candidate who scored in the low 30s on the exam paper. Even a very high ICASS mark cannot pull that candidate over the line, because the exam mark is below the threshold that DHET will accept regardless of the composite. The second is the candidate who scored between 37 and 39.99 on the exam paper and is close enough to the threshold to consider a remark. Those borderline cases are the ones that a formal remark application occasionally converts into a pass, because a small upward adjustment on the exam mark can take it over the 40 per cent line and the composite is then recomputed with the corrected figure.

Composite = (ICASS × 0.40) + (Exam × 0.60)
Pass conditions:
  1. Composite ≥ 40.0
  2. Exam mark ≥ 40.0
Both conditions must hold.

Classification bands and what they mean

Once the composite is calculated and the sub-minimum is audited, DHET places the outcome into one of several bands. Each band has a different meaning for your academic record and your progression towards the National N Diploma.

BandCompositeExamOutcome
Distinction75.00 to 10040+Full credit, eligible for merit standing
Standard Pass50.00 to 74.9940+Full credit, clear progression to next N-level
Condoned Pass40.00 to 49.9940+Subject credit meets the baseline threshold

Composite FailBelow 40AnyRepeat the subject or apply for a supplementary

The distinction band is a meaningful target because it is what most N6 candidates aim for when they are hoping to be recommended for graduate programmes or for jobs where the certificate is presented alongside a transcript. The standard pass band is where most successful candidates sit. The condoned pass band is the fallback: the subject counts as a credit but does not carry merit standing, and some institutions treat it as weaker evidence of competence when reviewing progression to the next level.

The two fail bands are separated for a reason. An exam sub-minimum fail requires only the examination to be rewritten, using the same valid ICASS mark, provided the ICASS is still within its validity window. A composite fail requires the subject to be repeated in full, which means re-registering and building a fresh internal mark alongside the examination. The distinction between the two matters because the first route is much faster, and candidates who understand the difference can plan their recovery realistically.

A worked example across three candidates

Three candidates at the same TVET college each wrote a national examination paper in an N4 Business Studies subject. Each carried a different combination of ICASS and exam marks, and each ended up in a different band.

CandidateICASSExamCompositeOutcome
Candidate A58.062.060.40Standard Pass
Candidate B78.036.052.80Exam Sub-Minimum Fail
Candidate C84.088.086.40Distinction

Candidate A is the ordinary case. The ICASS contribution is 58.0 multiplied by 0.40, which is 23.20, and the exam contribution is 62.0 multiplied by 0.60, which is 37.20. The composite is 60.40, comfortably inside the standard pass band, and the exam mark of 62 is well above the 40 per cent sub-minimum. The subject counts as a full credit and Candidate A can register for the next N-level in the normal way.

Candidate B is the case that catches people out. The ICASS mark of 78 is strong, and the ICASS contribution of 31.20 is above average. But the exam mark of 36 is below the 40 per cent sub-minimum. The weighted composite of 52.80 is well above 40 and would look like a comfortable pass on the arithmetic alone. DHET records the subject as a fail regardless, because the exam condition was not met. The good news is that Candidate B can carry the ICASS mark into the next examination cycle and rewrite only the national exam paper, provided the ICASS is still within the one-year validity window.

Candidate C is the case that runs cleanly from start to finish. The ICASS contribution of 33.60 and the exam contribution of 52.80 add to a composite of 86.40, well into the distinction band, and the exam mark of 88 clears the sub-minimum without difficulty. Candidate C receives distinction standing for the subject and, once the N6 level is completed and the workplace experiential training logbook is verified, is eligible for the National N Diploma.

Common mistakes that turn a pass into a rewrite

A handful of recurring errors turn an otherwise successful NATED subject into a rewrite. Understanding them in advance is worth more than any single study session.

Treating the ICASS mark as a formality. Some candidates coast through the classroom work and intend to catch up in the exam. Because ICASS carries 40 per cent, that strategy is mathematically weaker than it looks. A candidate with an ICASS mark of 40 needs an exam mark of at least 40 just to reach the composite threshold, and realistically needs 50 or more for a comfortable pass. A candidate with an ICASS mark of 70 can absorb a much weaker exam and still land in the standard pass band.

Underestimating the sub-minimum. The sub-minimum rule catches candidates who focused everything on the composite and assumed a strong internal mark would cover a weak exam paper. It does not. If there is any realistic chance that the exam mark could fall in the low 30s, the exam deserves direct, targeted preparation regardless of how the ICASS has gone.

Missing the remark window. Candidates who scored between 37 and 39.99 on the exam paper and did not realise a remark was available often discover too late that they missed the deadline. DHET publishes the remark window after each national results release, and it is a short window. If the exam mark is anywhere near the threshold, lodge the remark.

Assuming the ICASS mark lasts indefinitely. The validity window is one calendar year, which usually covers two consecutive examination cycles. A candidate who intends to rewrite but delays past that window will need to re-register the subject from scratch, which means a full trimester or semester of classroom work before the next sitting. Plan the rewrite inside the window.

Ignoring rounding. DHET rounds the final composite to the nearest whole percentage point. A candidate sitting on 39.5 or 49.5 can move up by a full point depending on the exact arithmetic. A candidate sitting on 39.4 does not round up. The calculation is unforgiving at the boundary, and this tool shows the unrounded figure so the rounding outcome is visible in advance.

Confusing NATED with NCV. The two qualification streams look similar from the outside but are assessed under different frameworks and have different progression routes. A candidate who applies the wrong rules to their own results can misjudge the outcome. NATED is the Report 191 stream, assessed under the 40:60 ICASS and examination model described on this page. NCV is a separate three-year vocational qualification with its own assessment architecture.

From N6 certificate to the National N Diploma

Passing the N6 subjects is not the end of the NATED pathway. The N6 certificate is the instructional portion of the qualification, but the fully accredited National N Diploma at NQF Level 6 requires one more element: verified workplace experiential training.

Business Studies candidates need a minimum of 18 months, or 2,000 verified hours, of relevant in-service workplace experience. Engineering Studies candidates need a minimum of 24 months, or 2,670 verified hours, of relevant industrial or trade experience, typically completed under certified mentorship. The experience is logged in the official DHET logbook, signed off by the employer, and submitted to the college for verification before being forwarded to the DHET National Diploma Directorate.

Each subject pass along the way contributes to that end goal. A distinction or a standard pass both count as a full subject credit. A condoned pass also counts, though it carries weaker merit standing. An exam sub-minimum fail does not count until the examination is rewritten and passed, and a composite fail does not count until the subject is repeated in full. The N6 certificate is only issued once all required subjects at N4, N5, and N6 levels have been passed, so the pathway is cumulative and each level depends on the one before it.

Where this calculator stops

This tool estimates the final NATED subject mark from the ICASS and examination marks you enter, applying the standard 40:60 weighting and the 40 per cent examination sub-minimum rule. It does not know your college's specific ICASS weighting plan for the subject, does not know whether any occupational or practical component uses a different threshold, and does not know whether your ICASS mark is still within its validity window. It also does not account for the official remarking process or the effect of any adjusted mark that may come out of it.

The classification bands used here match the published DHET Report 191 framework. The final, official outcome is the one recorded on the statement of results issued by DHET after the examination scripts have been marked and moderated, and that statement is the authoritative document for any application to the next N-level, to the National N Diploma, or to an employer. Use this calculator to plan realistically, to see where the composite sits relative to the pass bands, and to understand what a change in the exam mark would do to the outcome before results are released.

Frequently asked questions

How is the final NATED subject mark calculated?

The DHET Report 191 framework weights two components. Your ICASS term mark counts for 40 per cent of the final subject mark, and your national examination mark counts for 60 per cent. The composite is calculated as (ICASS mark times 0.40) plus (national exam mark times 0.60). The result is expressed as a percentage out of 100 and is the figure your college records on your statement of results.

Why can a weighted total above 40 per cent still be a fail?

Because DHET applies a two-condition pass rule. You must reach 40 per cent on the weighted composite, and you must also reach 40 per cent on the national examination paper on its own. A strong ICASS mark cannot rescue a weak examination mark. If your examination mark is below 40 per cent, the subject is recorded as a sub-minimum failure even if your weighted total is well above 40 per cent.

What ICASS mark do I need before I am allowed to write the exam?

Each TVET college sets an internal threshold for exam admission, and DHET requires that you hold a valid ICASS mark before you may sit the national examination. In most colleges the threshold is 40 per cent on the ICASS component, though some occupational and practical subjects use a higher baseline of 50 per cent. Your lecturer will confirm the exact requirement for your subject at the start of the trimester or semester.

How long is my ICASS mark valid for a rewrite?

In the standard DHET framework, an ICASS mark remains valid for one calendar year, which usually covers two consecutive national examination cycles. If you fail the national examination on the first attempt, you can carry the same ICASS mark into the next sitting without repeating the classroom assignments. After that window closes, the ICASS mark expires and you must re-register the subject to build a fresh internal mark.

What is the difference between a NATED pass and a NATED distinction?

A standard pass is awarded when the weighted composite reaches 50 per cent and the exam sub-minimum is met. A distinction is awarded when the weighted composite reaches 75 per cent or higher and the exam sub-minimum is met. The band between 40 and 49 per cent is a condoned or minimum pass in some subjects, while anything below 40 per cent on the composite, or any exam mark below 40 per cent, is a failure that requires a rewrite.

Can I apply for a remark if my exam mark is between 37 and 39 per cent?

Yes. DHET publishes a remarking and re-checking window after each national results release, and candidates who landed just below the 40 per cent exam threshold are the ones who use it most. Applications are lodged through your college examination office and a gazetted administrative fee applies. A successful remark that lifts the exam mark to 40 per cent or above can convert a sub-minimum fail into a pass, because the weighted composite is recomputed with the corrected exam mark.

What is the difference between NATED Report 191 and NCV?

NATED Report 191 is the older trimester or semester modular qualification, typically taken after Grade 11 or Grade 12, and it runs from N1 to N6 in Engineering and N4 to N6 in Business Studies. NCV is the National Certificate Vocational, a three-year full-time programme at Levels 2, 3, and 4 designed mainly for Grade 9 school leavers. The two routes lead to different qualifications and different workplace pathways, and they are assessed under separate DHET frameworks.
