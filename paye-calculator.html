What this PAYE and net salary calculator does

Contractual salary offers are almost always quoted in gross terms, while rent, groceries, loan repayments, and savings come exclusively out of net cash. Between the headline gross number on an employment letter and the transfer that lands in your bank account sits a multi-step statutory payroll calculation covering progressive income tax, retirement contributions, and mandatory social or health insurance levies. This calculator bridges that gap across five major Pay As You Earn (PAYE) jurisdictions: Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, South Africa, and the United Kingdom.

When you select a country, enter either a monthly or an annual gross salary, choose whether to apply the standard employee pension deduction, and click Calculate net salary, the tool runs the jurisdiction's 2026 statutory deduction rules locally inside your browser. It returns four diagnostic views so you can inspect every step rather than trusting a black-box number:

  • Headline monthly and annual net take-home pay: The cash deposit remaining after employee income tax and mandatory statutory deductions are withheld.
  • Total deductions and effective deduction rate: Combined monthly tax, pension, and social insurance withheld, expressed both in local currency and as a blended percentage of total gross salary.
  • Line-by-line deduction breakdown: Separate rows showing pre-tax retirement contributions (PRA pension, SSNIT, NSSF, South African retirement fund, or UK workplace pension), health or social insurance levies (Kenyan SHIF or UK Employee Class 1 National Insurance), and net PAYE income tax.
  • Progressive tax band summary: The exact monthly tax generated inside each statutory income slice, plus any direct tax credits or rebates subtracted from gross tax (such as KRA Personal Relief in Kenya or the SARS Primary Rebate in South Africa).

All arithmetic executes client-side in your browser tab. Your salary figure is never saved to a database or transmitted to AfriWidget's servers. Only if you actively press the optional AI insight button is an anonymous numeric summary—country code, rounded gross and net totals, pension toggle state, and effective rate—sent once to generate a brief plain-English note.

How the gross-to-net PAYE payroll waterfall works

Pay As You Earn (PAYE) is a source-withholding mechanism. Rather than waiting until the end of the tax year for employees to file a lump-sum tax bill, tax statutes require employers to act as collection agents on every monthly, fortnightly, or weekly payroll cycle. Crucially, payroll software does not apply a flat percentage to your gross salary, nor does it subtract deductions in arbitrary order. The sequence in which pre-tax deductions, progressive brackets, post-tax rebates, and separate social levies are applied directly alters the final net payout.

Across the five jurisdictions modeled in this tool, standard payroll engines execute five sequential steps on an annualized basis and then divide annual totals by twelve for a standard monthly pay run:

  1. Annualize gross employment income: If you enter a monthly salary, the engine multiplies by 12 so annual statutory thresholds, caps, and rebates align on a common 12-month scale.
  2. Deduct approved pre-tax contributions to determine chargeable income: Statutory employee pension contributions (and, in Kenya, mandatory SHIF health contributions under current KRA rules) are subtracted from gross pay before income tax is calculated. In the UK, if chargeable income exceeds £100,000, the tax-free Personal Allowance is also tapered at this stage before computing taxable income.
  3. Slice chargeable income across progressive tax brackets: Chargeable income is partitioned from the bottom band upward. Each slice is multiplied strictly by that band's statutory rate, and the resulting band taxes are summed to produce gross income tax.
  4. Subtract statutory tax credits or rebates: In jurisdictions that grant fixed-currency tax credits rather than a zero-rate bottom bracket—specifically Kenya's Personal Relief (KES 28,800 per year) and South Africa's Primary Rebate (R17,820 per year)—the credit is deducted dollar-for-dollar (or shilling-for-shilling) from gross income tax, floored at zero so PAYE never turns negative.
  5. Subtract separate non-PAYE payroll levies to arrive at net pay: Separate social insurance charges calculated on their own earnings thresholds—such as UK Employee Class 1 National Insurance—are deducted alongside net PAYE and employee pension contributions to yield final take-home pay.
Core Gross-to-Net PAYE Equations (Plain-Text Reference):
Chargeable Annual Income = Gross Annual Salary − Deductible Pre-Tax Contributions
Gross Band Tax = Sum across bands i of [ Band Slice(i) × Statutory Rate(i) ]
Net Annual PAYE = max(0, Gross Band Tax − Statutory Tax Relief or Rebate)
Total Annual Deductions = Net Annual PAYE + Employee Pension + Separate Statutory Levies
Monthly Net Take-Home Pay = (Gross Annual Salary − Total Annual Deductions) ÷ 12
Effective Deduction Rate (%) = (Total Annual Deductions ÷ Gross Annual Salary) × 100

Marginal tax rate vs. effective deduction rate

One of the most persistent myths in personal finance is the belief that accepting a salary increase or overtime shift that crosses into a higher tax bracket can leave you with less take-home cash than before. Mathematically, progressive PAYE brackets make that impossible under standard marginal schedules. Understanding the distinction between your marginal rate and your effective rate prevents costly negotiation errors.

Your marginal tax rate is the tax percentage charged on the next single unit of chargeable income you earn—for example, the next ₦1,000, GH₵100, KSh 1,000, R100, or £100 added to a pay raise or performance bonus. Crossing a bracket boundary never reprices the income you already earned in lower bands; only the excess portion sitting above the threshold pays the higher rate.

Your effective tax rate (or, more broadly on a payslip, your effective total deduction rate) divides total deductions withheld by your entire gross salary. Because zero-rate bands, personal allowances, and rebates shelter your initial slices of earnings at 0% or low starter rates, your effective rate rises along a smooth curve that always remains below your top marginal bracket step.

How to estimate how much of a raise or bonus you actually keep

When you already earn an established base salary, your lower tax bands and fixed rebates are already used up by your existing pay. Consequently, a cash bonus or mid-year salary raise is taxed at your combined marginal deduction rate—not your blended effective rate. To estimate quickly how much of a proposed raise reaches your bank account:

Marginal Retention Ratio = 100% − Marginal PAYE Rate − Marginal Uncapped Levy Rate
Estimated Net Cash from Raise = Gross Raise × (Marginal Retention Ratio ÷ 100)

For example, if you work in the UK earning £60,000 per year, your overall effective deduction rate (with a 5% pension) is 27.4%. However, an extra £5,000 cash bonus sits entirely above the £50,270 Higher Rate threshold, where it faces 40% Income Tax plus 2% upper-band National Insurance (42% combined marginal deduction if pension does not apply to bonuses), leaving £2,900 net (58% retained)—not the 72.6% implied by your average rate. Running your current salary and your proposed new salary through the calculator above and comparing the two monthly net figures gives you the exact net raise down to the last unit of currency.

Nigeria PAYE, the 2026 Tax Act overhaul, and PRA pension

Nigeria's personal income tax framework underwent its largest structural reform in decades starting January 2026 under the comprehensive Nigeria Tax Act. Previously, payroll officers under the Personal Income Tax Act (PITA) had to compute a complex Consolidated Relief Allowance (CRA)—equal to the higher of ₦200,000 or 1% of gross income plus 20% of gross income—and then run the remainder through six brackets from 7% up to 24%, subject to a 1% minimum tax floor on gross pay.

The 2026 reform abolished the old percentage-based CRA formula and minimum income tax trap for low-wage workers, replacing them with an explicit ₦800,000 annual zero-tax band (₦66,666.67 per month of chargeable income) and a modernized six-tier progressive schedule peaking at 25% for high earners above ₦50,000,000 per year.

PRA pension deductibility and Nigerian payroll nuances

Under the Pension Reform Act (PRA 2014), private-sector employers with qualifying headcount deduct a minimum 8% employee contribution toward the worker's Retirement Savings Account (RSA), alongside a minimum 10% employer contribution paid on top of gross package. Statutory employee pension contributions are tax-deductible before PAYE bands are applied. Note one practical payslip distinction in Nigeria: strict PRA wording defines minimum mandatory pension on monthly emoluments comprising Basic Salary + Housing Allowance + Transport Allowance (BHT). Many corporate employers apply 8% across total gross pay for simplicity (which is what this calculator models when the pension toggle is enabled), whereas structured compensation packages that allocate 30% to 40% of gross pay to meal, utility, or entertainment allowances will show a slightly smaller 8% pension deduction—and correspondingly slightly higher chargeable income—on the live payslip.

In addition, the 2026 framework introduced a capped Rent Relief deduction (20% of annual rent actually paid, capped at ₦500,000 per year upon submitting verified tenancy documentation to your employer or state internal revenue service) as well as optional or sector-specific National Housing Fund (NHF at 2.5% of basic salary) and National Health Insurance deductions. Because rent paid and BHT splits vary per household, the calculator keeps the core model focused on gross pay and the 8% RSA toggle so you have a clean baseline.

Ghana PAYE and the three-tier SSNIT pension system

In Ghana, the Ghana Revenue Authority (GRA) administers PAYE on employment income—including basic salary and taxable cash allowances—using a seven-band progressive scale ranging from 0% on the minimum-wage-linked exempt slice up to 35% on chargeable income exceeding GH₵600,000 per year (GH₵50,000 per month). On monthly payroll runs, employers divide each annual band width by twelve.

How Ghana's Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 pensions interact with GRA tax

Under Ghana's National Pensions Act (Act 766), mandatory retirement funding totals 18.5% of basic salary: the employer contributes 13%, and the employee contributes 5.5%. Payroll splits that combined 18.5% into 13.5% remitted to the Social Security and National Insurance Trust (SSNIT Tier 1) and 5% remitted to a privately managed occupational scheme (Tier 2). Crucially for your payslip, the entire 5.5% employee share is deducted before GRA calculates PAYE, lowering your chargeable income.

Ghana also offers a powerful voluntary tax-planning lever under Tier 3 (personal provident fund and group personal pension schemes): combined employee and employer Tier 3 contributions of up to 16.5% of basic salary enjoy full income tax exemption provided funds remain invested for at least ten years (for provident funds) or until statutory retirement. Finally, check whether your Ghanaian employment contract splits remuneration into basic salary plus cash allowances (fuel, housing, responsibility): SSNIT 5.5% is legally levied on basic salary, whereas GRA taxes cash allowances alongside basic salary under PAYE, and qualifies certain overtime and bonus payments of junior/qualifying staff for preferential flat withholding rates rather than regular marginal bands.

Kenya PAYE, SHIF health insurance, and tiered NSSF caps

Kenyan payslips have changed substantially between 2023 and 2026 due to three overlapping statutory reforms administered by the Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA), the Social Health Authority, and the National Social Security Fund (NSSF): the introduction of 32.5% and 35% upper PAYE brackets, the replacement of NHIF with uncapped SHIF, and the multi-year phase-in of higher Tier I and Tier II NSSF pension ceilings—paired with tax deductibility for mandatory social contributions.

  • KRA Personal Relief (KES 2,400/month or KES 28,800/year): Kenya taxes the first KES 24,000 of monthly chargeable income at 10% (= KES 2,400) and then grants every resident individual a KES 2,400 monthly Personal Relief credit. That makes the first KES 24,000 of monthly chargeable income effectively PAYE-free.
  • SHIF (2.75% of gross monthly pay, minimum KES 300/month): Replacing the old NHIF banded schedule (which capped out at KES 1,700 per month at salaries of KES 100,000+), the Social Health Insurance Fund deducts a flat 2.75% across your entire gross salary with no upper cap. Under Kenya's Tax Laws (Amendment) reforms, mandatory SHIF contributions are deducted from gross income when arriving at chargeable PAYE income, which softens the net impact by your marginal tax rate (typically 30% to 35% for middle and upper earners).
  • NSSF Tier I and Tier II (6% capped at KES 6,480/month): Under the 2026 NSSF schedule, employees contribute 6% of pensionable pay (matched 6% by the employer) across Tier I (Lower Earnings Limit up to KES 9,000/month = KES 540) and Tier II (between KES 9,001 and the KES 108,000/month Upper Earnings Limit = up to KES 5,940). Once gross monthly pay reaches KES 108,000 (KES 1,296,000 per year), mandatory employee NSSF locks at its statutory ceiling of KES 6,480.00 per month (KES 77,760.00 per year) and is deducted before PAYE.
  • Note on Kenya's Affordable Housing Levy (AHL): Kenyan employers also withhold a statutory 1.5% Affordable Housing Levy on gross monthly salary (matched 1.5% by the employer), which is likewise tax-deductible before PAYE under current KRA rules. This multi-country calculator models the three core universal lines (PAYE, NSSF, and SHIF); if your employer deducts the 1.5% Housing Levy on your payslip, expect an additional net reduction of roughly 1.05% of gross pay (1.5% gross levy minus 30% PAYE tax relief on that levy for earners in the 30% band).

South Africa PAYE, SARS tax rebates, and Section 11F limits

The South African Revenue Service (SARS) assesses individual PAYE on a March-to-February tax year using seven progressive brackets from 18% up to 45%. Instead of publishing a 0% bracket at the top of the table, SARS taxes all taxable income from the first rand at 18% and then subtracts age-based tax rebates directly from the resulting annual tax bill.

For the 2026/27 schedule, the Primary Rebate (applicable to all natural persons under age 65) is R17,820 per year (R1,485.00 per month). Dividing R17,820 by the 18% bottom bracket rate produces an effective annual tax-free threshold of R99,000 (R8,250 per month). Taxpayers aged 65 to 74 qualify for an additional Secondary Rebate, and those aged 75 and older receive a further Tertiary Rebate, raising their tax-free thresholds accordingly.

Under Section 11F of the South African Income Tax Act, combined employee and employer contributions to pension, provident, and retirement annuity funds are deductible up to 27.5% of remuneration or taxable income, subject to an absolute annual ceiling of R350,000. When the pension toggle is checked, this calculator deducts a standard 7.5% employee retirement fund contribution capped at R350,000 per year. When comparing your output against a live South African payslip, keep two extra items in mind: most private-sector employees pay 1% Unemployment Insurance Fund (UIF) capped at the R17,712 monthly earnings ceiling (a maximum deduction of R177.12 per month), and members of registered medical schemes receive fixed monthly Medical Scheme Fees Tax Credits that directly reduce monthly PAYE.

United Kingdom income tax, Class 1 NI, and the £100,000 taper

In England, Wales, and Northern Ireland, HMRC collects two separate payroll deductions on employee wages: Income Tax (computed on annual cumulative taxable pay under tax code 1257L for most employees) and Employee Class 1 National Insurance (NI) (computed per pay period on gross earnings). Standard employees receive a £12,570 tax-free Personal Allowance, followed by three Income Tax bands.

UK Earnings Slice (England, Wales & NI) Taxable Slice Width (Standard PA) Income Tax Employee Class 1 NI
Up to £12,570 (Personal Allowance / NI Primary Threshold) £0 taxable 0% 0%
£12,571 to £50,270 (Basic Rate / NI Upper Earnings Limit) First £37,700 of taxable income 20% 8%
£50,271 to £125,140 (Higher Rate Band) Next £87,440 of taxable income (at £0 PA) 40% 2%
Above £125,140 (Additional Rate Band) Taxable income above £125,140 45% 2%

Why UK earners face a 60% marginal tax trap between £100,000 and £125,140

Once your adjusted net income (gross pay minus qualifying pre-tax pension contributions) exceeds £100,000, HMRC withdraws £1 of your £12,570 Personal Allowance for every £2 of income above £100,000. By the time adjusted net income reaches £125,140 (£100,000 + 2 × £12,570), your Personal Allowance is completely extinguished. Inside that £25,140 corridor, every £100 of extra salary triggers £40 of Higher Rate tax on the £100 itself plus £20 of extra tax because £50 of formerly tax-free income gets pulled into the 40% band. That creates an effective 60% marginal Income Tax rate—and 62% total marginal deduction once 2% Class 1 National Insurance is included.

Notice also how UK workplace pension structure affects National Insurance: under a standard Net-Pay or Relief-at-Source workplace pension (modeled by this calculator), your 5% contribution reduces taxable pay for Income Tax, while Employee Class 1 NI is still calculated on your gross salary before pension. If your employer operates a contractual Salary Sacrifice pension scheme, you agree to reduce your contractual gross salary by 5% in exchange for an equivalent employer pension contribution—saving an extra 8% NI (if earning £12,570–£50,270) or 2% NI (if earning above £50,270) on top of Income Tax relief. Scottish residents (`S`-prefixed tax codes such as `S1257L`) face HMRC's separate six-band Scottish Income Tax schedule (19% Starter, 20% Basic, 21% Intermediate, 42% Higher, 45% Advanced, and 48% Top rate), though UK-wide Class 1 National Insurance thresholds remain identical.

Step-by-step worked PAYE examples across five countries

Every worked scenario below uses the exact formulas implemented in the calculator on this page so you can enter the same inputs above and verify each line item directly.

Example 1: Lagos software engineer earning ₦500,000/month gross (Nigeria)

Suppose Chinedu receives an offer of ₦500,000 per month (₦6,000,000 per year) in Lagos with standard 8% PRA employee pension enabled:

  • Step 1 — Pre-tax PRA pension (8%): ₦6,000,000 × 0.08 = ₦480,000.00/year (₦40,000.00/month).
  • Step 2 — Annual chargeable income: ₦6,000,000 − ₦480,000 = ₦5,520,000.00/year.
  • Step 3 — Progressive 2026 Nigeria tax slices: First ₦800,000 × 0% = ₦0.00; Next ₦2,200,000 × 15% = ₦330,000.00 (₦27,500.00/month); Remaining ₦2,520,000 × 18% = ₦453,600.00 (₦37,800.00/month).
  • Step 4 — PAYE and net take-home pay: Annual PAYE = ₦330,000 + ₦453,600 = ₦783,600.00/year (₦65,300.00/month). Total monthly deductions = ₦40,000 + ₦65,300 = ₦105,300.00 (21.1% effective deduction rate). Monthly net take-home pay = ₦394,700.00 (₦4,736,400.00/year).
  • Tax-shield check (Pension OFF comparison): Without the 8% pension deduction, chargeable income is the full ₦6,000,000, pushing monthly PAYE up to ₦72,500.00 (₦427,500.00 net cash). Saving ₦40,000/month into Chinedu's retirement account cuts his monthly tax bill by ₦7,200.00, costing only ₦32,800.00 in spendable cash.

Example 2: Accra operations manager earning GH₵12,000/month gross (Ghana)

Suppose Ama earns GH₵12,000.00 per month (GH₵144,000.00 per year) in Accra with standard 5.5% SSNIT employee pension enabled:

  • Step 1 — Pre-tax SSNIT employee deduction (5.5%): GH₵144,000 × 0.055 = GH₵7,920.00/year (GH₵660.00/month).
  • Step 2 — Annual chargeable income: GH₵144,000 − GH₵7,920 = GH₵136,080.00/year.
  • Step 3 — GRA progressive tax slices: First GH₵7,056 × 0% = GH₵0.00; Next GH₵960 × 5% = GH₵48.00 (GH₵4.00/mo); Next GH₵1,200 × 10% = GH₵120.00 (GH₵10.00/mo); Next GH₵34,800 × 17.5% = GH₵6,090.00 (GH₵507.50/mo); Remaining GH₵92,064 (GH₵136,080 − GH₵44,016) × 25% = GH₵23,016.00 (GH₵1,918.00/mo).
  • Step 4 — Monthly net take-home pay: Annual PAYE = GH₵29,274.00 (GH₵2,439.50/month). Combined monthly deductions = GH₵660.00 + GH₵2,439.50 = GH₵3,099.50 (25.8% effective rate), leaving GH₵8,900.50/month net take-home pay (GH₵106,806.00/year).

Example 3: Nairobi financial analyst earning KSh 150,000/month gross (Kenya)

Suppose Wanjiku earns KSh 150,000.00 per month (KSh 1,800,000.00 per year) in Nairobi with NSSF enabled:

  • Step 1 — Capped NSSF and uncapped SHIF: Because KSh 150,000 exceeds the KSh 108,000/month Tier II Upper Earnings Limit, employee NSSF caps at 6% × KSh 108,000 = KSh 6,480.00/month (KSh 77,760.00/year). SHIF at 2.75% of gross = KSh 150,000 × 0.0275 = KSh 4,125.00/month (KSh 49,500.00/year).
  • Step 2 — Annual chargeable income: KSh 1,800,000 − KSh 77,760 − KSh 49,500 = KSh 1,672,740.00/year (KSh 139,395.00/month).
  • Step 3 — KRA bands and Personal Relief: First KSh 288,000 × 10% = KSh 28,800.00 (KSh 2,400.00/mo); Next KSh 100,000 × 25% = KSh 25,000.00 (KSh 2,083.33/mo); Remaining KSh 1,284,740 × 30% = KSh 385,422.00 (KSh 32,118.50/mo). Gross tax = KSh 439,222.00/year. Subtracting KRA Personal Relief of KSh 28,800.00/year (KSh 2,400.00/mo) yields net PAYE = KSh 410,422.00/year (KSh 34,201.83/month).
  • Step 4 — Monthly net take-home pay: Total deductions = KSh 6,480.00 + KSh 4,125.00 + KSh 34,201.83 = KSh 44,806.83/month (29.9% effective rate), leaving KSh 105,193.17/month net (before any 1.5% Affordable Housing Levy).

Example 4: Johannesburg project lead earning R45,000/month gross (South Africa)

Suppose Thabo earns R45,000.00 per month (R540,000.00 per year) in Johannesburg, is under age 65, and contributes 7.5% to an employer retirement fund:

  • Step 1 — Section 11F retirement contribution (7.5%): R540,000 × 0.075 = R40,500.00/year (R3,375.00/month), well within the R350,000 annual Section 11F ceiling.
  • Step 2 — Annual taxable income: R540,000 − R40,500 = R499,500.00/year.
  • Step 3 — SARS brackets less Primary Rebate: First R245,100 × 18% = R44,118.00 (R3,676.50/mo); Next R138,000 × 26% = R35,880.00 (R2,990.00/mo); Remaining R116,400 (R499,500 − R383,100) × 31% = R36,084.00 (R3,007.00/mo). Gross tax = R116,082.00/year. Less Primary Rebate of R17,820.00/year (R1,485.00/mo) = R98,262.00/year PAYE (R8,188.50/month).
  • Step 4 — Monthly net take-home pay: Total deductions = R3,375.00 + R8,188.50 = R11,563.50/month (25.7% effective rate), leaving R33,436.50/month net (prior to R177.12 capped UIF or medical tax credits).

Example 5: London product manager at £60,000/year vs. £110,000/year (United Kingdom)

Suppose Oliver earns £60,000.00 per year (£5,000.00/month) in London with a 5% auto-enrolment workplace pension:

  • Pre-tax workplace pension (5%): £60,000 × 0.05 = £3,000.00/year (£250.00/month), leaving £57,000.00 chargeable income.
  • Income Tax (after £12,570 Personal Allowance): Taxable pay = £57,000 − £12,570 = £44,430.00. First £37,700 × 20% = £7,540.00 (£628.33/mo); remaining £6,730 × 40% = £2,692.00 (£224.33/mo). Total Income Tax = £10,232.00/year (£852.67/month).
  • Class 1 Employee National Insurance (on £60,000 gross): (£50,270 − £12,570) × 8% = £3,016.00, plus (£60,000 − £50,270) × 2% = £194.60, totaling £3,210.60/year (£267.55/month).
  • Net take-home pay: Total deductions = £16,442.60/year (£1,370.22/month, or 27.4% of gross), leaving £3,629.78/month (£43,557.40/year).
  • At £110,000/year (Taper Shield effect): Without pension, Oliver's Personal Allowance shrinks from £12,570 to £7,570, producing £33,432.00 Income Tax + £4,210.60 NI = £37,642.60 deductions and £72,357.40 net cash. Toggling 5% pension ON (£5,500/year) restores £2,750 of Personal Allowance (to £10,320), cutting Income Tax by £3,300.00 down to £30,132.00—so £5,500 enters his pension pot while annual net cash drops by only £2,200.00 to £70,157.40 (£5,846.45/month).

How to audit your payslip and compare cross-border job offers

Running your salary here takes ten seconds; knowing how to reconcile the estimate against a real employer payslip or a competing international offer is where the real financial leverage lies. Use this four-point checklist whenever you start a new job, receive a bonus, or evaluate relocation:

  1. Verify your tax code and cumulative status in Month 1: When changing employers mid-year without handing over a prior-employment tax certificate (such as a UK P45 or South African IRP5), payroll software often places you on an emergency or non-cumulative code (for example, 1257L W1/M1 or 0T in the UK). That restricts or removes unused allowances from earlier months and temporarily over-withholds PAYE until your cumulative earnings record updates.
  2. Separate contractual basic pay from non-pensionable allowances: In Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, and South Africa, employers frequently structure total cost-to-company (CTC) or gross packages into basic pay plus housing, transport, or car allowances. Check which lines feed your pension percentage and whether employer pension/medical contributions are quoted on top of gross salary or inside a total CTC figure.
  3. Check how one-off bonuses and 13th cheques are taxed: Monthly payroll engines that project single-month pay × 12 can spike PAYE during a bonus month if the software treats a quarterly commission as recurring monthly salary. Ask payroll whether bonuses are taxed using annualized cumulative averaging or marginal bonus tables, and confirm year-end reconciliation.
  4. Compare cross-border offers on net purchasing power, not gross exchange rates: Converting a £55,000 UK offer and a R1,100,000 South African offer at spot FX rates on gross figures misleads twice—first because effective deduction rates differ, and second because public healthcare access, council tax/municipal rates, transport, and rent consume vastly different shares of net pay. Convert each offer to monthly net salary here first, then subtract realistic local rent (using our Rent Affordability Calculator) and currency conversion spreads (via our Currency Converter) to compare true monthly disposable savings.

Common mistakes when estimating take-home pay

  • Assuming your top bracket applies to your whole salary: Entering the 30% or 40% tax band does not mean 30% or 40% of your salary vanishes. Progressive bands tax each slice independently, which is why the effective rate card in the results panel is almost always 8 to 18 percentage points lower than your highest marginal band.
  • Ignoring uncapped percentage levies on high salaries: Before 2024–2026 reforms, Kenyan NHIF capped at KES 1,700 per month regardless of how high your salary climbed. Under SHIF (2.75% uncapped), a KES 400,000 monthly earner pays KES 11,000 per month toward health insurance instead of KES 1,700—a major shift when budgeting senior compensation.
  • Overlooking benefits-in-kind (taxable perks): Company cars, employer-funded private medical insurance (in the UK under P11D rules, or taxable fringe benefits in South Africa, Kenya, and Ghana), low-interest staff loans, and non-exempt housing provisions increase your taxable income without adding spendable cash to your monthly bank transfer—reducing net cash pay below a pure cash-salary calculation.
  • Forgetting student loan payroll deductions: Statutory income-contingent student loan repayments—including Kenya HELB, Tanzania HESLB, and UK Student Loan Plans 1, 2, 4, 5, and Postgraduate—are deducted through payroll alongside PAYE once gross income crosses plan thresholds, reducing actual bank deposits by an extra 6% to 15% of above-threshold earnings.
  • Treating pre-tax pension deductions as lost money: Unlike income tax, employee pension deductions remain your personal invested asset—and almost always unlock matching employer contributions (such as 10% employer PRA in Nigeria, 13% employer SSNIT in Ghana, 6% matched NSSF in Kenya, or 3%+ employer auto-enrolment in the UK) plus immediate marginal tax relief.

Frequently asked questions

What is PAYE and how does it affect my monthly take-home pay?

PAYE stands for Pay As You Earn, the payroll withholding system through which employers calculate and remit income tax and statutory social contributions directly to the revenue authority on every pay run. Your gross salary is the contractual figure on your offer letter before deductions, while your net salary (take-home pay) is the cash balance transferred to your bank account after PAYE income tax, pension contributions, and mandatory health or social insurance levies are subtracted.

How do I calculate my net salary after tax step by step?

First, convert your gross salary to an annual basis if your country's tax brackets are defined annually. Second, subtract statutory pre-tax deductions recognized by the tax authority—such as employee pension, SSNIT, NSSF, or SHIF contributions—to find chargeable income. Third, slice chargeable income through each progressive tax band from lowest to highest and sum the resulting band taxes. Fourth, subtract any statutory tax credits or rebates (such as Kenya's Personal Relief or South Africa's Primary Rebate), deduct separate payroll levies like UK National Insurance, and divide the remaining annual net pay by twelve.

Can getting a pay raise push me into a higher tax bracket and reduce my total net salary?

No. Under progressive PAYE systems in Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, South Africa, and the United Kingdom, a higher tax bracket never applies retroactively to your entire salary. Only the slice of chargeable income above the bracket threshold is taxed at the higher marginal rate, while every currency unit below the threshold continues to be taxed at the lower rates. Even in the UK's £100,000 to £125,140 Personal Allowance taper zone—where the effective marginal rate reaches 62% including National Insurance—you still keep £38 of every additional £100 earned.

Why is my effective deduction rate lower than my top marginal tax rate?

Your marginal rate is the percentage charged on the very next unit of income you earn, whereas your effective rate divides total deductions by your total gross salary. Because zero-rate allowances, rebates, and lower introductory brackets shield the first slices of your pay, your blended average rate across all earnings stays substantially below your top bracket rate. For example, a Nigerian employee earning NGN 6,000,000 annually reaches the 18% marginal PAYE bracket, yet their actual PAYE tax is NGN 783,600 per year—just 13.06% of gross pay.

How do pre-tax pension contributions lower my PAYE income tax bill?

Approved employee pension contributions—including PRA Retirement Savings Accounts in Nigeria (8%), SSNIT Tier 1/2 in Ghana (5.5%), NSSF in Kenya (6% up to ceiling), Section 11F retirement funds in South Africa, and workplace pensions in the UK—are deducted from gross pay before chargeable income tax is computed. Because the deduction comes off the top of your chargeable income, it saves tax at your highest marginal PAYE rate, meaning a pension deposit reduces your take-home cash by less than the full amount added to your retirement pot.

How did Nigeria's 2026 Tax Act change PAYE compared with the old Consolidated Relief Allowance?

Effective January 2026, Nigeria replaced the old Consolidated Relief Allowance formula (NGN 200,000 or 1% of gross plus 20% of gross income) and old 7%–24% brackets with a cleaner six-band progressive schedule starting with a full 0% tax-free band on the first NGN 800,000 of annual chargeable income, followed by bands of 15%, 18%, 21%, 23%, and 25% above NGN 50,000,000. Low-income earners up to NGN 800,000 chargeable pay zero PAYE, while approved employee pension contributions remain deductible before bands are applied.

How do SHIF and the Tier II NSSF cap work on a Kenyan payslip in 2026?

Kenya's Social Health Insurance Fund (SHIF) replaced the old capped NHIF table (which maxed out at KES 1,700 per month) with an uncapped flat contribution of 2.75% of gross monthly salary, subject to a KES 300 monthly minimum. Meanwhile, NSSF employee contributions are 6% of pensionable pay across Tier I (up to KES 9,000/month) and Tier II (KES 9,001 to the KES 108,000/month Upper Earnings Limit), capping the mandatory employee NSSF deduction at KES 6,480 per month (KES 77,760 per year). Both NSSF and SHIF reduce chargeable income before KRA PAYE bands and the KES 2,400 monthly Personal Relief are applied.

How do South African SARS tax rebates work, and what is the tax-free threshold?

Unlike countries that list a 0% bracket at the top of the tax table, SARS taxes the first ZAR 245,100 of taxable income at 18% and then subtracts a fixed rand tax credit—the Primary Rebate of ZAR 17,820 per year for taxpayers under age 65—directly from the calculated tax. Because ZAR 17,820 divided by 18% equals ZAR 99,000, any taxpayer under 65 with annual taxable income of ZAR 99,000 or less (ZAR 8,250 per month) owes zero PAYE. Taxpayers aged 65–74 and 75+ receive additional Secondary and Tertiary rebates.

Why is there an effective 60% income tax trap between £100,000 and £125,140 in the UK?

HMRC withdraws the standard £12,570 tax-free Personal Allowance by £1 for every £2 of adjusted net income above £100,000 until the allowance reaches zero at £125,140. Within that £25,140 corridor, earning an extra £2 triggers 40% Higher Rate tax on the £2 itself (£0.80) plus 40% tax on £1 of lost Personal Allowance (£0.40), totaling £1.20 of income tax on £2 of earnings—an effective 60% income tax rate, or 62% once 2% upper-band Class 1 National Insurance is added. Pre-tax pension contributions inside this band reclaim the lost allowance and earn 60% income tax relief.

Why was so much PAYE withheld in the month I received an annual bonus or overtime pay?

Standard monthly payroll software annualizes irregular spikes unless a cumulative or special bonus calculation method is applied: if your normal monthly pay of 4,000 jumps to 10,000 in a bonus month, a non-cumulative payroll run may briefly treat you as someone earning 120,000 per year and withhold tax at higher brackets for that pay period. Under cumulative PAYE systems (such as UK cumulative tax codes or year-end employer reconciliations in South Africa, Kenya, Ghana, and Nigeria), any excess withholding is either smoothed across subsequent pay runs or refunded through annual assessment.
