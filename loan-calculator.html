What this calculator does

This tool takes four numbers about a loan — the currency, the amount borrowed, the annual interest rate, and the term in years — and returns the fixed monthly payment, the total repayment across the full term, the total interest cost, and a month-by-month amortization schedule that shows how every installment splits between interest and principal.

The point of the tool is not to produce a number you could not look up on a lender's website. It is to make the structure of the loan visible. A loan is not a single transaction; it is a schedule of payments where the split between interest and principal changes every month. The first installment on a long loan is almost entirely interest, and the last is almost entirely principal. Seeing that change in a table is the fastest way to understand why paying a little more each month, or choosing a shorter term, has a much larger effect on the total cost than most people expect.

Every calculation runs inside your browser. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored, and nothing is tracked. If the AI insight button is pressed, only an anonymous summary of the loan amount, the rate, the term, the monthly payment, and the computed totals is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

The amortization formula in plain terms

Almost every consumer and commercial loan uses the same annuity formula to work out the fixed monthly payment. The mathematics is more than a century old and it is not complicated once you strip away the notation.

PMT = P × [ r × (1 + r)^n ] ÷ [ (1 + r)^n − 1 ]

P = principal borrowed
r = monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100)
n = number of monthly installments (years × 12)

Read it as three steps. First, take the annual rate and divide it by twelve to get the monthly rate. Second, compound that monthly rate over the full number of installments, because the lender is entitled to interest on interest for the duration of the loan. Third, divide the compounded figure by itself minus one, which smooths the interest out evenly across every month so the payment stays the same from start to finish.

That even spread is the whole trick of the fixed-rate loan. The lender is not waiting until the end to collect interest; the interest is priced into each installment from the beginning. What changes month by month is only the split between how much of that installment is interest and how much is principal. The cash amount the borrower pays never moves, which is why fixed-rate loans are popular with households that want predictable monthly outgoings.

How to read an amortization schedule

An amortization schedule is a table with one row per month and five columns: the month number, the installment paid, the portion of that installment that went to interest, the portion that went to principal, and the remaining balance.

In the first month, the balance is at its highest, so the interest column is at its highest too. On a typical thirty-year mortgage the interest column in month one is three or four times the size of the principal column. That is not a design flaw of the loan; it is arithmetic. Interest is charged on the outstanding balance, and the balance is largest at the start.

As the months pass, the principal column grows and the interest column shrinks. On a long loan there is a specific crossover month where the split reaches fifty-fifty, and after that the majority of every installment goes to principal. On a short loan the crossover happens early, sometimes in the first year, which is one reason a shorter term saves so much money across the full schedule.

The single most useful column to watch is the balance. The interest column and the principal column move against each other, but the balance column is the one that actually tells you how far along you are. If the balance barely moves in the first year, the loan is structured with a long tail of interest in the early months, and the loan will take much longer to clear than the borrower might assume from the fixed monthly amount.

Interest rate versus APR

The interest rate is the rate the lender charges on the outstanding balance. The Annual Percentage Rate, or APR, is the interest rate plus the fees that are mandatory for the loan: origination fees, documentation fees, mandatory credit insurance, and any other charges the lender requires as a condition of lending.

The APR is always equal to or higher than the nominal interest rate, and on short consumer loans the gap can be wide. A lender advertising a ten per cent rate with a five per cent origination fee is really lending at closer to fifteen per cent on a two-year loan once the fee is spread across the term. The rate is the honest figure only if there are no fees; the APR is the honest figure in every other case.

When comparing two loan offers, always compare the APR, not the rate. If a lender refuses to disclose the APR in writing before you sign, that is a signal worth taking seriously, because every regulated lending market requires the disclosure precisely because the rate alone is not a fair comparison.

Short-term and long-term loans compared

A loan term is a trade-off between monthly cash flow and total cost. A shorter term forces a higher monthly installment but reduces the number of months the balance is outstanding, which reduces the total interest bill. A longer term softens the monthly installment but lets interest accrue for many more months on a larger average balance, which raises the total interest bill.

The trade-off is not symmetrical. Extending a loan term rarely reduces the monthly installment by the same proportion that it increases the total interest. Doubling the term often cuts the monthly payment by only twenty or thirty per cent, while doubling the total interest paid. That asymmetry is why short terms are almost always cheaper across the full life of the loan, and it is why the term is the single most important number in the loan agreement after the rate.

A worked example

Take a personal loan of ten thousand dollars at an annual interest rate of twelve per cent, over three years. The monthly rate is one per cent, and the number of installments is thirty-six. Running the annuity formula gives a fixed monthly payment of about three hundred and thirty-two dollars.

Multiply the monthly payment by the term to get the total repayment: 332 × 36 = 11,952. Subtract the principal to get the total interest: 1,952. The cost of borrowing ten thousand dollars over three years at twelve per cent is roughly nineteen hundred and fifty-two dollars, or just under twenty per cent of the principal.

Now extend the same loan to five years. The monthly rate stays at one per cent, but the number of installments rises to sixty. The fixed monthly payment falls to about two hundred and twenty-two dollars, a saving of roughly one hundred and ten dollars per month in cash flow. The total repayment rises to about 13,347, and the total interest rises to roughly 3,347. The five-year term costs about 1,395 dollars more in interest than the three-year term, in exchange for the lower monthly installment.

Now shorten the same loan to two years. The monthly payment rises to about four hundred and seventy-one dollars, but the total interest falls to roughly twelve hundred and ninety dollars. The two-year term saves about six hundred and sixty dollars in interest compared to the three-year term, in exchange for a monthly payment that is about one hundred and forty dollars higher.

The three scenarios describe the same ten thousand dollar loan under different terms. The monthly payment moves by a factor of two; the total interest moves by a factor of almost three. That is the trade-off the calculator is designed to expose.

Common mistakes when comparing loans

Comparing the rate instead of the APR. A loan with a lower rate and a high origination fee can cost more than a loan with a slightly higher rate and no fees. The rate is a piece of the cost; the APR is the whole cost.

Assuming a longer term is more affordable. A longer term lowers the monthly payment, but it raises the total cost, sometimes by more than the monthly saving. The right question is not which term has the smaller installment; it is which term the borrower can sustain without falling behind.

Ignoring the compounding on the interest portion. The interest portion of each installment is charged on the balance, and the balance only falls as fast as the principal portion allows. On a long loan the balance moves very slowly in the first years, which means the interest cost compounds for much longer than a naive reading of the schedule suggests.

Not checking the prepayment rules. Some lenders charge a prepayment penalty for early repayment, which reduces or eliminates the benefit of paying extra. Others allow early repayment with no penalty and only a small administrative charge. The prepayment terms matter as much as the interest rate on any loan where the borrower expects to repay early.

Forgetting the insurance requirement. Many vehicle and mortgage loans require compulsory credit life or asset insurance. That cost is usually charged monthly and is separate from the amortization, which means the true monthly outlay is higher than the calculator figure. Ask the lender for the all-in monthly cost including insurance before signing.

Treating the calculator output as the loan contract. A loan calculator models the arithmetic of the principal and the rate you enter. The contract governs the loan. If the contract contains a clause that changes the rate, adds a fee, or alters the term, the contract wins, and the calculator output is only a reference point.

Where this calculator stops

This tool produces a repayment estimate from the amount, rate, and term you enter, on a fixed-rate annuity model. It does not know the fees your lender charges, the insurance the loan requires, the prepayment penalties in the contract, or the late-fee schedule. It does not model variable-rate loans, because those change the payment whenever the reference rate moves, and it does not model balloon payments, interest-only periods, or graduated repayment schedules.

It also does not provide financial advice. Whether a particular loan is affordable, whether a particular term makes sense for a particular household, and whether a particular lender is reputable are questions for a qualified adviser and for the borrower's own judgement, not for a browser calculator. The tool is a planning aid, and the loan contract is the document that governs.

What the tool does give you is a fast, transparent, private picture of what a loan will cost across its full life, and a schedule that shows how the cost is distributed month by month. That view is worth having before you sign, and it is often the difference between choosing a loan that fits a household and choosing one that quietly becomes a burden.

Frequently asked questions

How is a fixed monthly loan payment calculated?

A fixed monthly loan payment uses the annuity formula. The payment is the principal multiplied by the monthly interest rate multiplied by the compounded rate over the full term, divided by the compounded rate minus one. In plain terms, the lender takes the total interest that would accrue over the life of the loan and spreads it evenly across every monthly installment, so the cash amount stays the same from month one to the last month.

What is the difference between interest rate and APR?

The interest rate is the rate the lender charges on the outstanding principal. The Annual Percentage Rate, or APR, includes the interest rate plus the mandatory fees the lender charges to set up the loan, such as an origination fee, a documentation fee, and any mandatory insurance. The APR is therefore always equal to or higher than the nominal interest rate, and it is the more honest comparison figure when you are shopping between loan offers.

Why is most of my early payment going to interest?

Interest is charged on the outstanding balance every month. In the first month, the balance is at its highest, so the interest portion of that month's installment is also at its highest. As the principal is paid down, the balance shrinks, the monthly interest charge falls with it, and a larger share of each installment goes to principal. This is why the principal balance falls slowly at first and faster in the second half of the loan.

Does paying extra every month reduce the total interest?

Yes, and the effect is larger than most borrowers expect. Every extra amount paid above the scheduled installment goes straight to the principal. Because interest is charged on the outstanding balance, a smaller balance means less interest next month, which means the following month's installment pays down even more principal. The compounding works in the borrower's favour, and the loan clears earlier than the original term.

Should I choose a shorter or longer loan term?

A shorter term carries a higher monthly installment but a lower total interest cost, and the loan clears faster. A longer term carries a lower monthly installment but a much higher total interest cost, because interest accrues for more months on a larger average balance. The right choice depends on how much monthly cash flow the borrower can commit and how much buffer they want to keep for emergencies. A term that leaves no monthly buffer is usually the wrong term.

Are there hidden fees a loan calculator will not show?

Yes. A loan calculator models the mathematical payment on the principal and the rate you enter. Real lenders may also charge an origination fee, an insurance requirement, a documentation fee, a prepayment penalty, a late fee, and a bounced-payment charge. None of those are part of the base amortization, so the cash cost of the loan is always at least the calculator figure, and often higher. Ask the lender for the full fee schedule and the APR before you sign.

Can I use this calculator for a variable-rate loan?

The tool assumes a fixed interest rate across the full term, which is the correct model for a fixed-rate loan. For a variable-rate or adjustable-rate loan, the payment changes whenever the reference rate moves, so the correct way to use the calculator is to run several scenarios with different assumed rates. Modelling a best case, a likely case, and a worst case is a more useful way to plan than assuming one rate.

Does this calculator store my loan details?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored on any server. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the loan amount, the interest rate, the term, the monthly payment, the total interest, and the total repayment is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Your name, income, employer, and bank details are never collected because they are never asked for.
