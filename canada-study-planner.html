What this planner actually builds

This tool takes a list of courses, the hours you realistically have available each week, and the days remaining until each course's next major assessment. It then distributes those hours across the courses according to three factors: how difficult each course is, how wide the gap is between your current average and your target, and how soon the next high-stakes deliverable sits on the calendar. The output is a weekly hour budget per course, a seven-day timetable that interleaves subjects, and a set of daily block targets that match whichever focus protocol you picked.

Nothing about this is a magic formula. The arithmetic is transparent and you can replicate it on paper in about ten minutes. What the planner adds is speed, a consistent weighting scheme, and a printable weekly view that keeps the plan visible on a wall or a second screen instead of buried in a notes app. It also splits the week so that no single subject dominates a day, because interleaving is one of the few study techniques with strong experimental support and it is also one of the easiest to skip when you are tired.

The downloadable PDF is the practical anchor of the tool. Plans that stay inside a browser tab tend to be forgotten by Wednesday. Plans that sit above a desk get used. The PDF is formatted as a single clean page with your week's hours, the per-course allocation, and the seven-day grid, and it prints in black, white, and yellow without any of the surrounding page furniture.

The cognitive science behind the schedule

Three research findings shape the algorithm, and none of them are new. The first is that distribution beats concentration. The same total study time produces substantially better long-term retention when it is spread across several days instead of concentrated into one block. This was first measured by Hermann Ebbinghaus in the 1880s and has been confirmed many times since, most thoroughly in the 2013 review by John Dunlosky and colleagues in Psychological Science in the Public Interest. The plan reflects this by scheduling each subject across multiple days in the week rather than stacking it into one or two long sessions.

The second finding is that retrieval beats review. Testing yourself with closed-book recall strengthens memory more than re-reading the same material. The 2006 study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke at Washington University in St. Louis is the standard reference here, and it is the reason the blueprint panel pushes flashcards and problem sets instead of highlighting and re-reading. The plan does not force you to use these methods, but the day-by-day cadence is arranged around the assumption that day three is for recall and day seven is for application.

The third finding is that interleaving beats blocking. When you practise several related topics in a mixed sequence rather than in contiguous blocks, you get better at distinguishing between them and at choosing the right approach when the problem is presented cold. Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor's 2007 paper in Instructional Science is the classic demonstration in mathematics education, and the same effect shows up in language learning and in procedural skills. The planner interleaves subjects within each day for this reason, even though the schedule looks slightly busier than a blocking approach would.

What this does not mean. Interleaving is not the same as multitasking. Switching between two subjects deliberately every hour is different from scrolling through four browser tabs while trying to study. The plan assumes a single block of focused attention on one subject at a time, with subject changes happening at block boundaries.

How Canadian academic terms are structured

Canadian education is governed at the provincial level, which means there is no single national term pattern. The five main structures the planner accounts for are set out below.

SystemTerm shapeAssessment pattern
Ontario high school (OSSD, Grade 11 and 12)Two semesters of four courses each, or four quadmesters of two coursesContinuous assessment with unit tests, a final culminating project, and a final exam worth up to 30 per cent

Alberta (Grade 11 and 12, 30-level courses)Two semesters of four courses each, diploma exams in 30-level core subjectsContinuous assessment with a diploma exam worth 30 per cent of the final blended mark
Quebec CEGEPTwo semesters per year, students typically take five or six coursesContinuous assessment feeding the R-score, with final exams at the end of each semester
Canadian university and collegeFall, Winter, and optional Summer terms, each with a midterm cluster and a final exam periodTwo midterms and a final in most science and engineering courses; a midterm and final plus papers in most arts courses

The distinction that matters most for planning is when the high-stakes deliverables sit. Alberta and Ontario high school courses tend to weight their final exams at twenty to thirty per cent, with the rest coming from continuous assessment. CEGEP courses weight continuous assessment more heavily because every mark contributes to the R-score. University midterms cluster in the second half of October and in mid-February, and reading week sits either side of that cluster depending on the institution. A plan that assumes a single big exam at the end of term will be wrong for most Canadian students, which is why the planner asks for the days remaining until the next major assessment rather than the end of term.

How the allocation algorithm works

The calculation has three inputs per course and produces one output: the number of hours per week that course should get.

Difficulty weight. Each course is tagged as low, moderate, high, or challenging. A challenging course, typically a lab science or an advanced mathematics sequence, carries a weight of 1.45. A high-difficulty course carries 1.25. A moderate course carries 1.00, and a low-difficulty course carries 0.80. The weight shifts the relative share but does not change the total.

Grade gap factor. The tool calculates the gap between the current average and the target. A five-point gap widens the course's share by roughly 7.5 per cent, a ten-point gap by about 15 per cent, and a fifteen-point gap by roughly 22 per cent. The effect is bounded, because a course where the student is already at the target still needs some attention, and a course where the student is far behind cannot absorb the whole week's hours without sacrificing everything else.

Urgency factor. The days remaining until the next major assessment determine how much the course's share is boosted in the current week. A course with an exam within seven days gets a 1.60 multiplier, within fourteen days gets 1.35, within twenty-one days gets 1.15, and beyond that sits at 1.00. The urgency boost is temporary, and the planner assumes you will re-run it every one to two weeks as assessments pass and new ones appear on the horizon.

The three factors multiply together into a raw score for each course, and the raw scores are then normalised so that the sum of all hours exactly equals the weekly budget you entered. Rounding is applied at the end so the total matches to one decimal place. The result is a distribution that puts more weight on courses that need more work, but that never starves any course entirely.

A worked Ontario Grade 12 example

Take a Grade 12 student in Ontario with four courses in the fall semester and twenty-four hours available each week for independent study. The four courses are MCV4U Calculus and Vectors, SPH4U Physics, SCH4U Chemistry, and ENG4U English. The student's current averages are eighty-four, eighty-six, eighty-eight, and ninety respectively, and their targets are ninety-four, ninety-two, ninety-two, and ninety-two. The next major deliverables sit at twelve days for calculus, sixteen days for physics, eight days for chemistry, and twenty days for English.

The raw scores come out approximately as follows. Calculus: difficulty 1.45 (challenging), gap factor 1.15 (ten-point gap), urgency 1.35 (twelve days) gives 2.25. Physics: difficulty 1.25, gap factor 1.09 (six-point gap), urgency 1.35 (sixteen days) gives 1.84. Chemistry: difficulty 1.25, gap factor 1.06 (four-point gap), urgency 1.60 (eight days) gives 2.12. English: difficulty 1.00, gap factor 1.03 (two-point gap), urgency 1.15 (twenty days) gives 1.18.

Summing the raw scores gives 7.39. Distributing the twenty-four-hour budget proportionally gives roughly 7.3 hours to calculus, 6.0 hours to physics, 6.9 hours to chemistry, and 3.8 hours to English. Rounded, that is about seven and a half hours of calculus, six of physics, seven of chemistry, and four of English per week. The chemistry allocation is boosted by its imminent quiz, and the English allocation is kept modest because the student is already close to their target and the next deliverable is not close.

That distribution is the number the planner hands back. The specific algorithm is one of many reasonable weightings, and a student who runs the same numbers by hand with slightly different multipliers will get a similar but not identical answer. What matters is that the allocation is systematic, transparent, and anchored in the three factors that actually differentiate one course from another.

A worked first-year university example

Now take a first-year science student at a Canadian university with five courses and thirty hours a week for independent study. The courses are MATH 100 Differential Calculus, CHEM 121 with a three-hour lab, BIOL 112 Cell Biology, PHYS 101 Energy and Waves, and WRDS 150 Research Writing. Current averages are around eighty, seventy-eight, eighty-two, eighty-five, and eighty-six, and targets are ninety, eighty-five, ninety, eighty-eight, and eighty-eight. The next major deliverables sit at eighteen, twenty-five, twelve, twenty, and ten days respectively.

WRDS 150 has the nearest deliverable at ten days, so it gets a strong urgency boost even though it is a moderate-difficulty course. CHEM 121 has the heaviest difficulty weighting because of the lab component. BIOL 112 has the widest gap between current and target, so it gets a substantial gap factor. MATH 100 sits in the middle on every dimension and ends up with a modest allocation that reflects its steady workload rather than any single spike.

The result, rounded, is approximately seven hours for MATH 100, seven and a half for CHEM 121, seven for BIOL 112, five for PHYS 101, and three and a half for WRDS 150. The total is thirty hours. The student would then re-run the planner every two weeks, because the deliverable dates shift and the urgency weighting is designed to respond to that.

What this example shows is that a plan with five courses of varying weight does not look like a flat spread of six hours per course. It looks like a distribution with a clear peak around the courses that need the most attention in the current two-week window. That is the point. A study plan that treats all courses equally is almost always a plan that under-serves the courses where the risk is highest.

Five mistakes that ruin a study plan

Most plans fail for one of a small handful of reasons, and none of them is a shortage of good intentions.

Overestimating the hours available. A student who enters forty weekly study hours when they realistically have twenty-two will abandon the plan by week two. Enter the number you can actually sustain, even if it feels low. A plan that runs for twelve weeks at twenty hours a week beats a plan that runs for two weeks at forty.

Treating all courses as equally weighted. A first-year biology course and a fourth-year seminar in the same student's timetable do not require the same weekly hours. If your plan splits the week evenly, it is probably over-serving the courses you find easiest and under-serving the ones that need the most work.

Skipping the interleaving. Spending a full Saturday on one course feels productive, and it usually is not. The brain consolidates better when it revisits a subject on multiple days rather than grinding through it in one long block. The seven-day grid exists to make interleaving the default rather than a decision you have to make each morning.

Ignoring the deadlines. A plan that ignores upcoming assessment dates is a plan that will be wrong by the middle of the term. The urgency factor exists because exams do not spread themselves evenly across the semester, and the week before a midterm looks nothing like the week after one.

Not reviewing the plan. The numbers shift as the term progresses. A plan built in week one is out of date by week four. The best practice is to re-run the planner every two weeks, enter the new deliverable dates, and adjust the hours so the heaviest load sits on the courses with the nearest deadlines.

Where this planner stops

This tool builds a weekly template based on your inputs. It does not read your calendar, does not know your work schedule, does not account for the weeks when you have three midterms at once, and does not know which courses you find genuinely difficult versus which ones simply have a heavy reputation. It also cannot know whether you actually follow the plan, which is the only factor that determines whether any of this works.

What it does provide is a clean, printable starting point that a student can put up on a wall and follow. The allocation logic is straightforward, the weighting is transparent, and the PDF gives you something physical to return to each morning. If the plan does not fit your real week, change the hours input and re-run it. If a course turns out to be harder or easier than expected, change the difficulty rating. The point of a planner is to be useful, not to be perfect.

The tool also does not model the effect of part-time work, long commutes, or family responsibilities. Those factors consume real hours and they belong in the weekly hours field rather than being ignored. A student working fifteen hours a week who enters forty hours of study time is not being ambitious, they are being unrealistic, and the plan will fail for that reason rather than any flaw in the arithmetic.

Frequently asked questions

How many hours per week should a Canadian university student study outside of lectures?

The standard guideline at Canadian universities is two to three hours of independent study for every one hour spent in lectures and labs. A full-time semester of five courses usually means fifteen to eighteen contact hours per week, so the corresponding independent study load sits between thirty and forty hours. Many students find that thirty hours is a realistic target for a first-year science or engineering programme, while humanities and social science degrees often sit closer to twenty-five hours. The number that matters is not the total in the abstract but the total you can actually sustain across the whole term without burning out by week eight.

What is the Pomodoro technique and does it work for exam preparation?

The Pomodoro technique breaks study into short, focused intervals separated by brief breaks. The classic version uses twenty-five minutes of work followed by five minutes of rest, with a longer break every four cycles. The strength of the method is that it lowers the cost of starting a session and it protects against the drift that takes over long, unstructured blocks. For exam preparation, the technique works best as scaffolding around active recall and past-paper practice. A twenty-five minute block of reading is worth less than the same block spent doing closed-book retrieval, so pair the timing protocol with a method that actually forces recall.

What is spaced repetition and why is it better than cramming?

Spaced repetition is the practice of reviewing material at increasing intervals rather than in a single long block. The underlying research, dating back to Ebbinghaus in the 1880s and refined in several large studies since, shows that the same total study time produces substantially better long-term retention when it is distributed across days rather than concentrated in one sitting. Cramming can produce a passable exam result in the short term, but the information fades within days and it leaves nothing behind for the next course that builds on it. Spaced repetition trades a small amount of short-term discomfort for a large gain in durable memory.

How should I prepare for Alberta 30-level diploma exams?

Alberta diploma exams count for thirty per cent of the final blended mark in most 30-level courses, so the exam itself carries real weight. The most efficient preparation is timed practice on official past papers published on the Alberta Education Quest A+ portal. Work through at least four or five released diploma exams under exam conditions, review every wrong answer, and pay attention to the pacing required for the three-hour format. Reviewing content is useful, but the students who gain the most from the final three weeks are the ones who practise under the exam's actual time pressure.

How does reading week fit into a study plan?

Reading week sits in the middle of the winter term at most Canadian universities, and its purpose is catch-up and midterm preparation rather than a true break. The pattern that works well is to spend the first two days consolidating outstanding notes and finishing overdue assignments, the middle three or four days on intensive spaced review for the midterms that cluster right after the break, and the final two days on rest. Skipping the rest days is a common mistake and it usually costs more in the last four weeks of term than it saves during reading week itself.

Can I hold a part-time job and still keep my grades up?

Many Canadian students work ten to fifteen hours a week during the academic term and still graduate with strong records. Above twenty hours a week during a full course load, the risk of dropping a grade band rises sharply, particularly in first year. If you need to work, block your shifts at fixed times each week, protect the study windows that surround them, and treat the study blocks as non-negotiable in the same way you would treat the shifts themselves. The students who struggle most are not the ones who work a lot, but the ones who never decide in advance when they will study.

Why does the tool ask for a target grade rather than just my current average?

Because the gap between your current standing and your target is one of the strongest signals of how much review a course actually needs. A student sitting at eighty-two per cent and aiming for ninety-two needs a very different weekly plan from a student sitting at eighty-eight and aiming for the same target, even if their final goal is identical. The gap factor in the algorithm widens the study allocation for courses where the required improvement is larger, and it narrows it for courses where the student is already close to the goal.

Does the tool work for CEGEP students in Quebec?

Yes. The CEGEP education level uses the same spaced-repetition scheduling logic that applies to other Canadian systems, with additional weight given to the continuous assessment pattern that feeds the R-score. CEGEP students typically sit more frequent evaluations than university students in the same subject, so the weekly review cadence matters more than a single exam countdown. Enter each course separately, include the date of the next major evaluation in the days field, and the plan will build a review rhythm that matches the CEGEP assessment pattern.

How do I actually download the timetable as a PDF?

Press the Download Timetable PDF button inside the results section. The browser opens its native print dialog with the timetable formatted for paper. Choose Save as PDF from the destination menu, set the margins to default, and enable background graphics so the yellow accents print properly. The resulting PDF is a single clean page showing your weekly hours, the course allocation breakdown, and the seven-day timetable. You can print it, keep it open on a second screen, or pin it above your desk.

Does the tool store my courses or grades?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the education level, weekly hours, technique, course count, top urgency days, and total weekly study blocks is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Course names, grades, and target averages never leave the browser.
