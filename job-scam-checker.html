What this checker does and how recruitment fraud operates

This utility scans the full text of a job advert, email, SMS, LinkedIn direct message, Telegram invite, or WhatsApp forward inside your browser and flags linguistic, financial, and contact patterns strongly linked to employment fraud. Within one second of clicking Scan for red flags, it produces a weighted 0–100 risk score, breaks down every triggered rule by category, checks five screening benchmarks, and suggests whether the message looks broadly normal, requires caution, or warrants immediate disengagement.

Recruitment fraud succeeds because scammers exploit urgency, aspiration, and asymmetric information. Job seekers who have sent dozens of applications over several months often feel relieved when a recruiter reaches out proactively with an offer letter or promises immediate shortlisting. Fraud rings deliberately design their messages to bypass sceptical review by copying logos from real multinational companies, naming genuine government ministries, quoting plausible corporate registration numbers, and pressuring candidates to pay or send identity documents within hours before a "slot closes."

Because privacy matters when you are evaluating sensitive career messages or private email headers, the scanner runs 100% locally in JavaScript. Nothing you paste into the text box is saved to a database or sent across the internet. Only if you manually click the optional Get AI insight button are anonymous counts (such as number of triggered flags, numerical risk score, and top category name) sent once to generate a brief written readout; your pasted advert text and personal details never leave your device.

How the 0–100 risk score and 11 rule weights work

Instead of guessing vaguely whether text sounds suspicious, the engine tests your pasted message against eleven distinct rule categories derived from published advisories by the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the Australian ACCC Scamwatch, the Ghana Cyber Security Authority (CSA), Nigeria's Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC), the South African Police Service (SAPS), SAFPS, and Action Fraud UK. Each matched category adds its fixed risk weight once, and the cumulative score is capped at 100.

Red flags that separate fraudulent adverts from verified hiring

Fraudsters rarely rely on a single lie; instead, they combine persuasive formatting with small structural anomalies that a legitimate HR workflow avoids. Comparing an unverified social recruitment message against how corporate hiring actually functions reveals those differences immediately.

  • Official career portal vs forwarded chat screenshot: Real companies publish open vacancies on an official corporate career page (company.com/careers) or verifiable job boards linked from their main site. Scammers push PDF flyers or copy-pasted text chains designed to spread in WhatsApp groups without a single verifiable company URL.
  • Structured selection vs instant appointment: A real employer takes time to screen CVs, check references, and conduct at least one live audio, video, or in-person interview because hiring the wrong employee is expensive. Any "instant offer" after filling out a Google Form or texting a WhatsApp contact is engineered to move straight to fee extraction.
  • Clear operational duties vs generic buzzwords: Genuine job specifications explain tools, workflows, shift hours, reporting manager, and qualifications. Fraudulent notices promise flexible hours from home using only a smartphone with no prior skills required.

Upfront fees, fake cheques, and equipment reimbursement traps

Every major consumer protection agency enforces one absolute boundary: legitimate employers never charge candidates money to apply, interview, train, or receive work equipment. Even in regulated industries where background checks or uniforms exist, legitimate employers arrange and pay those vendors directly—or deduct a clearly documented statutory uniform/equipment allowance only after an employee is on payroll with a countersigned contract.

Scammers constantly rotate the label they put on candidate payments so victims do not recognize the pattern:

  • "Registration," "form," or "slot reservation" fees: Common in fake government agency, banking, oil-and-gas, and security recruitment posts. Victims are told only a small fee (for example ₦3,500–₦25,000, GHS 150–600, or R500–R2,800) secures an aptitude test code or shortlist spot.
  • "Medical clearance" or "background check" deposits: Scammers send an appointment letter instructing the victim to transfer cash to a specific "accredited clinic" or "vetting officer" phone number. Both the clinic and vetting officer are controlled by the scammer.
  • "Equipment reimbursement" or fake-cheque schemes: Common in fake remote US, UK, and international assistant offers. The scammer mails or emails a mobile-deposit cheque (or fake bank transfer confirmation) for $1,500–$3,500, tells the candidate to keep $300 as a sign-on bonus, and instructs them to wire the balance to an "authorized company laptop supplier" via Zelle, gift cards, or cryptocurrency. Banks temporarily make cheque funds look available under clearing rules, then reverse the entire amount weeks later when the cheque bounces—leaving the victim personally liable to the bank for every cent wired out.

Zero-exception rule: If any recruiter, HR contact, or "onboarding coordinator" asks you to pay money—whether via bank transfer, MTN MoMo, Telecel Cash, M-Pesa, Capitec/eWallet, gift cards, or USDT—end communication immediately. No legitimate job costs money to obtain.

Task scams, WhatsApp recruiting, and domain spoofing

Over recent years, two digital tactics have overtaken traditional classifieds fraud: encrypted messenger outreach and algorithmic "task scams."

In a task scam, victims receive an unsolicited WhatsApp or Telegram message claiming to represent an e-commerce platform, digital marketing agency, or app-optimization firm. The recruiter explains that merchants pay commissions to click "like," submit product ratings, or simulate checkout orders for 30 minutes a day. To hook scepticism, the scammer often sends a real small payout ($5 to $25 equivalent in local currency or USDT) after your first batch of dummy clicks. Once you believe the system actually pays, the dashboard assigns a "lucky combination order" or "VIP batch" showing a negative account balance that you must top up with your own money before you can withdraw your accumulated commission. Each top-up triggers a slightly larger negative order until the victim runs out of money and the Telegram group vanishes.

In domain and identity spoofing, scammers create free consumer emails that mimic corporate brands (for example dangote.hr.careers@gmail.com, careers.shellnigeria@outlook.com, or shoprite.recruitment.sa@yahoo.com), or register lookalike domains that differ by one subtle word or extension (such as company-careers.org instead of company.com). Remember that anybody in the world can register an @gmail.com address containing a famous multinational's name in two minutes for free. Real HR teams send interview invitations strictly from their company's primary authenticated domain.

Unrealistic pay, vague duties, and market salary checks

Pay figures far above prevailing local or remote market wages exist to create emotional excitement that overrides caution. When a listing promises ₦150,000 per week, GHS 3,000 per week, or $45 per hour for "copy-pasting data," "attaching tags," or "reposting photos" with zero prior experience, pause and perform two sanity checks:

  1. Value-creation test: Ask why a profit-seeking business would pay a remote beginner triple the salary of an experienced university graduate when automated software handles basic data entry or simple rating tasks for fractions of a cent.
  2. Market-band comparison: Compare the quoted wage against real active listings on established corporate portals in your country. You can also calculate net after-tax take-home pay for salaried offers using AfriWidget's salary calculators (such as the PAYE & Net Salary Calculator for Nigeria, Ghana, South Africa, and Kenya, or the Hourly-to-Salary Calculator) to spot implausible net-pay promises.

Identity theft: why scammers harvest BVN, NIN, ID cards, and passports

Even when a scammer asks for zero money upfront, sending unverified recruiters a full KYC packet can cause financial damage that lasts years. Fraud syndicates collect high-resolution scans of national identity cards (Nigeria NIN slips, Ghana Cards, South African Green Barcoded IDs or Smart IDs, Kenyan National IDs, UK/US driving licences, and international passports) together with dates of birth, home addresses, bank account numbers, BVN digits, and selfie photos.

That identity packet enables four specific secondary crimes:

  • Fraudulent instant digital loans: Unregulated or poorly secured mobile lending apps sometimes issue credit lines using national ID images, BVN numbers, and spoofed phone records—leaving the victim chasing collections disputes for loans they never requested.
  • Bank account and SIM-swap compromise: Knowing your full legal name, date of birth, national ID number, and bank account details gives social engineers the exact security answers needed to attempt SIM swaps or reset banking credentials.
  • Mule account opening and money laundering: Stolen identity documents are used to pass remote KYC checks on fintech wallets and crypto exchanges, routing proceeds of other scams under your legal name.
  • Persona recycling: Your stolen selfie and ID card become the "verified recruiter profile photo and ID badge" shown to the next job seeker to win their trust.

Safe document timing rule: During application and interview stages, share only your CV, portfolio links, city/region (never your full house number or BVN/NIN), and a professional email address. Supply government tax IDs, bank account numbers for payroll, and passport copies only after you have passed verifiable interviews, inspected the company physically or over live corporate video, and hold a countersigned employment contract.

Three worked message audits: task scam, fake offer letter, and borderline local SME

Running real-world scenarios through the tool shows how to read individual flags alongside context rather than treating every single flag mechanically.

Worked Audit 1 · WhatsApp / Telegram Product-Boosting Trap

Message text

URGENT HIRING! Work from home using your phone. Earn ₦150,000 per week rating products and simple online tasks. No experience needed, limited slots left today! Send ₦5,000 registration fee to secure your slot. Chat on WhatsApp 08012345678 immediately.

Scanner readout: Triggers Upfront fee (+25), Suspicious contact / WhatsApp (+18), Unrealistic salary (+20), Vague description (+12), Urgency (+15), and All-caps urgency (+8) = 98 / 100 (High risk — Do not engage). Even before reading the ₦5,000 fee line, the combination of effortless high weekly pay, vague phone tasks, and anonymous WhatsApp outreach clearly marks a classic task/registration scam.

Worked Audit 2 · Fake Corporate Offer Letter & KYC Harvest

Message text

Congratulations! Based on your CV, you have been selected automatically with no interview required to join our multinational energy partner. Reply to hr.recruitment.desk2026@gmail.com with a scanned copy of your passport, national ID, BVN, and bank account details within 24 hours to begin processing fee clearance.

Scanner readout: Triggers Upfront fee / processing (+25), Sensitive info / passport + ID + BVN + bank (+22), Suspicious contact / @gmail.com (+18), Offer without interview (+18), and No verifiable company (+14) = 97 / 100 (High risk — Do not engage). Notice how omitting a flashy weekly pay figure does not save this message: an immediate offer without an interview coupled with passport/BVN harvesting and a free Gmail address is unmistakable identity and fee fraud.

Worked Audit 3 · Borderline Genuine Local SME Vacancy

Message text

Accra Accounts Officer needed at Osu Retail Ltd (2 years bookkeeping experience, Tally or QuickBooks required, full-time Mon-Fri). Send CV to careers@osuretail.example or drop questions via WhatsApp office line 0241234567. Salary GHS 3,200/month.

Scanner readout: Triggers only Suspicious contact / WhatsApp mentioned (+18) = 18 / 100 (Low risk). Many genuine West and East African small businesses list a corporate email alongside a WhatsApp reception desk line. Because the role specifies concrete accounting software, clear work hours, realistic local salary, zero upfront fees, and zero sensitive document requests, 18/100 tells you not to panic—simply confirm the corporate email domain, check RGD registration, and attend an in-person office interview safely.

Five-step independent verification checklist before replying

Before answering any job message that triggers even one flag or comes from an unsolicited source, run this five-step check in under five minutes without using any link, phone number, or email provided inside the suspicious message itself.

  1. Inspect the exact sender domain and type the corporate site manually: Check whether the reply-to address matches the organization's real primary domain. Open a fresh browser window and search or type the official domain yourself—never tap a link inside a WhatsApp or SMS message.
  2. Look for the exact vacancy on the official careers portal: Genuine multinational, bank, and government vacancies almost always appear on their official website's careers section or verifiable LinkedIn company page under matching title and job ID.
  3. Check the corporate registry: Look up the registered entity name on CAC (Nigeria), RGD/ORC (Ghana), CIPC (South Africa), Companies House (UK), or local equivalents. Brand-new entities created days before a mass recruitment campaign—or firms whose registered trade has zero connection to the offered job—deserve extreme caution.
  4. Call the official switchboard out-of-band: Find the company's reception phone number on its real website (not the number in the advert), dial it, and ask HR to confirm whether they are hiring for that role and whether the named individual works there.
  5. Enforce the zero-fee and document-hold boundary: Refuse any fee under any pretext and withhold IDs, passports, bank details, and BVN/NIN numbers until after a verifiable live interview and formal signed offer. Keep a clean record of legitimate applications in the Job Application Tracker so you always know which companies you genuinely applied to.

If you already sent money or documents: Stop all communication immediately, preserve screenshots and bank transaction references, notify your bank or mobile money provider's fraud desk immediately to freeze compromised accounts or recall transfers, alert your credit bureau or national fraud registry (such as SAFPS protective registration in South Africa), and file an official report with EFCC, CSA, SAPS, Action Fraud, or the FTC.

Where this checker stops and screening disclaimer

This scanner is a deterministic, pattern-based educational text analyzer—not an identity verification bureau, background-check service, or law-enforcement database. Because it runs strictly offline inside your browser without querying WHOIS servers, corporate registries, email MX records, or external threat feeds, it cannot prove whether a named company actually posted a vacancy, whether an email domain was legitimately authenticated via SPF/DKIM, or whether a clean-looking advert is a carefully written phishing attack.

Conversely, an informal advert from a genuine small business (such as a local shop asking applicants to message an office WhatsApp line) can legitimately trigger one or two style flags without being a scam. Treat the score and breakdown purely as an informational first-pass risk estimate—not legal advice—and always confirm employer authenticity independently before sharing personal data, attending distant locations alone, or making any financial commitment.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most common signs of a job scam?

The most common signs are: a request for an upfront payment for training, equipment, or a processing fee; contact through a personal WhatsApp, Telegram, or Gmail account instead of a corporate domain; an offer letter or job without any real interview; a salary that is far above the market rate for the role; vague job duties; and a request for sensitive personal information such as your bank details, ID number, or passport before you have signed a contract.

Should I ever pay money to get a job?

No. Legitimate employers never charge candidates for applications, interviews, training, or equipment. Any request for payment from a recruiter or employer is a clear sign of fraud. This rule applies everywhere, including Nigeria, Ghana, South Africa, the UK, the US, and Australia. Government labour departments and fraud prevention agencies state this plainly.

How can I verify if a job offer is real?

First, check the company's official website and look for a careers page. Second, search the company name plus the word 'scam' or 'complaint'. Third, call the company's main switchboard number (found on their official website, not from the message you received) and ask if the recruiter works there. Fourth, check the email domain: legitimate recruiters use corporate email addresses, not free Gmail or Yahoo accounts. Fifth, if the offer involves a fee, stop immediately.

What should I do if I think I have been scammed?

Stop all contact with the scammer immediately. Do not send any more money or information. Report the scam to your national fraud reporting body: the EFCC in Nigeria, the Cyber Security Authority in Ghana, the SAPS or SAFPS in South Africa, Action Fraud in the UK, or the FTC in the US. If you shared bank details, contact your bank straight away to freeze or monitor the account. If you shared identity documents, report that to the relevant identity theft service as well.

Do legitimate employers use WhatsApp or Gmail for recruitment?

In most cases, no. Legitimate employers and recruitment agencies use corporate email domains and their official applicant tracking systems. Some smaller companies may use a personal email for initial contact, but they will quickly move to a formal process. Contact exclusively through WhatsApp, Telegram, or a personal Gmail account with no corporate footprint is a major red flag.

Why do job scams target young people?

Job scams target young people, recent graduates, and anyone urgently seeking work because they are motivated, less familiar with recruitment norms, and often under financial pressure. Fraudsters exploit that pressure with promises of fast money and minimal effort. The best defence is knowing the red flags and never paying for a job.

Can a job scam steal my identity?

Yes. Many job scams are designed not just to take money but to harvest personal information. Scammers ask for a copy of your ID, your bank details, your passport number, or your date of birth under the guise of 'onboarding paperwork'. That information can then be used for identity theft, loan fraud, or to open accounts in your name. Never send sensitive documents to an unverified recruiter.

Is this tool an official fraud reporting service?

No. This is an independent educational checker that scans pasted text for known red flags. It does not report anything to any authority, it does not verify whether a company is legitimate, and it does not store anything you paste. Use it as a first-pass screening tool, and always follow up with the official verification methods recommended by your country's fraud prevention agency.

Does the checker store the text I paste?

No. Every scan runs entirely inside your browser tab. The text you paste never leaves your device. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the number of red flags and the risk score is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. The text of the job advert itself is never uploaded.

What is a realistic salary for an entry-level job?

Realistic salaries vary widely by country and sector, but the key point is comparison. If a job advert promises a salary that is several times the typical entry-level rate for that role in that location, and the duties sound simple or vague, treat it as a red flag. Fraudsters use unrealistic pay to override your scepticism. Check salary benchmarks on official statistics sites or reputable salary surveys for your country before you trust an unusual number.

Why do fake recruiters ask for a BVN, NIN, Ghana Card, or South African ID before an interview?

Fraudsters collect national identity numbers, bank verification numbers (BVN), passport scans, and birth dates under the excuse of payroll setup or security clearance. Because lenders, mobile money wallets, and digital credit apps use those identifiers to verify identity, stolen onboarding packets let criminals open fraudulent accounts, apply for instant loans, or impersonate you in future scams.

What is a WhatsApp or Telegram task scam and how does it work?

A task scam begins with an unsolicited message offering easy daily income for liking videos, boosting online shop ratings, or completing simple app reviews. After paying a small initial amount to build trust, the platform claims your account must deposit money or crypto to unlock higher-paying tasks or withdraw frozen earnings, trapping victims in escalating deposit demands.

Are embossed PDF appointment letters from oil, banking, or logistics firms proof that a job is real?

No. Anyone can copy a real company logo, registration number, and executive signature into a PDF template within minutes. Fraudsters routinely attach polished offer letters from well-known oil companies, airlines, NGOs, and government agencies right before demanding a medical screening fee, visa fee, or training deposit. Always verify directly through the organisation's official website and switchboard.

How can I verify a recruiter without tipping off a scammer?

Do not reply to the suspicious email or WhatsApp thread while checking. Open a fresh browser tab, manually type the company's official web address, check its careers portal for the exact vacancy, inspect the corporate registry (CAC in Nigeria, RGD in Ghana, CIPC in South Africa, or Companies House in the UK), and call the switchboard number listed on the official website to confirm whether the named recruiter and email domain are genuine.
