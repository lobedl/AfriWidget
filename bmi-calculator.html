What this calculator actually does

This page does three things, and it does them separately. It works out your body mass index, which is a rough measure of whether your weight is proportionate to your height. It works out your basal metabolic rate, which is roughly how many calories your body would burn in a day if you did nothing but rest. And it works out your total daily energy expenditure, which is your basal rate adjusted for how active you actually are.

Each of those numbers answers a slightly different question. BMI answers "roughly where does my weight sit for my height?" BMR answers "how much energy do I burn just existing?" TDEE answers "how much energy do I burn on a real day, given the way I live?" Used together, they give you a clearer picture than any one of them alone.

Every calculation runs inside your browser. When you press Calculate, the maths happens on your device using the numbers you typed. The result appears on your screen and disappears the moment you close the tab. The one exception is the optional AI insight button further up this page. That button sends a short, anonymous summary of your result to a small serverless function only when you press it, and only to generate a short written explanation. If you never press it, nothing about your calculation leaves your device at all.

What BMI measures, and what it does not

Body mass index is the relationship between your weight and your height. You take your weight in kilograms, divide it by the square of your height in metres, and you have your BMI. That is the entire formula. It was first proposed in the 1830s by a Belgian statistician named Adolphe Quetelet, who was trying to describe populations rather than individuals. That origin still matters, because it explains what BMI is good at and where it goes wrong.

At a population level, BMI is genuinely useful. Across millions of people, there is a consistent statistical relationship between a higher BMI and a higher risk of conditions like type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, and joint problems. Public health agencies rely on it because it is fast, cheap, and reasonably predictive when applied broadly.

At an individual level, though, BMI has real blind spots. It cannot tell the difference between muscle and fat. It cannot tell where on your body your weight sits, which matters a lot because fat around the abdomen carries far more metabolic risk than fat around the hips and thighs. It cannot tell whether your weight has changed because you gained muscle or lost it. It is, in short, a broad screening tool, not a full assessment.

A rugby player at the top of his game, weighing 105 kilograms at 1.85 metres, will calculate a BMI of 30.7 and be classified as obese by the standard thresholds. He is not obese. His body composition is dominated by muscle. A similar number can show up for someone with very little muscle and a lot of visceral fat, whose risk profile is completely different. BMI sees neither of them clearly.

The WHO BMI categories

The World Health Organization publishes the categories most countries use. They were designed for adults aged twenty and older. Here is the full table, along with the clinical meaning of each band.

Severe underweightBelow 16.0Significant nutritional deficit. Medical review is important.
Moderate underweight16.0 – 16.9Elevated risk of hormonal and immune issues.
Mild underweight17.0 – 18.4Slightly below the healthy range. Worth watching.

Overweight25.0 – 29.9Modestly raised risk of metabolic and joint conditions.

Obesity class 235.0 – 39.9High risk of metabolic and cardiovascular complications.
Obesity class 340.0 and aboveVery high risk. Medical supervision is strongly recommended.

Two caveats apply to this table. First, the WHO itself recommends lower thresholds for people of South Asian, East Asian, and some African ancestries, because the same BMI tends to correspond to more body fat and more metabolic risk in those populations. Under the adjusted thresholds, overweight begins at 23.0 and obesity at 27.5. Second, the table does not apply to children, teenagers, pregnant women, or highly muscular athletes. Each of those groups needs different reference points.

BMI Prime in plain terms

BMI Prime is an alternative way of reading the same number. Instead of expressing your BMI on its own, you divide it by 25, which is the upper edge of the healthy range. The result tells you how far you are from that edge as a proportion.

A BMI Prime of 0.85 means you are at 85 percent of the healthy ceiling. A BMI Prime of 1.00 means you are exactly at the boundary between healthy and overweight. A BMI Prime of 1.20 means you are twenty percent above the healthy ceiling. A BMI Prime of 0.72 means you are twenty-eight percent below it, and therefore underweight.

The reason some clinicians prefer BMI Prime is that it makes the comparison to the healthy limit more intuitive. If your BMI is 27.5, the number alone does not tell you much. If your BMI Prime is 1.10, you immediately know you are ten percent above the healthy limit. That framing can be easier to work with when you are setting a target.

Why BMI is only a starting point

If BMI is this limited, why does anyone use it? Because it is a fast, universally available first filter. A doctor with ten minutes and no equipment can take your height and weight and get a number that flags whether a deeper conversation is needed. That is genuinely useful. The mistake is treating that first filter as the final answer.

Three tools fill in the picture that BMI misses.

Waist circumference is the simplest. Measure around your belly button with a tape measure, without pulling it tight. For most adults, risk rises when the waist goes above 94 centimetres (37 inches) for men and 80 centimetres (31.5 inches) for women. Above 102 centimetres (40 inches) for men and 88 centimetres (35 inches) for women, the risk is substantially higher.

Waist-to-height ratio is even simpler. Divide your waist measurement by your height. If the answer is below 0.5, you are in a good place. If it is above 0.5, you are carrying more abdominal fat than is ideal, regardless of what your BMI says. This single ratio is one of the strongest predictors of cardiometabolic risk available without laboratory testing.

Body fat percentage gives you the most detail, but it is harder to measure accurately outside a clinic. The most accurate methods involve equipment that most people do not have access to. Consumer scales and handheld devices give numbers that vary widely from one device to the next. A rough estimate is usually enough for tracking your own progress over time, as long as you use the same method each time and do not treat the exact figure as gospel.

BMR and the Mifflin-St Jeor equation

Your basal metabolic rate is the amount of energy your body uses to keep you alive while you are at complete rest. Your heart is beating, your lungs are breathing, your kidneys are filtering, your brain is running, your cells are repairing themselves. All of that costs energy, and BMR is the measure of that cost over twenty-four hours.

For most people, BMR accounts for sixty to seventy-five percent of everything you burn in a day. That is why it matters so much: even a modest change to your baseline metabolism has a bigger effect on your daily energy budget than an extra hour at the gym.

The equation this calculator uses is called the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, and it was published in 1990 in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. It is the most widely used modern predictive formula because a large body of research has shown it to be more accurate than the older Harris-Benedict equations for most people. Here is the formula itself.

For men: BMR = (10 × weight in kilograms) + (6.25 × height in centimetres) − (5 × age in years) + 5

For women: BMR = (10 × weight in kilograms) + (6.25 × height in centimetres) − (5 × age in years) − 161

Notice that the only difference between the two is the final constant: plus five for men, minus one hundred and sixty-one for women. This reflects the average difference in lean body mass and hormonal profile between the two groups. It is an average, not a rule, which is one reason the number you get is an estimate rather than a precise reading.

To see it in action, take a thirty-five year old man weighing 80 kilograms at 180 centimetres. His BMR is (10 × 80) + (6.25 × 180) − (5 × 35) + 5, which equals 800 + 1,125 − 175 + 5, for a total of 1,755 calories per day. If you fed him exactly that many calories and he truly did nothing but rest, his weight would stay roughly steady.

TDEE and activity levels

Nobody actually rests all day. You walk to the bathroom, you make food, you sit at a desk, you stand in a queue, you carry shopping, you play with your children, you go for a run. All of that adds to your energy expenditure, and the combined total is what we call your total daily energy expenditure, or TDEE.

To estimate TDEE, the calculator multiplies your BMR by an activity multiplier. The five options on the form correspond to the five standard multipliers used in nutrition research.

  • Sedentary (1.20): A desk job, minimal walking, no structured exercise. This is the correct choice for most people who work in offices or at computers and do not exercise deliberately.
  • Lightly active (1.375): Light exercise one to three days a week, or a job that involves some walking, such as teaching or retail.
  • Moderately active (1.55): Moderate exercise three to five days a week, or a fairly physical job combined with light training.
  • Very active (1.725): Hard training six or seven days a week, or a physically demanding job combined with regular exercise.
  • Extra active (1.90): A very physical job such as construction, combined with twice-daily training, or full-time athletic preparation.

The single most common mistake people make with these calculators is picking a multiplier that flatters them. Studies that compared self-reported activity to measured activity consistently find that most people overestimate how much they move. If you work a desk job, exercise three times a week for an hour, and walk a bit on weekends, you are probably in the lightly active bracket, not the very active one. Choosing the higher number does not make your body burn more calories. It just makes your estimate wrong.

Turning numbers into a daily calorie target

Once you have a TDEE, you have a reference point. That figure is roughly the number of calories you would need to eat each day to stay at the same weight. From there, the adjustments are simple arithmetic.

To lose fat, subtract somewhere between 250 and 500 calories. The smaller figure produces slower but more comfortable progress, usually around a quarter of a kilogram per week. The larger figure produces about half a kilogram per week, which is the rate most guidelines consider safe and sustainable. Bigger deficits than that tend to produce faster loss at first, then rebound as your metabolism adjusts and hunger becomes difficult to manage.

To gain muscle, add about 250 calories. This small surplus is enough to support muscle protein synthesis during resistance training without adding unnecessary fat. Larger surpluses tend to add fat more efficiently than they add muscle, unless you are a complete beginner or are training under professional supervision.

The important thing is that these targets are starting points. Your real TDEE will be somewhere close to the estimate, but the exact number can be off by one or two hundred calories. The way to find your true maintenance level is to eat at your estimated TDEE for two to three weeks, weigh yourself at the same time each morning, and adjust based on what actually happens. If your weight stays flat, the estimate was accurate. If it drifts up or down, your real maintenance sits slightly below or above the estimate, and you can correct accordingly.

A word of caution. Do not try to eat well below your BMR for weeks at a time. Your BMR is roughly the energy cost of simply staying alive, and going under it for long stretches pushes your metabolism down, drains your energy, costs you muscle, and makes the weight come back the moment you eat normally again. If you feel you need to eat below your BMR to lose weight, that is a conversation to have with a doctor or dietitian, not something to work out on your own.

Common mistakes when using these numbers

A few pitfalls show up again and again when people start working with BMI, BMR, and TDEE.

Treating BMI as a diagnosis. It is a screening tool. A BMI of 27 does not mean you have a health problem. It means your weight-for-height ratio sits in a band that is worth a closer look, which is exactly what the calculator is designed to prompt.

Overestimating activity. Already covered above, but it is the single most common source of error. When in doubt, pick the lower multiplier. You can always adjust after seeing what happens on the scale.

Chasing the exact number. If your TDEE comes out as 2,347 calories and another calculator says 2,312, that difference is meaningless. Both are estimates with a margin of error of a couple hundred calories. Work in round numbers and adjust based on results, not on decimal places.

Eating back every calorie you burn. Fitness trackers tend to overestimate calories burned during exercise, sometimes by a wide margin. If you eat back everything your watch claims you burned, you will often end up eating more than your actual TDEE.

Ignoring the scale for too long. Body weight fluctuates day to day because of water, salt, hormones, and food in transit. That is why you weigh yourself at the same time every morning and look at the weekly trend rather than any single reading. It is also why a two or three week window is the shortest useful timeframe for judging whether a calorie target is working.

When to see a professional

Numbers from a calculator can be useful for personal planning, but they cannot replace the judgement of a trained clinician. There are specific situations where that judgement matters enough to seek it out.

If your BMI sits below 17 or above 35, a medical review is worth arranging regardless of how you feel. Both extremes carry risks that are better addressed early. If you have lost or gained a significant amount of weight without meaning to, that is another signal that a doctor should take a look. If you have an existing condition such as diabetes, thyroid disease, kidney disease, or an eating disorder, any calorie target should be set with your treating professional rather than from a website. And if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, your energy needs are completely different from the standard formulas here.

For everyone else, the numbers on this page are a reasonable starting point for personal planning. Bring them to a professional if you want a fuller picture, and treat them as one input among many rather than the final word on your health.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between BMI and body fat percentage?

BMI compares your total weight to your height. It does not know whether that weight is muscle, fat, bone, or water. Body fat percentage tries to measure how much of your weight is fat tissue. A muscular athlete can have a high BMI and a low body fat percentage at the same time. That is why BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnosis.

Why does the calculator use the Mifflin-St Jeor equation for BMR?

Because it is the equation most modern dietitians and clinical researchers use. Published in 1990, it predicts resting energy needs more accurately for most people than the older Harris-Benedict equations, which tend to overestimate calorie needs. It is the closest you can get to lab-level measurement without actually going to a lab.

What does BMI Prime mean?

BMI Prime expresses your BMI as a fraction of 25, which is the top of the healthy range. If your BMI Prime is 0.85, you are at 85 percent of the healthy ceiling. If it is 1.00, you are exactly at the boundary between healthy and overweight. If it is 1.20, you are twenty percent above the healthy ceiling. It is just another way of reading the same number.

Is it safe to eat fewer calories than my BMR?

For a short period and under medical supervision, yes. On your own, and for weeks at a time, no. Your BMR is roughly what your body burns just staying alive. Eating well below it for a long stretch pushes your metabolism down, drains your energy, costs you muscle, and makes it much harder to keep any weight off. Talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian before going that low.

Why does my result differ slightly from another calculator?

Small differences come from rounding, from the exact version of a formula being used, and from how activity multipliers are defined. Two calculators using the same WHO BMI thresholds but different BMR equations can easily differ by 50 to 150 calories a day. That is normal. What matters is the ballpark figure, not the last digit.

Does BMI work the same for everyone?

No. The World Health Organization suggests lower thresholds for people of South Asian and East Asian descent, because the same BMI tends to correspond to more body fat and higher metabolic risk in those populations. Athletes and older adults also need different interpretation. BMI is a rough starting point, not a one-size answer.

What should I do with my TDEE number?

Treat it as the number of calories you need per day to stay exactly where you are. If you want to lose weight slowly, subtract 250 to 500 from it. If you want to gain muscle slowly, add about 250. Anything more dramatic than that tends to backfire. And remember that TDEE is an estimate, so watch what actually happens on the scale over two or three weeks before adjusting.

Can children use this calculator?

The adult BMI categories do not apply to anyone under twenty. Children and teenagers need age and sex specific growth charts, which work differently. Our tool asks for an age of at least fifteen for general awareness, but for anyone under eighteen the results should be discussed with a paediatrician or a school nurse rather than acted on directly.
