What the ATAR actually measures

The Australian Tertiary Admission Rank is a percentile rank. Not a percentage, not a grade point average, not a sum of raw marks. When your admissions centre reports an ATAR of 85.00, it means you finished ahead of 85 per cent of the students in your state age cohort, and behind the other 15 per cent. The number runs from 0.00 up to 99.95, in steps of 0.05, and the top figure exists simply because no student can be ranked ahead of every other student including themselves.

The audience for this number is universities. Every undergraduate course in Australia that uses ATAR for admission publishes either a guaranteed ATAR, a lowest selection rank from previous years, or both. Meeting the published rank does not always guarantee a place, especially in courses that also interview or test, but falling below it almost always closes direct entry for that round. That is the practical meaning of the number: a threshold you either clear or do not clear, and a rough sense of how many courses sit within reach.

Because the ATAR is a rank, not a score, the exact same set of scaled marks can produce a slightly different ATAR from one year to the next. If the cohort is stronger in a given year, the same marks produce a slightly lower rank, and vice versa. This is one of the reasons a calculator can only ever give an estimate. The final rank comes from the actual cohort, and that is only known after every result is in.

How each state builds its aggregate

Every state and territory runs its own secondary certificate and its own tertiary admissions centre, and each centre applies its own rule for turning study scores into an aggregate. This calculator follows the standard published rule for each system.

State / SystemAuthorityAggregate rule
New South Wales, ACTUACBest 10 units, including at least 2 units of English. Each unit is worth up to 50 scaled marks, so the aggregate is out of 500.
VictoriaVTACPrimary 4 (compulsory English plus three highest scaled study scores) plus 10 per cent of a fifth and sixth subject. Each study score is out of 50.
QueenslandQTACBest 5 General subject scaled scores, each out of 100. Applied subjects and Certificate III VET qualifications can also count under specific rules.
Western AustraliaTISCBest 4 ATAR course scaled scores, each out of 100, producing a Tertiary Entrance Aggregate out of 400. Bonus points for specific subjects can be added before conversion.
South Australia, NTSATACA 90-credit SACE aggregate built from the best three full 20-credit subjects plus the best remaining 30 credits. Scaled scores run out of 20 per 10-credit subject.

The details vary, but the shape of the calculation is always the same. Take the required number of subjects under each rule, add the scaled scores, and use the total to look up a percentile rank. Some states count units rather than subjects. Some add increments for a fifth or sixth subject. Some apply subject bonus points before the conversion. The calculator applies the standard rule for the state you pick, and the article you are reading explains what that rule actually does.

Why scaling exists and what it does

Scaling is the part of the ATAR process that confuses students the most, so it is worth explaining plainly. Raw marks from different subjects are not directly comparable. An 85 in one subject and an 85 in another can reflect very different levels of achievement relative to the cohort that sat each one. Some subjects attract a strong cohort on average, others attract a mixed one, and the same raw mark represents a different position within each group.

Scaling is the process each admissions centre uses to place every subject's results on a common footing. It uses the performance of each subject's cohort across all of their other subjects to estimate how strong that cohort is overall, then adjusts scores so that comparable levels of achievement land at comparable scaled scores across subjects. After scaling, a scaled score of 35 in one subject means roughly the same thing as a scaled score of 35 in another, in terms of how it positions a student within the state cohort.

Two things follow from this. First, scaling cannot rescue a weak mark. A student who struggles in a subject will still receive a low scaled score, no matter how strong the cohort. Second, scaling rewards students who do well in whatever they have chosen, and especially in subjects that match their strengths, because a top mark in a moderately scaled subject typically beats a middle mark in a heavily scaled one. The choice that consistently produces the best ATAR is a set of subjects a student can genuinely do well in, with prerequisites for the course they want kept in place.

A common trap. Choosing a "high-scaling" subject you dislike, in the hope that scaling will carry you, rarely works. Scaling runs on cohort performance, not on the difficulty label students assign to a subject.

Your ATAR and your selection rank are two different things

The ATAR is issued by your state's tertiary admissions centre. The selection rank is what individual universities use once adjustment factors are applied. The two numbers are often the same, but they diverge whenever a university adds equity adjustments, subject bonuses, or program-specific schemes.

Educational Access Schemes and Special Entry Access Schemes exist to recognise circumstances that affected a student's study, such as a long illness, a regional or remote school, financial hardship, or being the first in a family to attend university. When a university approves an adjustment, it adds points to the base ATAR, and the resulting figure is used for that university's admission rounds. The adjustment does not appear on the ATAR certificate, and it does not transfer to another university that has not approved the same scheme.

Subject bonuses work in a similar way. A university may add a small number of points for a high result in a specific subject, such as Specialist Mathematics, a language, or a science relevant to the degree. Again, the bonus applies only at that institution. The calculator shows the base ATAR and a separate selection rank so the two figures stay distinct. If you are applying across multiple universities, use the base ATAR when comparing published cut-offs, and check each university's adjustment scheme separately.

A worked NSW example

Suppose a Year 12 student in Sydney sits 10 units of Board Developed HSC courses. After scaling, their results are English Advanced at 41 per unit, Mathematics Extension 1 at 44 per unit, Mathematics Extension 2 at 43 per unit, Physics at 40 per unit, and Chemistry at 39 per unit. Every course is worth two units, so all ten units are the ones they sat.

The NSW aggregate is the sum of the best 10 scaled units, which must include at least 2 units of English. In this case the student sat exactly 10 units and 2 of those are English, so all ten units count. The aggregate is 41 + 41 + 44 + 44 + 43 + 43 + 40 + 40 + 39 + 39 = 414 out of 500.

The published UAC percentile curve puts an aggregate of roughly 414 in the mid ninety per cent range, which corresponds to an estimated ATAR of about 93.45. That rank clears the standard cutoff for Bachelor of Engineering (Honours) at UNSW, the University of Sydney, and UTS, and it comfortably clears the guaranteed entry band for most science, commerce, and computer science programs at those universities.

Two things to notice from the example. First, because NSW counts units rather than subjects, the way subjects are paired matters. A 2-unit course contributes two entries, and a 1-unit course (extension studies in some cases) contributes one. Second, English is not dropped even if it is a student's weakest subject, because the rule requires at least 2 units of English to count. That is a real constraint on the calculation and one that surprises many students the first time they see it.

A worked Victorian example

Now suppose a Year 12 student in Melbourne takes five VCE Unit 3 and 4 sequences. Their scaled study scores are English 38, Mathematical Methods 37, Business Management 41, Legal Studies 39, and Economics 36. English is the compulsory subject, and it must sit in the Primary 4 alongside the student's three highest other scores.

The Primary 4 are English (38), Business Management (41), Legal Studies (39), and Mathematical Methods (37), for a sum of 155. Economics becomes the fifth subject, and VTAC adds 10 per cent of its scaled score as an increment. That is 3.6 points. The full VCE aggregate is 158.6.

The VTAC conversion curve places an aggregate of about 159 at an estimated ATAR of roughly 87.20. That rank clears direct entry into Bachelor of Commerce at Monash, Bachelor of Business at RMIT and Deakin, and Bachelor of Laws at Deakin. It sits just inside the guaranteed band for several Bachelor of Arts and Bachelor of Science degrees at the University of Melbourne.

The Victoria rule rewards students who take a fifth or sixth subject, because each additional subject beyond the Primary 4 contributes an increment. Even a modest scaled score in a fifth subject adds a few points to the aggregate, and those few points can push a student across a cutoff. The trade-off is real, though. Taking an extra subject means less time for the others, and a weak fifth subject that drags down the Primary 4 can cost more than the increment gains.

Choosing subjects without chasing scale

Year 10 subject selection is where most of the ATAR conversation actually happens, and it is worth separating the two questions students usually merge into one. The first question is which subjects will keep the doors open to the courses they want. The second is which subjects they will do best in. Both matter, and neither is well served by choosing subjects purely on the basis of how they scale.

Prerequisites come first. If a student wants to study engineering, most universities require Mathematics Methods or an equivalent, and several require a science. If they want to study medicine, most require Chemistry. If they want to study economics, many require Mathematics. These requirements do not disappear because a student's ATAR is high. Missing a prerequisite is a hard stop, no matter how strong the rank.

Once prerequisites are covered, the honest answer about which subjects produce the best ATAR is almost always the ones a student is genuinely engaged with. Students who enjoy a subject study it more, prepare for it better, and receive higher marks. Scaling adjusts marks up and down around the cohort average, but it does not turn a struggling performance into a strong one. Two students with the same ability and effort will generally do better in subjects they like.

There is one further point worth making. Different universities apply different subject bonus schemes. Some award points for a strong result in a specific language, some for advanced mathematics, and some for a science subject. A student who has not yet decided on a course can hedge by keeping at least one strong mathematics or science subject in their pattern, since that preserves options and often unlocks subject bonuses at the same time.

Where this calculator stops

This tool applies the standard aggregate rule for each state and estimates the ATAR using a percentile curve from recent year cohorts. It is a close estimate, and it is useful for understanding where you stand, for testing how a change in one subject score would affect the outcome, and for preparing questions for a careers advisor or an admissions officer. It is not the official figure.

The official ATAR is produced by your state's tertiary admissions centre, using the actual scaled scores from the actual cohort in the year you sat. Two students with the same subject scores in different years can receive slightly different ATARs because the cohort composition has shifted. The calculator cannot see that, and it does not try to.

What it does give you is a clear picture of the arithmetic. You can see exactly which subjects count under your state's rule, how a higher or lower score in any one of them moves the aggregate, and how the resulting rank sits against the common bands universities use for admission. That transparency is the point. The numbers on this page are yours to test with, so the decisions you make about subjects, effort, and applications are informed ones.

Frequently asked questions

Is 99.95 really the highest possible ATAR?

Yes. The ATAR is a percentile rank, not a percentage, and it is reported in steps of 0.05. The highest rank ever issued is 99.95, which places a student in the top 0.05 per cent of their state age cohort. An ATAR of 100.00 does not exist, because no student can be ranked ahead of every other student including themselves.

What is the difference between my ATAR and my study scores?

Study scores are the scaled marks you receive in each individual subject, usually out of 50 in NSW, Victoria, and South Australia, and out of 100 in Queensland and Western Australia. Your ATAR is a single percentile rank calculated from a specific combination of those scores using your state's rules. Study scores feed into the ATAR. They are not the same thing.

Does choosing harder subjects automatically raise my ATAR?

No. Scaling adjusts scores so that similar levels of achievement across subjects sit on a comparable footing, but it cannot rescue a weak mark. A strong result in a well-matched subject usually beats a struggling result in a subject that scales well. Choose subjects you can do well in, keep the prerequisites for the course you want, and let the scaling do its job.

How do I know which subjects count towards my ATAR?

Each state has its own rule. NSW counts 10 units including at least 2 units of English. Victoria uses a Primary 4 plus increments. Queensland uses the best 5 General subjects. Western Australia uses the best 4 ATAR courses. South Australia uses a 90-credit aggregate. The calculator applies the standard rule for whichever state you select.

Can I still get into university if my ATAR is below the course cutoff?

Yes, in many cases. Adjustment factors can lift your selection rank without changing your ATAR certificate. Many universities also run diploma-to-degree pathways, tertiary preparation programs, and single-subject study routes that lead into the same degree after a year or two. A below-cutoff ATAR closes one door, not every door.

Do bonus points change my ATAR certificate?

No. Bonus points and equity adjustments apply to your selection rank at specific universities. They never appear on your ATAR certificate and they do not change the rank issued by your state's tertiary admissions centre. That is why the calculator shows both the base ATAR and the selection rank separately.

Does my ATAR expire?

It depends on the institution. Most Australian universities accept an ATAR from the current year and sometimes the year before. Some accept it for longer, and some do not accept it at all once you have started tertiary study elsewhere. If you are applying after a gap year, check the admissions policy for each university you are targeting.

Why does this calculator give a slightly different number from my admissions centre?

Because every state's official ATAR calculation uses the full cohort's scaled scores, and those are only known after all results are finalised. This calculator uses published percentile curves and the standard aggregate rule to give a close estimate. For anything formal, your tertiary admissions centre's figure is the one that counts.

Which state rules does the calculator follow?

It follows the standard published rules for New South Wales (UAC, 10 units with 2 English), Victoria (VTAC, Primary 4 plus 10 per cent increments for a 5th and 6th subject), Queensland (QTAC, best 5 General subjects), Western Australia (TISC, best 4 ATAR courses for the Tertiary Entrance Aggregate), and South Australia and the Northern Territory (SATAC, 90-credit SACE aggregate).

Is my data sent anywhere?

No. The form and the calculation run entirely inside your browser. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of your state, subject scores, aggregate, and estimated rank is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. No account, no name, no school, no history.
