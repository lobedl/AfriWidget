The difference between an invoice and a receipt

Both documents record the same transaction, but they are used at different moments. An invoice asks for payment. A receipt confirms that payment has already happened. That is the entire difference, and it is why you cannot use one when the other is called for.

If you are a freelancer who has just finished a project and is waiting to be paid, you send an invoice. If you are a shop that has just taken money at the counter, you hand over a receipt. If you take a deposit and then bill the balance later, you would normally send a receipt for the deposit and an invoice for the remainder.

Some businesses issue both documents for the same job: an invoice when the work is done, and a receipt after the money arrives. That is not duplication. It is a clear paper trail, and it is exactly what an accountant or a tax office will ask for if there is ever a question about whether a payment was made.

What belongs on each document

A well-built invoice or receipt carries the same core information either way. Some fields are essential; others are nice to have. The essentials first:

  • The word "Invoice" or "Receipt", clearly at the top, so nobody has to guess which one they are looking at.
  • A unique document number, so you can reference it later. INV-2026-014 or REC-2026-083 is fine. Sequential numbers make bookkeeping easier.
  • The date the document was issued.
  • Your details as the seller — name or business name, contact information, and address.
  • The buyer's details — the name and contact information of the person or business being billed.
  • A description of what is being sold, with quantities and prices.
  • The total amount due or paid.
  • Any tax that applies, shown separately from the subtotal.

Then there are things that some countries and some clients require. A tax identification number is a common one. A payment deadline is another. Bank details or mobile money instructions are almost always expected on an invoice, since the client needs to know where to send the money. For services, some jurisdictions ask you to mention the date the service was delivered.

If you are unsure what your country requires, the safest approach is to look at a sample invoice from a business in your sector and check it against the guidance published by your local tax authority. This tool gives you the layout and the arithmetic; the specific legal requirements are set by the rules of your country.

Writing line items that make sense

The line items are the heart of the document. A confusing invoice invites a delayed payment. A clear one gets paid faster.

Each line should describe one product or service, with the quantity and the unit price clearly separated from the description. There is a strong temptation to combine several things into one line to save space. Resist it. A client who sees "Consulting and materials and delivery — $800" has no way to check the numbers. A client who sees three separate lines, each with its own price, can verify each one and is far more likely to approve the invoice without questions.

Descriptions should be specific enough that a stranger could understand the charge months later. "Design work" is too vague. "Logo design, three revisions, delivered 14 March" is clear and defensible. If you worked on multiple dates for the same project, you can either list the dates in one line or split into multiple lines by date. Both are common; pick whichever suits your client relationship.

Quantities can be whole numbers or decimals. An hour of consulting is 1 or 2. A part-day might be 0.5. A retail sale might be 4 units of the same item. Whatever you choose, keep the units consistent within a document: do not mix "hours" on one line with "days" on another without saying so in the description.

Discount, tax, and the order they apply in

The order in which discount and tax are applied changes the final total, so it matters. This tool applies the discount first, then calculates tax on the reduced amount. That is the standard order on invoices in most countries, and it is the one tax authorities expect to see.

Here is a worked example. Suppose your subtotal is 200. You apply a 10 percent discount. The discount amount is 20, so the taxable figure becomes 180. You then apply a 15 percent tax. Fifteen percent of 180 is 27. The grand total is 180 plus 27, which is 207.

If tax were calculated first, the numbers would be different, and depending on your jurisdiction that could be treated as an error on a formal invoice. When in doubt, the safe default is: subtotal, then discount, then tax on the discounted figure, then total.

Two things worth saying plainly. First, the tool does the arithmetic you enter; it does not decide which rate applies to your transaction. Tax rates, exemptions, thresholds, and rules about what is taxable all vary between countries and even between goods and services inside a single country. Second, if your discount is expressed as a fixed amount in your head, convert it to a percentage before entering it, or apply it as a negative line item if that matches how you keep your books.

Numbering your documents

A document number sounds trivial until you need to find an invoice from eight months ago and you are scrolling through a folder of files all called "invoice-final.pdf". A sensible numbering scheme saves hours of that kind of frustration.

The two most common approaches are simple sequential numbering (INV-001, INV-002, INV-003) and date-based numbering (INV-2026-03-014, where the middle parts indicate the year and month). Sequential numbering is easier to follow if you rarely issue more than a handful per month. Date-based numbering is easier to sort if you issue dozens. Either works; the key is to be consistent.

What matters more than the format is that you never reuse a number. If you make a mistake on an invoice, cancel it and issue a new one with a fresh number rather than editing the old one. Auditors and accountants look for gaps and duplicates, and a document that has been silently edited is harder to defend than a clean cancellation plus a replacement.

Payment terms and what to put in notes

The notes field is where you put the practical details that do not fit anywhere else: how to pay, by when, and anything else the client needs to know.

At a minimum, an invoice should state when payment is due. Common terms are "Due on receipt", "Net 7" (seven days), "Net 30" (thirty days), or a specific calendar date. If you are working with a new client, shorter terms are usually safer; once you have a working relationship, longer terms are often fine.

Beyond the due date, most freelancers and small businesses include bank or mobile money details directly in the notes field. That saves the client from having to email and ask. If you accept multiple payment methods, list them clearly with the account details for each. Some businesses add a late fee policy, a thank-you line, or a short note about how to raise questions about the invoice. All of those belong here.

For a receipt, the notes field is usually shorter. A brief "Thank you for your payment" and a reference to how it was paid (cash, card, bank transfer) is enough.

Saving to PDF from your browser

Once the totals look right, the button at the bottom of the results opens your browser's print dialog. From there, choose "Save as PDF" or "Microsoft Print to PDF" as the destination, and the browser will produce a clean A4 document.

This approach has three advantages over a dedicated PDF library. It works offline, it never fails because a third-party script failed to load, and it produces a document that carries only the information you entered. There is no AfriWidget logo, no footer URL, no watermark, and no hidden metadata on the final file. What you print is exactly what you would expect a professional invoice to look like.

The print dialog also lets you set the paper size, orientation, and margins, in case your printer or your country uses a different paper standard. The default is A4, which is the most common size for business documents outside North America. If you are printing for a client in the United States, you may want to switch to Letter.

Keeping records and how long to keep them

Most tax authorities expect businesses to keep copies of invoices, receipts, and supporting documents for several years after the end of the tax year they relate to. The exact number of years varies: some countries ask for five, some for seven, and a few ask for ten for certain kinds of transaction.

The practical approach is to keep everything for at least seven years unless you have a specific reason to keep it longer. Store your saved PDFs in one clearly named folder, ideally backed up to a second location, and give the files names that match the document numbers. An accountant or auditor who asks for a specific invoice will usually reference it by number, not by date.

There is no cloud storage built into this tool. You save the PDF to your own device and you decide where it goes from there. Some freelancers keep a folder on their computer and sync it to a cloud service; others store everything on an external drive. Either is fine as long as the folder is backed up and you can find it when you need it.

When to talk to an accountant or tax adviser

This tool handles the layout and the arithmetic. It does not decide whether you should charge tax, how much, to whom, or under what circumstances. Those are questions about your specific situation, and they depend on where you live, what you sell, who your customers are, and whether you are registered for VAT or an equivalent.

Four situations where it is worth talking to a qualified professional rather than guessing:

  • You have just started a business and are unsure whether you need to register for tax.
  • You are selling across borders and are unsure which country's tax rules apply.
  • Your revenue has crossed a threshold and you are unsure whether the registration requirements have changed.
  • A client is asking for something on the invoice that you are not sure you are allowed to provide, such as a specific tax number or a particular wording.

A short consultation is usually far cheaper than fixing a mistake later. Once you understand the rules that apply to you, this tool becomes the fast and clean way to produce the documents themselves.

One small honesty note. The wording "VAT" and "tax" appears throughout this page because that is what most of our visitors call the line. Whether the number you enter is called VAT, GST, sales tax, or something else in your country does not change the arithmetic, and it does not change the layout of the document. Just label it clearly when you fill in the notes field if your local authority expects a specific term.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use this for both invoices and receipts?

Yes. The toggle at the top of the form switches between INVOICE and RECEIPT. The layout and the arithmetic are the same for both; only the label at the top of the printed document changes. Use INVOICE when you are asking for payment, and RECEIPT when you are confirming that payment has already been received.

Does the tool save my invoice data anywhere?

No. Everything you type stays inside your browser tab. Nothing is sent to any server, nothing is stored in a database, and nothing persists after you close the page. If you want to keep a copy, print it or save it as a PDF before you navigate away.

Will there be a watermark or AfriWidget logo on the PDF?

No. The printed document contains only the information you entered: your business details, the client details, the line items, and the totals. There is no AfriWidget branding, no footer URL, no hidden watermark, and no attribution of any kind on the final PDF.

How is tax calculated when there is also a discount?

The discount is applied first to the subtotal, then tax is calculated on the reduced amount. So if your subtotal is 100, your discount is 10 percent, and your tax is 15 percent, the taxable figure is 90 and the tax works out to 13.50. This matches the order most tax authorities expect on a standard invoice.

Can I add my own logo or business colour?

Not in this version. The layout uses a clean black-and-white design that prints reliably on any printer and stays readable when scanned or archived. Adding custom logos is on the roadmap. If it matters for your work, send us a note and it will move up the list.

What if my currency is not in the dropdown?

Pick the closest major currency and then overwrite the currency label in the preview before printing. The maths does not depend on the currency symbol, so the totals remain correct regardless of which one you choose.

Is this tool a legal invoice or receipt in my country?

It produces the layout and the arithmetic, but every country and every tax authority has its own rules about what a compliant invoice or receipt must contain. Some places require a tax identification number, a specific wording, or a sequential numbering scheme. Check your local requirements before you rely on any invoice for a formal or legal purpose.

Can I send the invoice by email from this page?

The page does not send email directly. What it does is let you save the document as a PDF, and from there you can attach it to any email you like using your usual mail app. That keeps the final step in your hands and out of ours.
