What the UCAS Tariff actually is

The UCAS Tariff is a single number that summarises your post-16 qualifications in a form any UK university can read at a glance. Each grade on each qualification type carries a fixed number of points, and those points are added together to give you a total. A university that wants to admit someone with "roughly three good A-Levels" can express that as 128 Tariff points and be confident that an applicant with a mixed A-Level and BTEC portfolio is being measured on the same scale.

The Tariff was introduced because the UK has an unusually diverse set of post-16 qualifications. A-Levels, BTECs, T-Levels, Cambridge Technicals, the International Baccalaureate, Scottish Highers, and Welsh Baccalaureate all feed into higher education, and each one grades students in a different way. Without a common scale, comparing an A-Level applicant with a BTEC applicant would come down to guesswork.

What the Tariff is not is a ranking of quality. It is a conversion system. A BTEC Extended Diploma at DMM carries the same 128 points as A-Level grades ABB, but universities understand that the two qualifications teach very different skills. Many programmes welcome both, and some specialise in one or the other. The number is a starting point, not the whole picture.

How totals are added up

Adding up UCAS points is straightforward in principle. You take each qualification you have completed, look up the grade in the appropriate table, and add the point value to your running total. Nine grades across four subjects means nine lookups and one addition. There is no weighting, no averaging, and no cap at the top end (though universities sometimes specify a maximum number of qualifications they will count).

There is one rule worth knowing about, though. If you have both an AS-Level and a full A-Level in the same subject, only the higher qualification counts. A student who took AS History and then continued to A-Level History does not add the two totals together; the A-Level points are used and the AS points are dropped. This is called the non-duplication rule and it exists to prevent someone from padding their score by counting the same subject twice.

Most universities also publish their own version of a "maximum qualifications" rule in the offer. A typical offer might read "128 points from a minimum of three A-Levels or equivalent qualifications, excluding General Studies." That means the university will count no more than three A-Levels, and it will not count General Studies even if you took it. Read the offer carefully, because the small print can change how many points you actually have.

What about GCSEs? GCSEs are Level 2 qualifications and carry no UCAS Tariff points at all. That said, most universities still require a pass at grade 4 or above in GCSE English Language and Mathematics as a condition of admission. Passing those two is compulsory; the Tariff points come from your Level 3 qualifications on top.

Three worked examples

Example one: a standard three A-Level candidate

A student completes three A-Levels with grades A in Mathematics, B in Physics, and B in Economics. The points are 48 + 40 + 40 = 128. That is one of the most common profiles in the UK, and 128 points sits at the entry bar for a lot of selective Russell Group and high-ranking university courses in business, science, and engineering.

Example two: a combined portfolio with an EPQ

A student takes two A-Levels (A in Biology, A in Chemistry), one BTEC National Extended Certificate in Applied Science at Distinction, and an EPQ at grade A. The points work out as 48 + 48 + 48 + 24 = 168. That matches the top of the tariff for three A* grades, which is why the EPQ is such a popular addition for students aiming at the most competitive courses.

Example three: a BTEC National Extended Diploma candidate

A student completes the BTEC Level 3 National Extended Diploma in Information Technology with a DDM grade. That is 128 points, which is exactly the entry bar for BSc Computer Science and software engineering programmes at a number of selective UK universities. A BTEC-only applicant who hits DDM is competitive with A-Level applicants who hit ABB.

Which universities actually use points

Not every UK university expresses its offers in Tariff points, and it is worth being clear about which ones do and which do not.

Oxford, Cambridge, Imperial College London, the London School of Economics, and a handful of other highly selective institutions almost always make offers in specific letter grades — AAA with an A in a specific subject, A*AA, and so on. Those offers do not translate directly to a points target. If you are aiming at one of those universities, the UCAS Tariff is not the number you should be looking at; the specific A-Level or IB grade requirements are.

Most other UK universities do use Tariff points, especially for courses that draw applicants from a mix of academic and vocational backgrounds. Russell Group universities such as Manchester, Leeds, Sheffield, Nottingham, and Birmingham frequently publish entry requirements in points for some of their programmes, particularly in the arts and social sciences. Mid-tier comprehensive universities almost always do.

The place where Tariff points matter most is UCAS Clearing. When a course has unfilled places and universities are trying to fill them quickly, offers often come down to a simple points threshold. A student with 120 points can clear onto a course with a published 128-point offer if the department is willing to be flexible, and Tariff gives both sides a common language for that negotiation.

Common mistakes with UCAS points

A few errors show up again and again when students try to work out their own tariff total.

Counting AS-Levels on top of the corresponding A-Level. If you have both, only the higher one counts. Adding them together inflates the total and leads to mismatched expectations.

Including GCSEs. GCSEs are not part of the Tariff. Some students add them in by mistake because they see points on their GCSE certificates, but those are not Tariff points.

Forgetting about the "number of qualifications" limit. An offer that says "128 points from three A-Levels or equivalent" is not the same as "128 points from any combination of qualifications." If you have four A-Levels and the offer only counts three, the fourth is not used.

Ignoring the subject requirement. A points total above the threshold does not help if you are missing a required subject. A Chemistry degree that requires A-Level Chemistry will not admit you on 144 points without Chemistry, even if 144 is well above the published entry bar.

Assuming Clearing always follows the published number. Clearing is flexible. Universities will sometimes go below their published tariff for a course that has not filled, and sometimes they will refuse a candidate who exactly matches it if demand is strong. The Tariff is a benchmark, not a guarantee in either direction.

Where this calculator stops

This tool applies the official UCAS Tariff tables and produces a total in points. That covers what the majority of UK universities use as a first-pass filter for applicants from a mix of qualification routes.

It does not know the specific subject requirements, grade requirements, or admission policies of the course you are applying to. A strong total will not help if you are missing a required subject or if the university has moved to grade-based offers.

It does not account for the "number of qualifications" rule that many universities apply. An offer that says "128 points from three A-Levels" and one that says "128 points" are different, and the calculator cannot tell which applies to your situation.

What it does give you is a clean starting figure you can take to the course pages on the UCAS website. Use it to understand roughly where you stand, to compare routes, and to prioritise your final-year study. The specific course entry profile is the number that actually decides.

Frequently asked questions

What is the UCAS Tariff?

The UCAS Tariff is a national system that converts post-16 qualifications into a single point score. Each grade on each qualification type carries a fixed number of points, and universities can ask for a total number of points as their entry requirement. It lets admissions teams compare an A-Level applicant with a BTEC applicant and an IB applicant on the same scale.

Do all UK universities use UCAS Tariff points?

No. Some of the most selective universities, including Oxford, Cambridge, Imperial, and LSE, make offers in specific letter grades rather than points. Their standard offers read AAA or A*AA with an A in a specific subject, and they do not express requirements in points at all. The UCAS Tariff system is used by most other UK universities, especially those that admit students from a mix of academic and vocational routes.

How many UCAS points is an A* at A-Level worth?

An A* at A-Level is worth 56 UCAS points. An A is worth 48, a B is worth 40, a C is 32, a D is 24, and an E is 16. Someone with three A* grades has 168 points, which is the maximum from three A-Levels. Adding a fourth A* takes the total to 224.

Can I combine A-Levels and BTECs for UCAS points?

Yes. The UCAS Tariff was built for exactly this. A student with a BTEC Extended Diploma at DMM earns 112 points, and one A-Level at grade B adds 40 more, for a total of 152 points. Many UK universities are happy to consider mixed portfolios as long as the subject content meets the programme requirements.

Does an EPQ count towards my UCAS points total?

Yes. The Extended Project Qualification carries up to 28 points for an A* grade, 24 for an A, 20 for a B, and so on. Many universities also make alternative offers to applicants holding a strong EPQ grade, sometimes reducing the main offer by one grade.

What is a good UCAS points score?

It depends entirely on the course and university. Highly selective Russell Group courses often ask for 128 to 168 points. Mid-tier comprehensive universities typically ask for 96 to 120. Foundation year entry points sit lower, often around 48 to 80. Look at the specific course entry profile on the UCAS website for the programmes you are interested in.

Can I include my GCSEs in the UCAS points total?

No. The UCAS Tariff only covers Level 3 qualifications such as A-Levels, BTECs, T-Levels, IB, and Scottish Highers. GCSEs are Level 2 and do not carry tariff points. That said, most UK universities still require a pass in GCSE English Language and Mathematics at grade 4 or above on top of your Level 3 points.

Do international A-Levels carry the same points as UK A-Levels?

Yes. Cambridge International (CAIE) and Pearson Edexcel International A-Levels carry the same UCAS Tariff points as the domestic UK versions: A* is 56, A is 48, B is 40, C is 32, D is 24, and E is 16. Universities in the UK treat them as equivalent for admissions purposes.
