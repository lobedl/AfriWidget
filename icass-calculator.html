What ICASS actually is

ICASS stands for Internal Continuous Assessment. It is the mark that a South African TVET college lecturer builds up across a semester or trimester from class tests, assignments, practical tasks, and an internal preparatory examination. Every student enrolled in a NATED Report 191 programme from N1 through N6, and every student in the NC(V) programme from Level 2 through Level 4, has an ICASS mark for each subject they take. The mark is calculated by the college, verified by the college's academic board, and submitted to the Department of Higher Education and Training through the TVET Management Information System.

The idea behind ICASS is simple and it is the same idea behind continuous assessment in most education systems. Instead of putting the entire weight of a subject on a single three-hour paper at the end of the term, the DHET spreads the assessment across the term. A student who has a bad day in the trial exam can still pass the subject, and a student who has a bad term in assignments can still rescue the mark in the exam. Neither route is closed off, and the final result reflects more of what the student has actually done.

What makes ICASS a genuine planning tool rather than just a record is its predictability. The weighting structure for each subject is published in advance, the tasks are known, and the arithmetic is fixed. A student who understands their own weighting can work out exactly where they stand at any point in the term, and exactly what they need from the remaining tasks. That is what this calculator is built to do.

How weighted ICASS is calculated

A weighted average is not the same as a simple average. If a test counts ten per cent of the final ICASS mark and an assignment counts twenty-five per cent, the assignment has two and a half times the effect of the test on the final figure. The calculator applies the weights you enter, task by task, and adds the contributions together.

On the standard five-task portfolio used by most NATED N4 to N6 subjects and NC(V) semester subjects, the weighting is twenty per cent for the two class tests, fifty per cent for the two assignments, and thirty per cent for the trial exam. Written as a formula, the calculation looks like this.

ICASS = (Test1 × 0.10) + (Assign1 × 0.25) + (Test2 × 0.10) + (Assign2 × 0.25) + (TrialExam × 0.30)

If a student scores 68 on Test 1, 80 on Assignment 1, 72 on Test 2, 78 on Assignment 2, and 65 on the trial exam, each contribution is calculated separately. Test 1 contributes 6.80, Assignment 1 contributes 20.00, Test 2 contributes 7.20, Assignment 2 contributes 19.50, and the trial exam contributes 19.50. The total is 73.00, which sits comfortably above the DHET exam entry threshold of 40 and close to the distinction band at 75.

Trimester engineering programmes at N1 to N3 use a simpler three-task structure, often weighted thirty per cent test, twenty per cent assignment, and fifty per cent internal exam. The calculator has a preset for this format, and it uses the same arithmetic to combine the three scores. If your college publishes a slightly different split, the Custom mode lets you enter any combination as long as the weights total one hundred.

NATED versus NC(V) assessment rules

The two qualifications that live side by side in the TVET system have separate assessment frameworks, and mixing them up is a common source of confusion. The NATED Report 191 route is the traditional engineering and business pathway, running on fast trimesters of roughly ten to twelve weeks or semesters of eighteen to twenty weeks. The National Certificate (Vocational) route runs across a full academic year at Levels 2, 3, and 4, and it includes a substantial practical component that is assessed separately from the theory tasks.

In NATED, the two class tests and two assignments are typically spread evenly across the term, with the trial exam at the end. The trial exam carries thirty per cent of the ICASS total on the standard five-task model, which makes it the highest-leverage single task in the portfolio. In NC(V), the internal assessment portfolio is referred to as the ICASS component and it works alongside the Integrated Summative Assessment Task, or ISAT, which is a hands-on project that tests practical workplace readiness. The NC(V) pass mark for vocational subjects is often fifty per cent, higher than the forty per cent sub-minimum that applies to NATED.

Both routes report to the National Qualifications Framework and both lead to recognised certificates and diplomas. The difference in assessment structure reflects the difference in what each programme is designed to prove. NATED focuses on technical theory that is verified by an external national exam paper. NC(V) focuses on demonstrated practical competence, so more of the mark is built up internally from work the student actually does.

The 40 percent sub-minimum explained

The DHET sets a minimum ICASS mark that a student must reach before they can be admitted to the national examination. For NATED Report 191 subjects, that minimum is forty per cent. A student whose cumulative ICASS mark is below forty per cent is marked DNQ, which stands for Did Not Qualify, and is barred from writing the national paper for that subject. The DNQ status is recorded against the subject and the student must re-enrol in the following semester or trimester to try again.

The threshold exists for a reason. A national exam paper is set and marked externally, and the DHET uses the exam results as the primary evidence for the credibility of a qualification. If a student has not engaged with the coursework through the term, the exam paper alone will not be a reliable measure of what they learned. The forty per cent sub-minimum is a signal that the student has met the minimum engagement threshold, not a statement about their ability.

In NC(V) practical and vocational subjects, the sub-minimum is usually fifty per cent, and the pass mark for the subject as a whole is also fifty per cent. Some fundamental subjects such as English, Mathematical Literacy, and Life Orientation use forty per cent. The exact figure for a specific subject should always be confirmed against the subject handbook, because a small number of programmes apply their own rule.

Watch the timing. The sub-minimum is calculated at the close of the ICASS capture window, which is a few weeks before the national exam begins. If your cumulative mark is close to forty per cent when the window opens, the last assignment or class test can make the difference. Ask your lecturer for the exact date the marks are locked.

How ICASS combines with the national exam

In most NATED Report 191 subjects, the final combined result is forty per cent ICASS and sixty per cent national exam paper. This split applies across engineering studies and business studies. It means that the ICASS mark cannot be ignored, but it also means that a strong performance in the national exam can still lift a moderate ICASS score, and a weak national exam can still pull down a strong one.

Working backwards, the mark a student needs in the national exam to reach a given overall percentage is a simple algebraic rearrangement. If the target is seventy-five per cent and the ICASS is seventy, the required exam mark is the value that satisfies the equation 0.40 × 70 + 0.60 × Exam = 75, which comes to 78.3 per cent. If the ICASS is already eighty, the required exam mark drops to 71.7. Every extra point of ICASS makes the exam target a little easier, which is why the sub-minimum is worth taking seriously even for a student who is confident about the exam itself.

For NC(V) subjects the split is different. The internal portfolio and the ISAT together account for fifty per cent of the final mark in most vocational subjects, and the national exam accounts for the other fifty. This is a much heavier internal weighting than NATED and it reflects the practical orientation of the qualification. Always check the split for your specific subject before you rely on a general figure.

A worked recovery scenario

Consider a student enrolled in NATED N3 Electrical Trade Theory. Call them Thabo. The subject uses the standard five-task portfolio: Test 1 at ten per cent, Assignment 1 at twenty-five per cent, Test 2 at ten per cent, Assignment 2 at twenty-five per cent, and the trial exam at thirty per cent.

Thabo's first term did not go well. He scored thirty-eight on Test 1 and fifty-two on Assignment 1. Converting those to contributions: Test 1 adds 3.80 and Assignment 1 adds 13.00, for a running total of 16.80 out of the thirty-five points that were available from those two tasks. The remaining sixty-five points come from Test 2, Assignment 2, and the trial exam. He needs at least 23.20 more points to reach forty per cent and secure exam entry.

Two paths are open. The first is to aim high on the remaining tasks and treat the trial exam as the primary opportunity. If Thabo scores seventy on Assignment 2, that adds 17.50 points and brings him to 34.30. He then needs only 5.70 more from Test 2 and the trial exam combined. A sixty-five on Test 2 adds 6.50 and lifts him to 40.80, which clears the sub-minimum before the trial exam has even been written. Anything he scores in the trial exam after that is upside.

The second path is to spread the effort differently. If he does poorly on Assignment 2, the trial exam becomes the last chance, and thirty per cent is a lot of weight to carry. Scoring sixty on the trial exam adds eighteen points, which brings the total to roughly 41 to 42 per cent if Test 2 and Assignment 2 come in around fifty-five each. It is achievable, but the margin is thin.

The point of the calculation is not that one path is right. It is that knowing where the points are lets a student decide which tasks deserve the most preparation time. A task worth twenty-five per cent is worth twice the effort of a task worth ten per cent, other things being equal, and the final week of term is where that difference shows up.

Umalusi moderation and mark consistency

Umalusi is the statutory quality assurance body for general and further education and training in South Africa. It moderates both the national exam papers and the internal assessment that feeds into them. When Umalusi reviews the marks from a group of colleges, it looks at the relationship between the internal ICASS marks and the external exam results. If a college's internal marks are much higher than what the national exam results would predict, or much lower, the internal marks may be adjusted to bring them into line with national distributions.

This process is called statistical moderation, and it is used in many countries to keep qualifications comparable across institutions. It does not punish a student for a college's marking habits, and it does not reward a student for a lenient lecturer. What it does is ensure that a seventy per cent ICASS at one college means roughly the same thing as a seventy per cent ICASS at another, which is the whole point of having a national qualification.

For students, the practical implication is to keep your own record of every task and every mark. If a mark is entered incorrectly on the college's system, or if a task is missed from the total, the record you keep is the fastest way to spot the discrepancy. The dispute window is short, usually a few days after preliminary marks are published, and having the paperwork ready is what makes a correction possible.

TVETMIS deadlines and admission permits

The final ICASS marks are submitted to the DHET through the TVET Management Information System, which is the national database that connects colleges, provincial education departments, and Umalusi. The submission happens on a fixed calendar published by the DHET at the start of each academic year, and the deadline is not negotiable. Once the capture window closes, the marks are locked and no college principal or lecturer can alter them.

A few weeks before the national exam timetable begins, colleges publish preliminary ICASS marks on campus notice boards and student portals. Students have a statutory window, typically forty-eight to seventy-two hours, to check the published marks against their own marked scripts and to file a written discrepancy appeal if something is wrong. A transposed digit in a mark entry, such as forty-eight instead of eighty-four, is the most common type of error, and it is the easiest to fix if you catch it during the window.

Once the marks are locked and the DHET has processed the data, the national examination admission permit is generated for every candidate whose ICASS mark is at or above the sub-minimum. The permit lists the examination centre, the seat allocation, and the timetable. Candidates whose marks fell below the sub-minimum are marked DNQ and are not issued a permit. If you have not received a permit by the week before the exam, contact your campus examination officer immediately rather than waiting.

Common mistakes when tracking ICASS

Seven mistakes show up repeatedly when students try to work out their own ICASS marks, and each one is easy to avoid once you know it is there.

Simple averaging instead of weighted. A student who averages four tasks equally when two of them carry ten per cent and two carry twenty-five per cent will get the wrong number. The weighting matters as much as the scores.

Forgetting the trial exam. On the standard model the trial exam carries thirty per cent on its own, which is more than any other single task. Students who focus only on class tests and assignments and treat the trial as a formality often lose the largest single block of points.

Entering weights that do not total one hundred. Some syllabuses include small components such as attendance or logbook marks that add up to five per cent, and it is easy to leave them out of a hand calculation. The calculator shows the running total so you can see immediately if something has been missed.

Using last year's weighting. Assessment structures are revised from time to time, sometimes between trimesters. Always confirm against your current subject handbook rather than assuming the split is the same as last year.

Assuming the DHET split is always forty-sixty. It is for most NATED subjects, but NC(V) subjects and a small number of specialised programmes use a different split. Checking the split only takes a moment, and it changes the exam target calculations.

Missing the dispute window. The forty-eight to seventy-two hour window after preliminary marks are published is the only chance to correct a marking or entry error. Missing it means living with whatever number appears on the DHET record, even if it is wrong.

Waiting until the last task to check. A student who discovers in week ten that they are four points below the sub-minimum has limited options. A student who checks after every task has time to redirect effort, request a supplementary practical, or speak to their lecturer about the portfolio.

Frequently asked questions

What is ICASS and who uses it?

ICASS stands for Internal Continuous Assessment. It is the mark that a South African TVET college lecturer builds up over a semester or trimester from class tests, assignments, practical tasks, and a trial examination. Every student enrolled in a NATED Report 191 programme (N1 to N6) or an NC(V) programme has an ICASS mark for each subject they take. It is separate from the national examination paper, which is set and marked externally by the DHET and moderated by Umalusi.

What is the minimum ICASS mark I need to write the national exam?

For NATED Report 191 subjects, DHET rules require a minimum ICASS mark of 40 percent before you are admitted to the national examination. Below 40 percent your status is DNQ, which stands for Did Not Qualify, and you are barred from the exam hall. In NC(V) practical and vocational subjects the sub-minimum is higher, usually 50 percent. Always confirm the exact threshold in your subject handbook, because a few programmes apply their own rule.

How much does ICASS count toward my final grade?

In most NATED Report 191 subjects, the final combined result is 40 percent ICASS and 60 percent national examination. This split applies to engineering studies and business studies alike. For NC(V) subjects the weighting differs: the internal portfolio carries a larger share because the programme is designed around demonstrated practical competence. Always check your own subject's weighting, because it determines how much the exam paper can move the final figure.

What is the difference between NATED and NC(V)?

NATED Report 191 programmes are offered at levels N1 through N6 and run on fast trimesters or semesters. They are the traditional route for engineering and business studies at TVET colleges. NC(V), the National Certificate (Vocational), is a three-year programme at levels 2, 3, and 4 that mixes theory with a heavy practical component and adds an integrated practical assessment called the ISAT. The two qualifications have separate assessment policies, separate pass marks, and separate reporting on the NQF.

Why does the calculator show my weight total separately?

Because weighted assessment only works when the weights add up to exactly 100 percent. If your lecturer uses a custom split, for example 40 percent assignments, 40 percent tests, and 20 percent attendance, you need to enter those exact weights rather than relying on a standard preset. The calculator shows the total so you can see straight away if a field has been missed or a weight is wrong, and it warns you before it does the final calculation.

Can I use the calculator if I am on a trimester schedule?

Yes. Trimester NATED programmes usually run a simpler three-task portfolio: one class test, one assignment, and one internal examination. The calculator has a dedicated Trimester mode that assumes a 30 / 20 / 50 split, which is the most common arrangement for engineering studies at N1 to N3. If your college uses a slightly different split, switch to the Custom mode and enter the exact weights from your handbook.

What happens if I miss a test due to illness?

DHET assessment policy requires that you submit a valid medical certificate from a registered practitioner within five working days of returning to campus. Your lecturer or department head can then arrange a sanctioned deferred test or an approved substitute average. Without a certificate, the missing task is usually recorded as zero and pulled into the weighted total, which can put exam entry at risk.

Does my ICASS score affect my NSFAS funding?

Yes. NSFAS requires TVET students to demonstrate consistent academic progression, and part of that progression is a passing status in a set proportion of your enrolled subjects. Because an ICASS mark below 40 percent results in a DNQ and an automatic subject failure, a weak continuous assessment record can endanger the next disbursement. Keeping the ICASS average above the DHET sub-minimum is one of the simplest ways to protect your funding.

How does Umalusi moderation affect my final mark?

Umalusi is the quality assurance body that moderates both the national exam papers and the internal assessment that feeds into them. If the internal marks from a college sit far outside the range the national exam results would predict, Umalusi can apply statistical moderation that adjusts the internal marks up or down to restore consistency across campuses. This is not a penalty, it is a national consistency check, and it is one reason the DHET insists that portfolios be properly kept and signed.

Does this tool store my marks?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of your weights, your scores, and the computed ICASS average is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. No name, no student number, no college identifier, and no history.
