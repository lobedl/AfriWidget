What this calculator does

This tool takes the WASSCE percentage marks you enter for each subject, converts them into the official WAEC 9-point letter grades, and audits the total against the statutory five-credit baseline that Nigerian universities apply to every degree applicant. It also estimates the Post-UTME O'Level screening points out of 20 that institutions such as UNILAG, LASU, UI, OAU, and UNIBEN calculate from the best five prerequisite subjects.

The arithmetic itself is short. What makes the tool worth using is the audit layer that sits on top of the grade conversion. The five-credit rule has three separate conditions, and a candidate who reaches five credits but misses English Language or Mathematics at credit level is still ineligible for direct degree admission at most universities. The tool checks each condition and reports the outcome, so the eligibility picture is clear before the applications are submitted.

Every calculation runs inside your browser. No mark, no subject name, and no personal detail is uploaded. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the number of subjects entered, the credit count, the distinction count, the pass count, the average mark, the English and Mathematics credit status, and the eligibility band is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

The WAEC 9-point grading scale

WAEC grades WASSCE subjects on nine levels that run from A1 at the top down to F9 at the bottom. The scale is inverted relative to the way most people think about percentages in some other systems, because the highest percentage band corresponds to the highest letter grade. The table below shows the full mapping.

The percentage range is applied to the composite mark that WAEC records for each subject. The composite combines the Continuous Assessment Score that the candidate's school submits during the two-year senior secondary programme and the raw marks from the objective, theory, and practical papers that WAEC marks and moderates. The composite is the figure that determines the final grade on the certificate, and it is the figure the tool uses to derive the letter grade from the percentage you enter.

Credit passes and distinctions

The nine grades split into four functional bands. Grades A1, B2, and B3 are the distinctions, and they cover the top thirty-five percentage points of the scale. Grades C4, C5, and C6 are the credit passes, and they cover the next fifteen percentage points. Grades D7 and E8 are ordinary passes, and grade F9 is a fail. The credit band is the one that matters for admission, because a credit pass in a subject is the level at which that subject can be used as a prerequisite for a degree programme.

The distinction band matters for the ranking at the top end. Most competitive Nigerian universities apply the Post-UTME O'Level screening points on top of the pass or fail test, and a distinction in a core prerequisite subject contributes more screening points than a credit does. On a competitive programme such as Medicine and Surgery, Pharmacy, Law, or Computer Engineering, the difference between a B2 and a C6 in one subject can be the difference between an offer and a rejection when the merit list is compiled.

Grade C6 is a full credit pass. It is not a marginal pass, and it is not treated differently from C4 or C5 for the purposes of the five-credit baseline. A candidate with five credits at C6 across the required subjects is fully eligible for direct degree admission on the O'Level requirement, and the screening points they receive from those credits are the standard C6 points. The distinction between C4 and C6 only matters for the ranking figure, not for eligibility.

The five-credit university baseline

The admission rules published by the Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board and applied by every Nigerian university set three conditions that must be met at the same time for direct degree entry on the O'Level record.

The first condition is five credit passes at Grade C6 or better. The five credits must be in subjects that are relevant to the programme being applied for, and the specific subject combination that counts as relevant depends on the programme and on the university. For Medicine and Surgery the five are usually English Language, Mathematics, Biology, Chemistry, and Physics. For Accounting they are usually English Language, Mathematics, Economics, Financial Accounting or Commerce, and one other approved subject. For Law they are usually English Language, Literature in English, Government or History, and two other approved subjects including Mathematics at some institutions.

The second condition is a credit pass in English Language. Every degree programme at every Nigerian university requires a minimum of C6 in English Language, and no programme waives this requirement. A candidate with five credits but an ordinary pass in English Language is ineligible for direct degree admission and must sit the subject again or use a bridging pathway.

The third condition is a credit pass in Mathematics for most programmes. Mathematics at C6 or better is compulsory for the sciences, engineering, technology, medicine, pharmacy, agriculture, social sciences, administration, and management sciences. A small number of pure humanities programmes at some universities waive the Mathematics credit requirement and accept a D7 or E8 in Mathematics provided five other credits including English Language are present. The specific waiver rule is published in the admissions brochure for the year, and the school's admissions office is the definitive source.

Post-UTME O'Level screening points

Universities that use O'Level screening points in their admission formula convert each of the five best prerequisite subjects into a numerical value and sum the values into a figure out of 20. The standard conversion assigns 4.0 points to A1, 3.6 to B2, 3.2 to B3, 2.8 to C4, 2.4 to C5, and 2.0 to C6. The five best prerequisite subjects for the programme contribute their points to the total, and any additional credits beyond the five are not counted in the screening figure, though they remain on the certificate and can help with programme-specific requirements.

Screening points (out of 20) = Sum of the points for the five best prerequisite subjects
Points map: A1 = 4.0, B2 = 3.6, B3 = 3.2, C4 = 2.8, C5 = 2.4, C6 = 2.0

The screening figure is then combined with the UTME score and the Post-UTME CBT mark in the final composite. The exact weightings differ between institutions, and some universities use the twenty-point scale directly while others scale it to fifty or one hundred points. The tool reports the twenty-point figure because it is the common foundation that every institution builds from, and the specific weightings that the receiving institution applies to it are published in the admissions brochure.

A worked example

Take a candidate applying to study Pharmacy at a university that uses the standard Post-UTME O'Level screening formula. The candidate's marks across nine subjects are shown below. The grade and the screening points are derived from each mark.

SubjectMarkGradeScreening points
English Language72B23.6
General Mathematics78A14.0
Biology80A14.0
Chemistry76A14.0
Physics71B23.6
Civic Education85A14.0
Economics68B33.2
Agricultural Science73B23.6
Further Mathematics64C42.8

The candidate has nine credits, which is well above the five-credit baseline. English Language is at B2, which is a credit pass. General Mathematics is at A1, which is a credit pass. The two conditions that are sometimes missed are both satisfied, and the candidate is eligible for direct degree admission on the O'Level record.

The five prerequisite subjects for Pharmacy are usually English Language, Mathematics, Biology, Chemistry, and Physics. The screening points from those five are 3.6 plus 4.0 plus 4.0 plus 4.0 plus 3.6, which equals 19.2 out of 20. That is a strong profile for the programme, and the remaining five points on the total are the additional credits beyond the five required, which do not enter the screening figure at this university but do help to show breadth on the application.

Now suppose the same candidate had earned a C6 in Physics instead of a B2. The screening points would drop by 1.6 to 17.6, and the margin against the typical Pharmacy cut-off would narrow. The difference between a B2 and a C6 in a single core subject is a full 1.6 points on the screening figure, which is often the difference between an offer and a rejection at the top end of the merit list. That arithmetic is why the tool reports the screening figure separately from the eligibility verdict: a candidate can be fully eligible and still be well below the competitive margin for the specific programme.

Common mistakes when reading WASSCE results

A handful of recurring errors make a candidate's own estimate of their standing diverge from what the admission office sees on the application, and some of those errors have real consequences for the applications that follow.

Treating D7 as a credit pass. D7 is an ordinary pass, not a credit. A candidate who reaches a D7 in a subject and counts it as one of the five credits will find that the application is treated differently by the university admissions office. Only A1 through C6 count as credits, and the specific subject in which the D7 was earned determines whether it can substitute for a credit in a related subject at some institutions.

Assuming that five credits alone are enough. The five-credit baseline has a subject combination requirement on top of the credit count. A candidate with five credits in subjects that do not match the programme prerequisites will be rejected regardless of the total, because the specific programme has its own subject list. The combination table in the JAMB brochure and in the university prospectus is the definitive source for the specific programme.

Missing the English Language or Mathematics requirement. A credit pass in English Language is required for every degree programme in Nigeria, and a credit pass in Mathematics is required for most. A candidate with eight credits across other subjects but an ordinary pass in English or Mathematics is ineligible for direct degree admission until the missing subject is cleared.

Confusing the average mark with the grades that matter. The average percentage across all subjects is a useful summary, but it is not the figure the admission office uses. The screening figure and the eligibility verdict both depend on the specific grades in the specific prerequisite subjects, and a candidate whose average is high but whose core subject grades are weak can find that the screening figure is below the typical cut-off.

Assuming a strong screening figure guarantees admission. The O'Level screening points are one component of a composite that also includes the UTME score and the Post-UTME CBT mark. A candidate with strong O'Level points but a weak UTME score can still be below the cut-off for a competitive programme. The three components move the final figure together, and the tool reports only the O'Level portion because it is the component this page is built to calculate.

Overlooking the sitting count in competitive faculties. A small number of programmes, especially Medicine and Surgery at the most selective universities, give priority in the ranking to candidates who obtained the five required credits in a single sitting. A candidate who combined two sittings can still be admitted, but the ranking may be adjusted under the institution's own rules. The prospectus for the specific programme states whether a sitting preference applies.

Single sitting, two sittings, and WAEC GCE

The Nigerian admission system recognises three main configurations for the examination record. A single sitting means all five required credits were obtained in one WAEC or NECO diet, and it is the configuration that most competitive programmes prefer. Two sittings means the five credits were combined across two diets, and it is accepted by the great majority of programmes provided the sittings are within the admissible window and the specific subject credits are present across the combined record. Some highly selective programmes apply a small penalty or a priority adjustment to two-sitting candidates in the ranking.

WAEC GCE, formally WASSCE for Private Candidates, is offered in a first series in January and February and a second series in August and September. The certificate is identical in academic value to the May/June certificate for the purposes of admission, and candidates who use GCE to complete or improve their five credits are treated exactly the same way as candidates who sat the same subjects in the May/June school diet. The only difference is the sitting date on the record.

Combinations of WAEC and NECO are accepted, and both bodies are recognised by JAMB and by every Nigerian university. The candidate combines the two records into a single five-credit set for admission purposes, and the same subject combination rules apply as if the credits had come from a single diet.

Where this calculator stops

This tool estimates the letter grade, the eligibility verdict, and the O'Level screening figure from the marks you enter, applying the standard WAEC grading scale and the standard Nigerian five-credit admission rules. It does not know the specific subject combination your target programme requires, does not know the specific UTME and Post-UTME weightings that a particular university applies, and does not know the current-year cut-offs that the merit lists will produce. Those details vary by programme and by year, and the JAMB brochure and the university prospectus for the year of application are the definitive sources.

The grade thresholds used here match the published WAEC WASSCE scale, and the screening point values match the standard conversion used by the institutions that apply O'Level screening. The final grades on the WASSCE certificate are the ones WAEC issues after marking and moderation, and the final admission decision is the one the receiving institution makes on the full application. Use this calculator to see where the current marks point, to understand which subject would improve the screening figure the most if it moved up a grade, and to plan the examination and application strategy with the eligibility and ranking picture in front of you.

Frequently asked questions

What is the official WAEC 9-point grading scale in Nigeria?

WAEC grades WASSCE subjects on nine levels. A1 covers 75 to 100 per cent and is Excellent, B2 covers 70 to 74 per cent and is Very Good, B3 covers 65 to 69 per cent and is Good, C4 covers 60 to 64 per cent and is Credit, C5 covers 55 to 59 per cent and is Credit, C6 covers 50 to 54 per cent and is Credit, D7 covers 45 to 49 per cent and is an ordinary Pass, E8 covers 40 to 44 per cent and is an ordinary Pass, and F9 covers zero to 39 per cent and is a Fail.

What counts as a credit pass in WAEC?

Grades A1, B2, B3, C4, C5, and C6 are the credit passes. A minimum of five credit passes including English Language and Mathematics is the statutory baseline for admission into Nigerian universities and for most National Diploma and NCE programmes. Grades D7 and E8 are ordinary passes and cannot be used as credit prerequisites for degree admission, though some polytechnic and non-science programmes accept them in specific subjects.

Can a D7 or E8 be accepted for university admission?

D7 and E8 are ordinary passes rather than credits. For most degree programmes in Nigeria, they do not satisfy the credit requirement in the specific subject. A small number of pure humanities programmes such as English, Religious Studies, Languages, and Theatre Arts waive the Mathematics credit requirement at some institutions and accept a D7 or E8 in Mathematics provided five other credits including English Language are present. The admissions policy of the specific university for the specific programme is the definitive source.

How are WAEC grades converted into Post-UTME O'Level screening points?

Universities that use O'Level screening points, including UNILAG, LASU, UI, OAU, and UNIBEN, assign a numerical value to each of the five best prerequisite subjects. A1 is four point zero, B2 is three point six, B3 is three point two, C4 is two point eight, C5 is two point four, and C6 is two point zero. The five subject values sum to a maximum of twenty points, which is then combined with the UTME score and the Post-UTME CBT mark in the final composite.

What percentage do I need for an A1 in WAEC?

Grade A1 requires a composite percentage mark of seventy-five per cent or higher on the final WAEC record for the subject. The composite mark combines the internal Continuous Assessment Score that the school submits and the raw marks from the objective, theory, and practical papers that WAEC marks and moderates. Because the composite is what determines the grade, a candidate whose mock marks are near seventy-five per cent sits close to the A1 boundary even before the internal assessment is factored in.

Is C6 a full credit pass for university admission?

Yes. Grade C6 at fifty to fifty-four per cent is officially designated as a credit pass. It satisfies the statutory baseline set by the Joint Admissions and Matriculation Board, the National Universities Commission, and every Nigerian university for degree admission, provided the subject is one of the required prerequisites for the programme being applied for. A candidate with five credits including English Language and Mathematics at C6 is eligible for direct degree admission on the O'Level requirement.

What is the difference between WAEC May/June and WAEC GCE?

WAEC May/June is the WASSCE for school candidates, written by final-year secondary school students registered through a recognised secondary school. WAEC GCE, also called WASSCE for private candidates, is offered twice a year in a first series in January and February and a second series in August and September, and is open to independent candidates. Both certificates carry the same academic and legal value for admission into Nigerian and foreign universities, and both are accepted in the same way in admission screening.
