What this checker screens

The NSFAS Eligibility Checker takes a small set of facts and returns an estimated funding category for a South African student. It is designed to answer a practical question: given your citizenship, your household income, your SASSA status, your institution type, your qualification level, and how many years you have already been funded, what kind of NSFAS support are you likely to receive?

The answer comes back as one of a small number of categories. A full bursary for a SASSA beneficiary or a household under the standard income threshold. A full bursary with disability support if the higher cap applies. A Missing Middle loan and bursary conversion if income sits between the two limits. Or an ineligible outcome if a hard rule blocks funding.

The whole calculation runs in your browser. No part of your answer is stored anywhere, and no identifier is captured. The AI insight button is the only part of the page that reaches the network, and it only fires when you press it.

The inputs, explained

Citizenship. NSFAS funds South African citizens and permanent residents. International students and non-permanent residents are not eligible for the scheme, though some institutions run their own aid programmes. The tool treats this as a hard filter.

SASSA status. If you or a parent or guardian receives a SASSA grant, NSFAS can clear your financial eligibility through an automatic database match with the Department of Social Development. This bypasses the usual proof-of-income route. The tool treats SASSA status as a fast-track trigger that overrides the income input.

Combined household income. This is the total pre-tax yearly income of every working adult in your home, including salaries, wages, pensions, and rental income. It is compared against the two statutory thresholds. Being accurate here matters, because NSFAS verifies with SARS and credit bureaus.

Disability status. A certified permanent disability raises the income ceiling for the household. The higher cap means some families who would otherwise fall into the Missing Middle tier qualify for a full bursary instead, plus the assistive-device and human-support allowances that come with the disability quota.

Institution type. NSFAS funds the 26 public universities and 50 public TVET colleges. Private higher education institutions are outside the scheme.

Qualification level. NSFAS funds the first undergraduate qualification. The two exceptions are the PGCE and the MBChB, which DHET funds because of national teacher and doctor shortages. Other postgraduate study is not covered.

Academic progress. The N+1 rule caps funding at the minimum duration of a qualification plus one extra year. Students with a certified disability fall under N+2. Going past the limit suspends funding unless an academic appeal succeeds.

Income thresholds in plain numbers

There are two income ceilings that decide which funding tier applies, and one disability uplift that changes the number for households with a certified disability.

The standard bursary threshold is a combined gross household income of R350,000 per year. At or below that number, the household qualifies for a full bursary that covers tuition, registration, accommodation, a monthly living allowance, learning materials, and the first-year laptop scheme. Nothing about the standard bursary is repaid.

The Missing Middle tier runs from R350,001 to R600,000 per year. Households in this band do not qualify for the free bursary, but they can apply for the Missing Middle loan and grant scheme, which covers tuition and accommodation with a conversion-to-bursary mechanism if the student maintains a strong academic average. The exact conversion rate is set by DHET and can change year by year, so check the current published rules before you plan around it.

The disability uplift raises the household ceiling to R600,000 per year. This means a household earning R420,000 with a certified disabled applicant moves from the Missing Middle tier into the full bursary tier, plus the disability-specific allowances that cover assistive devices and human support.

Above R600,000, the household falls outside both tiers. Candidates in that band generally look at commercial student loans, the ISFAP bursary scheme, or university merit awards.

SASSA fast-track and how it is verified

The SASSA fast-track exists because verifying household income by document is slow and easy to falsify. Instead of asking for payslips, NSFAS matches the applicant and their parents against the Department of Social Development's grant database. If the match confirms an active grant, financial eligibility is cleared without further income proof.

The grants that count include the Child Support Grant, the Foster Child Grant, the Care Dependency Grant, and the Older Persons Grant. The list is set by policy and can be updated, so check the current myNSFAS guidance if you are unsure whether the grant in your home qualifies.

Two points are worth keeping in mind. First, a SASSA match clears the financial rule, not the academic rules. You still need to meet the institution and N+1 requirements, and the school still has to accept your admission. Second, the match happens when NSFAS processes your application. If your parent's grant is granted or cancelled during the application cycle, that can affect the outcome, so keep the household's grant status current.

Institution and qualification rules

NSFAS only funds study at a public institution. Public universities and public TVET colleges are covered. Private colleges, private distance institutions, and private higher education institutions are not on the list. If you are considering a private route, ask the institution about its own bursary scheme and about the ISFAP programme, which funds a different set of institutions.

On the qualification side, NSFAS funds the first undergraduate qualification only. A three-year degree, a diploma, or a TVET certificate all sit inside the first undergraduate band. Honours, Masters, PhD, and most postgraduate diplomas are outside the scheme.

The two exceptions to the postgraduate rule are the PGCE and the MBChB. DHET funds both because the country needs teachers and doctors and the supply from the undergraduate route alone has not kept up. If you are applying for either of these, the checker will treat them as eligible postgraduate qualifications.

One more rule that catches some applicants: if you already hold an undergraduate qualification from NSFAS-funded study, you cannot use NSFAS to fund a second one. The scheme is a first-qualification funder by design.

The N+1 rule and academic progress

The N+1 rule caps how long NSFAS will fund you for a single qualification. In plain terms, NSFAS pays for the minimum duration of the qualification plus one extra year to allow for a module repeat. A three-year degree receives a maximum of four funded years. A four-year degree receives five. A one-year certificate receives two.

Students with a certified disability fall under the N+2 rule, which adds two extra years to the standard duration. This exists to accommodate the additional time that can come with disability-related study interruptions.

Two things typically go wrong with the N+1 rule. First, some students repeat more modules than the +1 allows and find their funding suspended without warning. Second, students who switch courses midway can carry the years from the first course into the count for the second, which reduces the funded window for the new qualification. If you are considering a course change, ask the institution's financial aid office how your funded years will be counted.

If funding stops because of the N+1 rule, the usual route back is an academic appeal submitted through myNSFAS. The appeal works best when it is supported by evidence: medical certificates, affidavits, or institutional letters that explain the delay. The appeal does not guarantee reinstatement, but it is the standard path.

A worked example

Take a matriculant from Soweto applying for a BSc in Biological Sciences at a public university. Both parents work and earn a combined gross household income of R240,000 per year. The family does not receive a SASSA grant. The applicant has no certified disability. This is the first undergraduate qualification, and the applicant is a first-time entering student.

The citizenship check passes. The SASSA fast-track does not apply, so the income input decides the tier. At R240,000, the household is well under the R350,000 threshold, so the standard bursary tier applies. The institution check passes because the target is a public university. The qualification check passes because this is a first undergraduate degree. The N+1 check passes because no years have been funded yet.

The result is a full NSFAS bursary covering tuition, registration, on-campus or accredited accommodation, a monthly living allowance, learning materials, and the first-year laptop scheme. Nothing in that package has to be repaid.

Now change one thing: the parents' combined income rises to R460,000 because one parent takes a promotion. The applicant has no SASSA grant and no certified disability. On the same target course, the household moves out of the full bursary tier and into the Missing Middle band. The outcome becomes a Missing Middle loan with a conditional bursary conversion, and the applicant needs to think carefully about whether the academic conversion target is realistic for them before committing.

Common mistakes applicants make

Submitting late. The myNSFAS application window usually opens in the spring and closes early in the following year. Applications that arrive after the deadline are usually not processed for that cycle, even when the applicant is otherwise eligible.

Leaving the household income blank. Applications without a household income figure or a SASSA match often stall. If the household genuinely has no income, an affidavit confirming unemployment is required, not an empty field.

Using the wrong institution category. Some applicants assume any accredited college counts. NSFAS only funds public institutions, and the rule is strict. If the school appears on the private side of the line, the scheme will not apply, regardless of the household income.

Forgetting the consent form. NSFAS needs the consent form signed so it can run the financial verification against SARS and Home Affairs. Missing consent is one of the most common reasons an application is delayed or rejected.

Ignoring the N+1 rule. Students who repeat modules without tracking funded years can lose funding and not know why until the suspension notice arrives. Keep a simple record of the years already funded, and check it against the minimum duration of your qualification.

Assuming the Missing Middle loan is free. The loan converts to a bursary under specific academic conditions. If the conditions are not met, part of the funding must be repaid after graduation. Read the current DHET terms carefully and plan around them.

Letting a strong first year slip. NSFAS renewals depend on academic progress. A slow first semester can trigger a review. Get into the habit of reading module outcomes and tracking passes from the start.

Limits of the estimate

The biggest limit is that this checker is a screening tool, not a determination. NSFAS makes the final decision based on its own verification of income, grant status, citizenship, and academic records. Two applicants with identical answers on this page can receive different outcomes, because the real system sees documents and database matches that this page does not.

The second limit is that funding rules change. Thresholds, conversion rates, the list of qualifying grants, and the postgraduate exceptions are all set by DHET and are updated from time to time. This page reflects the general shape of the current rules, but the authoritative current version lives on the NSFAS and DHET websites.

The third limit is that the tool cannot tell you what your institution will accept. Even a fully cleared NSFAS applicant needs an admission offer to be funded. The scheme funds registered students, not applicants waiting for a place.

Read the result as a first filter. If the screening says you are eligible for a full bursary, the next step is to file a complete application on myNSFAS with all supporting documents. If it says you fall into the Missing Middle tier, the next step is to read the current DHET terms and decide whether the conversion target is achievable for you. If it says you are outside the scheme, the next step is to look at the other funding options and to ask the institution's financial aid office what they can offer.

Frequently asked questions

Is AfriWidget the same as the official NSFAS website?

No. AfriWidget is an independent screening tool. It does not connect to NSFAS, DHET, SASSA, or Home Affairs, and it cannot submit an application or a consent form. All official applications, status checks, and appeal submissions happen at my.nsfas.org.za. Use this page to plan what to do next, not as a substitute for the official portal.

Does NSFAS fund private colleges?

Under current DHET rules, NSFAS bursaries go to students registered at South Africa's 26 public universities and 50 public TVET colleges. Private Higher Education Institutions (PHEIs) and private distance colleges are not funded through NSFAS. If you are considering a private institution, ask its financial aid office about their own bursaries and the ISFAP scheme.

What is the Missing Middle scheme and who qualifies?

The Missing Middle scheme is aimed at students whose combined gross annual household income falls between R350,001 and R600,000. It covers tuition and accommodation, and a large share of the funding can convert into a bursary that does not need to be repaid if the student keeps a strong academic average. The exact conversion rules are set by DHET and can change year by year.

How does SASSA status affect my application?

If you or your parent or guardian receives an active SASSA grant, NSFAS can clear your financial eligibility through a database match with the Department of Social Development. That means you usually do not need to submit parental payslips or an affidavit. SASSA status is a shortcut, not a guarantee, and academic rules still apply.

What is the N+1 rule?

NSFAS funds a student for the minimum duration of a qualification plus one extra year to allow for module repeats. A three-year degree, for example, gets four years of funding. Students with a certified disability fall under the N+2 rule, which adds two extra years. If you go past the limit, funding stops unless you win an academic appeal.

What happens if I fail a module?

Failing a single module does not automatically end your funding, but you must stay within the N+1 rule and meet the annual academic pass requirements set by NSFAS. Falling behind the required module pass rate, or going past the N+1 limit, can suspend your bursary. The usual path back is an academic appeal through myNSFAS with supporting documents.

Do I need to reapply every year?

Once you are approved for your first undergraduate qualification, NSFAS renews automatically each year, provided you keep meeting the academic requirements, stay within the N+1 rule, and remain registered at a public institution. If you change institution or course, or if your household income changes sharply, update your profile on myNSFAS.

Can I get NSFAS for postgraduate study?

NSFAS funds the first undergraduate qualification only. Honours, Masters, PhD, and most postgraduate diplomas fall outside the scheme. The two main exceptions are the Postgraduate Certificate in Education (PGCE) and the MBChB medical degree, which DHET funds because of national teacher and doctor shortages. For other postgraduate studies, look at the National Research Foundation and university merit awards.

Does this tool store my income or ID number?

No. The whole calculation runs on your device. Nothing is stored on AfriWidget, and no identifier is captured. The only thing that leaves your device is the anonymous summary you send when you press the AI insight button: the tool name, the values you typed, and the computed result. No name, no email, no ID number, and no history.
