How Compound Interest Works

Compound interest allows earned interest to remain invested so that future interest is calculated on both the original principal and earlier interest. The result can accelerate over a long time horizon, especially when regular contributions are added. This calculator lets you compare an initial investment with optional monthly contributions, different interest rates, and annual, quarterly, monthly, weekly, or daily compounding.

The Compound Interest Formula

For a single deposit, the standard formula is A = P(1 + r/n)nt. A is the future value, P is the principal, r is the annual rate written as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year, and t is the number of years. When monthly contributions are included, each contribution has its own time in the market, so the calculator adds the value of the contribution stream to the growth of the original deposit.

Example: Starting With $5,000

Suppose you invest $5,000 at an annual rate of 8%, compound monthly, and add $100 at the end of each month for 20 years. Your final balance will include three different components: the original $5,000, the $24,000 in contributions, and the interest earned on both amounts over time. Entering those values lets you see the balance year by year rather than relying on a single end-of-period estimate.

How to Use the Results

Use the final balance to estimate the future value of the plan, total contributions to see how much money you personally supplied, and total interest to see how much growth came from the assumed return. The return on investment compares growth with the money invested. Try several rates and contribution amounts to understand which changes have the greatest effect instead of treating one projection as a promise.

Assumptions and Limitations

This is a mathematical projection, not a guarantee of investment performance. It assumes the interest rate stays constant, contributions are made regularly, and interest is reinvested. It does not subtract taxes, inflation, account charges, withdrawals, market losses, or currency changes. Actual returns vary, and investments can lose value. Speak with a qualified financial adviser before making decisions based on a projection.

Time, Contributions, and Inflation

Time in the market and the amount added regularly can matter as much as the headline interest rate. A small monthly contribution made for many years may produce more growth than a larger deposit left for only a short period. The displayed balance is a future nominal amount, so it does not show purchasing power. Inflation may reduce what that balance can buy, while fees and taxes can reduce the amount that remains invested. Compare scenarios using realistic rates and consider an inflation-adjusted estimate when planning a long-term goal.

Compare Scenarios Instead of Chasing One Rate

A useful way to use this calculator is to compare conservative, middle, and optimistic assumptions. Keep the starting amount and contribution schedule the same, then change only the rate or time horizon. This shows how sensitive the outcome is to each assumption and makes uncertainty visible. If you are comparing accounts or investments, also check minimum deposits, access rules, penalties, fees, taxes, and whether the stated rate is fixed, variable, nominal, or an annual percentage yield.

Compound Interest Questions

Does compounding more often always create a much higher result?

More frequent compounding can increase the mathematical result when the nominal rate is the same, but the difference may be smaller than the effect of adding money regularly or investing for a longer period.

Are monthly contributions included in the interest calculation?

Yes. The calculator models the optional monthly contribution as recurring invested money and includes its assumed growth in the final balance.

Can I use this for a real investment decision?

Use it for education and scenario planning. Confirm rates, fees, tax treatment, currency, and product terms with the relevant provider or a qualified adviser.
