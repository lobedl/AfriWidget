Why Canada has no single GPA scale

In the United States, a 4.0 scale is essentially universal. In the United Kingdom, degrees are classified as first, upper second, lower second, and third. Canada has neither a federal standard nor a single convention. Higher education is governed at the provincial level, and each province, and often each university within a province, has developed its own grading scheme over decades. The result is a patchwork that looks similar on the surface and behaves quite differently once you look at the details.

That patchwork causes real confusion for students, especially those applying to graduate school, professional programs, or institutions outside the province where they studied. Two students with identical transcripts by letter grade can end up with different grade point averages, because one university treats an A+ as worth 4.00 and the other treats it as worth 4.33. Neither is wrong. They simply follow different conventions, and the difference matters when a program publishes a cutoff on one specific scale.

This tool exists to make that patchwork navigable. It computes your GPA on whichever Canadian scale you select, and then shows the equivalent on the other four so you can compare your record against cutoffs published on any of them. All of it runs in your browser, and nothing you type is stored or transmitted.

The five grading systems you will meet

Almost every Canadian university uses one of five systems, and knowing which one applies to your institution is the first step to reading your own transcript.

The differences matter most at the top of the range. On a 4.00 scale, a student who earns an A+ is in the same grade point band as a student with an A on most courses, since the two are close. On a 4.33 scale, the A+ pulls slightly ahead. Over a degree of forty courses, that difference accumulates into a meaningful gap, and it can shift where you sit relative to a cutoff.

How a term GPA is actually calculated

Every Canadian GPA calculation, on every scale, follows the same three steps.

Step one: convert each grade to a grade point. On the OMSAS scale, an A+ becomes 4.00, an A becomes 3.90, an A− becomes 3.70. On the 4.33 scale, the same grades become 4.33, 4.00, and 3.67. On the York scale, they become 9, 8, and 8 respectively. The conversion is defined by the scale, not chosen by the student.

Step two: multiply each grade point by the credit weight of the course. This produces what universities call quality points. A course worth 0.5 credits and a grade point of 3.90 contributes 1.95 quality points. The same grade point in a full-year 1.0 credit course contributes 3.90. The weighting is what makes the GPA a weighted average rather than a simple mean of letter grades.

Step three: sum the quality points and divide by the sum of credits. That is the term GPA. The division is by total credits, not total courses, which means a heavy course pulls the average harder than a light one.

The formula in one line. Term GPA = (sum of grade points × credits) ÷ total credits. Every Canadian scale is a variation on this same arithmetic, with different numbers plugged into the first step.

Cumulative GPA and why it moves slowly

A cumulative GPA is the same arithmetic applied across every graded course you have ever completed at the institution. It does not reset between terms, it does not weight recent work more heavily than early work, and it does not discard your worst grades. That last point is the one that surprises students the most, because it means a single rough term in first year follows you to graduation.

The practical consequence is that the CGPA moves slowly. Once you have completed 60 credits, a single new term of 15 credits carries one-fifth of the total weight, so even a term of straight A grades will only move the CGPA by a fraction of a point. Conversely, a rough term in second year will not undo a strong first year, but it will drag the cumulative figure down by a visible amount.

The tool handles this when you fill in the prior record section. If you enter your prior CGPA and the total prior credits, the calculator combines your prior quality points with the current term's quality points and divides by the combined credit total. That is the exact formula your registrar uses, and it gives you a running picture you can update every term.

A worked OMSAS example

Take a second-year student in Ontario applying to medical school through OMSAS. Their fall term has five half-courses, each worth 0.5 credits, with the following grades and OMSAS conversions.

Organic Chemistry IA3.900.501.95

Sum the quality points: 1.95 + 2.00 + 1.85 + 1.95 + 1.65 = 9.40. Sum the credits: 0.50 + 0.50 + 0.50 + 0.50 + 0.50 = 2.50. Divide: 9.40 ÷ 2.50 = 3.76. The term GPA on the OMSAS scale is 3.76, which sits comfortably in the competitive range for Ontario medical schools, though the cutoff moves year to year and depends on the whole application file.

Now look at the same grades on the 4.33 scale. A becomes 4.00, A+ becomes 4.33, A− becomes 3.67, B+ becomes 3.33. Sum the quality points: 2.00 + 2.165 + 1.835 + 2.00 + 1.665 = 9.665. Divide by 2.50: 3.866. The same transcript gives 3.87 on the 4.33 scale, which looks meaningfully higher than the 3.76 it produced on the OMSAS scale. That gap is entirely arithmetic. It is not a difference in the underlying achievement.

A worked UBC 4.33 example

Now take a third-year student in the Sauder School of Business at UBC. Their cumulative record from two years of study is a CGPA of 3.40 on the 4.33 scale, with 60 credits completed. That means they have accumulated 3.40 × 60 = 204.00 quality points.

In the fall term of their third year they take five courses, all three credits each, for a total of 15 credits. Their grades are A (4.00) in Strategic Management, A+ (4.33) in Corporate Finance, A (4.00) in Information Systems, A− (3.67) in Economic Statistics, and A+ (4.33) in Marketing Strategy.

Sum the quality points for the term: 12.00 + 12.99 + 12.00 + 11.01 + 12.99 = 60.99. Divide by 15 credits: term GPA 4.066 on the 4.33 scale. That is a very strong term.

The updated cumulative: prior quality points 204.00 plus current 60.99, divided by prior 60 credits plus current 15 credits, which is 264.99 ÷ 75 = 3.533. The CGPA has moved from 3.40 to 3.53 in a single term, which is a visible improvement but not a transformation. To reach a final CGPA of 3.70 by graduation at 120 credits, this student would need to accumulate 444.00 total quality points, which means 179.01 more across the remaining 45 credits, an average of 3.978 per credit. That is achievable with sustained A and A+ work but leaves little room for a weak term.

Same grade, different province, different number

The single most useful thing to internalise about Canadian GPA scales is that the numbers are not interchangeable through simple multiplication. A 3.80 on a 4.00 scale does not become 4.11 on a 4.33 scale. It becomes whatever the underlying letter grades produce when re-mapped onto the 4.33 scale and re-weighted. Depending on the mix of grades, that conversion can move the number up, down, or leave it roughly unchanged.

The reason is that the scales treat the top of the range differently. On the OMSAS scale, an A+ and an A are separated by 0.10 grade points. On the 4.33 scale they are separated by 0.33. On the McMaster 12.0 scale, they are separated by a full point, and the 12-point maximum only exists for A+ grades. A student with a mix of A and A+ grades on the 4.00 scale will see their number climb when re-mapped to 4.33, while a student with mostly A grades and few A+ grades will see it barely move.

Professional school applications handle this correctly by converting course by course rather than converting the CGPA in one step. OMSAS, for example, takes each individual grade from every transcript it receives, looks up the corresponding OMSAS value in its conversion table, and computes the admissions GPA from those converted values. The final number is not a transformed version of your institutional CGPA. It is a fresh calculation, and it can differ from what you see on your transcript.

Common mistakes with Canadian GPA

A handful of mistakes turn up again and again when students try to work out their GPA by hand.

Treating all courses as equal weight. Credit weight is not cosmetic. A full-year course with 1.0 credits moves the GPA twice as much as a half-course. If you average letter grades without weighting, the result will not match your registrar's figure, and it will usually flatter students who did well in light courses.

Assuming a percentage average is a GPA. An 85 per cent weighted average on the UofT or McGill scale is not the same as a 3.85 GPA. The conversion between percentages and letter grades is a separate step, and it differs by institution. Ontario universities in particular publish their own conversion tables that are not interchangeable.

Forgetting that transfer credits usually carry no grade. Transfer credits typically arrive without a letter grade, which means they add to your credit total but leave the GPA untouched. That is why transfer students often see their CGPA dilute in the first term after transferring, even when their new grades are strong.

Applying institutional GPA to OMSAS or OLSAS. Ontario professional school applications use their own conversion processes. A 3.80 institutional CGPA might convert to 3.75 on OMSAS, or it might convert to 3.85. The only way to know is to run the conversion course by course, which is what this tool does when you select the OMSAS scale.

Leaving out failed courses. An F contributes zero grade points, but it also contributes credit weight to the denominator, which pulls the GPA down. Excluding failed courses from your own calculation overstates your GPA and hides the actual picture your registrar will report.

Assuming CGPA moves quickly. A single strong term will not transform a CGPA that has 90 credits of history behind it. That is not a reason to give up on improving it, but it is a reason to plan for the improvement to take several terms rather than one.

Where this calculator stops

This tool applies the standard published conversion tables for each Canadian scale and computes the GPA on the arithmetic that every registrar uses. It does not know your institution's specific policies on repeats, exclusions, or non-graded courses. It does not know whether your programme uses a different scale than the university-wide one. It does not handle transfer credits, and it does not compute discipline-specific GPAs that some graduate programmes require.

What it does give you is a clean, transparent number you can plan around, and an equivalent on the other four scales so you can compare against any cutoff that is published in one of those scales. If you are preparing an application to a Canadian graduate or professional program, the number you should trust above everything else is the one the receiving institution calculates from your transcript. This tool is a planning aid, not an admissions decision.

If your institution publishes its own conversion table — and many do — that table is the authority for your institution. When a cutoff is published as "3.70 on a 4.0 scale" and your institutional scale is the McMaster 12.0, use this tool to see what your 12.0 average would look like if it were being evaluated on a 4.0 scale, and then confirm the specific policy with the receiving institution before you rely on the number.

Frequently asked questions

Why does Canada have more than one GPA scale?

Because higher education is governed at the provincial level in Canada, not federally. Each province and each institution sets its own grading convention, and the differences have accumulated over decades. Ontario universities largely converge on the OMSAS 4.0 scale for professional school applications. British Columbia and Atlantic Canada commonly use a 4.33 scale so that A+ results can be distinguished from A results. York University uses a 9-point integer scale. McMaster and Carleton use a 12-point scale. Many institutions work in percentage weighted averages instead of letter grades at all.

What is the difference between OMSAS GPA and my institutional GPA?

Your institutional GPA is calculated using your own university's scale, whatever that scale is. OMSAS, run by the Ontario Universities' Application Centre, is a separate process used by Ontario medical schools. It takes each individual course grade from your transcript, converts it to a standardised 4.00 scale using its own conversion tables, and computes a fresh GPA from those converted values. OMSAS does not simply take the CGPA from your transcript and re-label it. The conversion happens course by course, and the resulting OMSAS GPA can differ meaningfully from your institutional one.

How much does a full-year course count in the GPA calculation?

A full-year course usually carries twice the credit weight of a single-term course, which means it has roughly twice the impact on your GPA. In most Canadian systems a single-term half-course is weighted 0.5 credits, and a two-term full-year course is weighted 1.0 credits. Some institutions use unit counts instead, where a half-course is 3 units and a full course is 6 units. Either way, the weighting is what makes the GPA more than a simple average of letter grades.

What counts as a good GPA in Canada?

The honest answer is that it depends on what you are aiming for. A cumulative GPA around 3.70 out of 4.00, or 3.85 out of 4.33, or 10.0 out of 12.0, or a weighted average above 80 per cent is generally treated as an honours level result. That range opens graduate school, most scholarships, and Dean's Honour List recognition. For Canadian medical school, the competitive range is typically 3.85 to 3.95 on the OMSAS scale. For most professional employment outside academia, the specific number matters far less than the degree and the work history that comes with it.

What happens if I repeat a course?

Institutional policies vary. Some universities record both attempts on the transcript but use only the second attempt in the institutional CGPA. Others average the two attempts. Ontario professional schools through OMSAS usually count both attempts equally in the admissions GPA, regardless of what your home institution does. If you are considering a repeat, check both the institutional policy and the application rules for any professional programme you may apply to later, because the two can differ in ways that matter.

Why do I not see an exact match when I convert between Canadian scales?

Because the scales were designed independently, and the grade bands do not line up perfectly at the boundaries. An A+ is 4.00 on OMSAS, 4.33 on the UBC scale, 12 on McMaster, and 9 at York. Those numbers are not proportional to each other. A GPA of 3.80 on a 4.00 scale does not convert to 4.11 on a 4.33 scale by simple multiplication. The right way to convert is to look at each course's letter grade, map it to the target scale, and re-weight. This calculator runs that conversion for you on the equivalent-scales matrix.

Do I include failed courses in my GPA?

Yes, failed courses count. An F carries 0.00 grade points on every Canadian scale, but it still adds credit weight to the denominator, which pulls the GPA down. Some institutions allow a certain number of failed courses to be excluded from the CGPA calculation under a formal academic policy, but that is the exception rather than the rule, and the exclusion usually needs to be applied for. Unless your calendar says otherwise, assume every graded course counts.

Does the tool work for transfer credits or pass/fail courses?

Transfer credits usually arrive without a letter grade attached, which means they add to your total credits but do not affect your GPA. Pass/fail, audit, and credit/no-credit courses are treated the same way in most Canadian institutions. This tool assumes that any course you enter carries a numeric letter grade that counts towards the GPA. If a course on your record is pass/fail, leave it out.

Why does my CGPA move so slowly?

Because a cumulative GPA is a weighted average across every graded course you have ever taken at the institution. Once you have completed 60 or 90 credits, a single new term of 15 credits moves the CGPA by a small amount, even if every grade in that term is very high or very low. That arithmetic is what makes it hard to recover from a bad first year, and it is also what makes it hard to inflate a CGPA at the end of a degree. The practical consequence is that each term's grades matter in proportion to how much of the degree remains.

Does the tool send my grades anywhere?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the chosen scale, the number of courses, the rounded term GPA, the rounded cumulative CGPA, and the credit total is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Course names, course grades, and your prior record never leave the browser.
