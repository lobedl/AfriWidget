What this calculator does

This tool takes two lists of costs and one choice, and returns the total capital a business needs to open and survive its first few months. The first list holds one-time launch costs such as equipment, legal fees, branding, lease deposits, and initial inventory. The second list holds recurring monthly operating expenses such as rent, software subscriptions, marketing, and salaries. The choice is a working capital buffer of three, six, or twelve months of operating expenses held in reserve. The total is the sum of the one-time costs plus the buffer amount.

Everything runs inside the browser. Each line is added, multiplied, and totalled locally in the page, and nothing is sent to a server. The currency selector formats every figure consistently so you can work in USD, Ghanaian cedi, Nigerian naira, Kenyan shilling, South African rand, euro, pound, or one of the other supported currencies. If the AI insight button is pressed, only anonymous counts and totals leave the browser, never the item names or any personal detail.

The result is a planning figure, not a prediction. It is only as accurate as the numbers entered, and the sections below explain how to think about each of them, what commonly gets left out, and how to interpret the range between the three runway scenarios.

The two pillars of a startup budget

A startup budget is not one number, it is two. The first is a set of costs that happen once and then stop. The second is a set of costs that keep happening every month. Mixing the two together, or forgetting the second, is the most common reason a new business runs out of cash before it finds its market.

PillarTimingTypical examples
One-time launch costsBefore openingEquipment, registration, branding, deposits, initial inventory
Monthly operating expensesEvery monthRent, software, marketing, salaries, insurance, utilities

One-time costs are sometimes called capital expenditures or capex. They include everything required to turn an idea into an operating entity. Some of them are obvious, such as a laptop or a shop fit-out. Others are easy to overlook, such as legal filings, permit fees, utility connection deposits, and the cost of designing a logo and website before there are any customers.

Monthly operating expenses are the day-to-day burn. They continue whether or not the business is making sales in a given month, which is what makes them dangerous for a new business. A short list of them, multiplied by twelve, usually dwarfs the one-time total. That is why a buffer is essential: without one, the first slow month is also the last.

Why the working capital buffer matters

Most new businesses do not break even on day one. Reaching the point where monthly revenue covers monthly expenses can take anywhere from three to eighteen months depending on the industry, the market, and the founder's existing network. During that ramp-up period, the business still has to pay rent, software, and salaries, and the money for those payments has to come from somewhere.

That is what the working capital buffer covers. It is money set aside to fund the operating expenses during the months before the business is paying for itself. The choice of three, six, or twelve months is a practical judgement about how fast the business is likely to stabilise and how rigid the costs are.

BufferBest suited toWhy
3 monthsFreelancers, consultants, digital servicesCosts scale down easily; work can start before the business is "open"
6 monthsSmall retail, e-commerce, agenciesEnough time to iterate marketing and build a repeat customer base
12 monthsRestaurants, manufacturing, licensed businessesFixed costs cannot be reduced quickly, and approvals take time

A useful check. If the buffer you chose would leave the business unable to pay two consecutive months of rent from a standing start, you have chosen too little. Add a month or two of cushion before opening, or trim the one-time costs to free up cash for the buffer.

The formula for total capital required

The arithmetic behind the tool is deliberately simple so that you can reproduce it on paper or in a spreadsheet and check that the tool is giving sensible output.

Total Capital Required = One-Time Launch Costs + (Monthly OpEx × Buffer Months)

A worked example makes the formula concrete. Suppose a new design agency has one-time costs of five thousand dollars and monthly operating expenses of two thousand eight hundred dollars. If the founder chooses a six-month buffer, the reserve is sixteen thousand eight hundred dollars, and the total capital required is twenty-one thousand eight hundred dollars. If the founder chose a three-month buffer instead, the reserve would be eight thousand four hundred dollars and the total would be thirteen thousand four hundred dollars. The difference between the two figures is the extra safety that six months of buffer buys.

The matrix in the results panel shows all three scenarios side by side for exactly this reason. The three scenarios use the same one-time costs and the same monthly burn, and only the buffer length changes. Reading down the total column shows how much extra cash each additional month of safety represents, which is often the single most useful number on the page.

Common startup cost categories

The right list of one-time costs and monthly expenses depends on the business, but a handful of categories appear in almost every case and are worth checking before you finalise the plan.

Legal and registration. Business registration, filing fees, licenses, permits, trademark applications, and any professional advice required to set up the structure. These are the smallest line items on most budgets but also the ones that stop a launch dead if they are missed.

Branding and digital presence. Logo, domain, hosting, website build, initial content, social media setup, and any printed materials such as business cards or signage. A working website is a one-time cost; the ongoing hosting and domain renewals belong in the monthly list.

Equipment and hardware. Computers, phones, point-of-sale systems, machinery, tools, office furniture, and any specialised equipment the business cannot operate without. If the equipment is leased rather than bought, the monthly lease belongs in the recurring list instead.

Lease and location. Security deposits, utility connection fees, and any fit-out or renovation costs for a physical location. The monthly rent and utilities go in the operating expenses, not the one-time list.

Initial inventory and supplies. The first batch of raw materials or finished goods, packaging, and any baseline office supplies the business needs to operate for its first few weeks.

Insurance and professional retainers. General liability, professional indemnity, and any ongoing accounting or legal retainers. The one-time setup fees belong here; the monthly premiums belong in the operating list.

Taxes and payroll. Any registration taxes, first payroll cycle costs, and the employer-side contributions for the first hire. This is the category founders most often forget because it appears a month or two after launch rather than on day one.

Consider a new e-commerce store selling handmade goods. The founder has saved fifteen thousand dollars and wants to know whether that is enough to launch with a comfortable buffer.

The founder has fifteen thousand dollars, so the six-month plan leaves a small surplus of three thousand three hundred dollars. That surplus is a real safety net for the first few months if sales are slower than expected. The founder could also choose the twelve-month buffer, which would push the total to seventeen thousand seven hundred dollars and require raising an additional two thousand seven hundred dollars from savings or a small loan.

Reading the matrix this way is the point of the tool. It turns a vague sense of "I think I have enough" into a specific number for each runway horizon, so the decision about whether to open now or wait a few more months is made with real figures rather than with optimism.

Common mistakes

Forgetting the founder's own living costs. If you are leaving a salaried job, your rent, food, transport, and insurance have to be paid from somewhere. Add a founder draw line to the monthly expenses, set to the minimum you actually need to live on. Leaving it out makes the runway look longer than it is.

Treating the buffer as optional. The buffer is the part of the budget that keeps the business alive during the first slow months. A launch with the one-time costs funded but the buffer missing is not a launch, it is a countdown.

Underestimating the one-time costs by ten to thirty per cent. Real costs usually come in higher than the first estimate because of shipping, customs, taxes, installation, and small setup fees that are only discovered at the till. Add a contingency line before you finalise the numbers.

Ignoring payment processor fees and chargebacks. If the business sells online, the payment processor takes a cut of every transaction and may charge fees for disputes. This is not a one-time cost; it belongs in the monthly operating list at a realistic estimate of transaction volume.

Assuming month-one revenue will cover month-one costs. Very few businesses reach that point on the first month. The buffer exists because the first few months are unpredictable, and the useful question is not "will the business make money in month one?" but "how many months can the business survive if it does not?"

Borrowing the entire buffer. If the total capital required is funded mostly by a loan, the loan repayments themselves become a monthly operating expense and the buffer needs to grow to cover them. Include the loan repayment in the monthly list before choosing the buffer length.

Never revisiting the numbers. Costs change. Rent goes up, software prices increase, and shipping rates move. Rerun the calculation every few months during the first year, and adjust the numbers with what you have actually learned.

Where this tool stops

This calculator adds the numbers you enter and applies a buffer multiplier. It does not know whether the numbers are realistic for your market, whether the itemised costs are complete, or whether the buffer length is appropriate for the specific business. It does not include tax calculations, which vary by jurisdiction, business structure, and revenue level. It does not model seasonality, supplier payment terms, or the timing difference between a sale and the cash actually landing in the bank.

It also does not provide business advice or investment advice. The decision to open a business, to raise capital, to take on a loan, or to delay a launch is a decision for the founder and for any qualified advisor they choose to work with. The tool produces a planning figure, and its usefulness depends on the quality of the numbers that go into it.

What the tool does give you is a fast, transparent, private way to build a startup budget, see the total capital required under three different runway scenarios, and copy the summary to a clipboard for a pitch deck or a conversation with an accountant. Used as one input among several, it is a practical starting point for anyone planning to open a business.

Frequently asked questions

How many months of working capital should a new business hold?

Six months is the standard recommendation for most new businesses, because that is roughly how long it takes for sales to stabilise into a predictable pattern. Low-overhead service businesses such as consultants, writers, and developers can often run safely on three months because their costs scale down easily if sales are slow. Brick-and-mortar shops, restaurants, manufacturers, and any business that requires fit-out or regulatory approval usually benefit from twelve months because the ramp-up is slower and the fixed costs cannot be reduced quickly.

What is the difference between startup capital and working capital?

Startup capital is the full amount of money it takes to open the business, including the one-time costs such as equipment, legal fees, branding, deposits, and initial inventory. Working capital is the day-to-day cash that keeps the business running while sales are still building. On this calculator, the one-time costs section is startup capital, and the working capital buffer is the money set aside for the first several months of operating expenses before the business reaches break-even.

Should I include my personal living costs in the startup budget?

Yes, and this is one of the most common mistakes new founders make. If you are leaving a salaried job, you have to cover your own rent, food, transport, health insurance, and family obligations from somewhere while the business is still building. The right way to handle it is to add a founder draw or personal salary line to the monthly operating expenses, set to the minimum you actually need to live on. That way the runway figure you get at the end includes the personal safety net rather than pretending it does not exist.

What startup costs are commonly overlooked?

The costs most often missed are insurance premiums, payment processor fees and chargebacks, accounting and bookkeeping fees, ongoing software price increases after the first year, equipment maintenance, utility connection deposits, domain and hosting renewals, professional memberships, licenses and permits that renew annually, marketing costs beyond launch, and tax obligations that come due before you may feel ready. Adding a small contingency line of ten to fifteen per cent of the one-time costs is a practical way to absorb these without redoing the whole budget later.

Should I use the amount I have saved or the amount I actually need?

Always start with the amount you need, then compare it against what you have. If the total capital required comes out higher than what is available today, you have three honest options: delay the launch until you have saved more or raised the difference, reduce the scope of the launch by trimming one-time costs, or lower the monthly operating expenses so the required buffer shrinks. What you should not do is pretend the lower number is the real one, because the shortfall will find you in month two or three, and it is far harder to recover from then.

How accurate is a startup cost calculator?

A calculator is a planning tool, not a prediction. It is only as accurate as the numbers you put into it, and most new founders underestimate both the one-time costs and the monthly burn. In practice, real costs often come in ten to thirty per cent higher than the first estimate. That is why the tool includes a buffer rather than trying to produce a single exact figure. Run the calculation once with your best estimate, add a reasonable contingency on top, and revisit the numbers every month as you learn what things actually cost in your market.

Does this calculator include taxes?

Not automatically, because tax treatment varies by country, business structure, and revenue level. You should add an estimate for company tax, VAT or sales tax registration, payroll taxes if you are hiring, and any local business tax into the monthly operating expenses. In most markets a reasonable starting figure is somewhere between ten and thirty per cent of net profit for tax, but the exact number depends on the jurisdiction. A local accountant can give you a much better figure in one conversation than any calculator can.

What if my business makes sales in month one?

That is a good sign, but it does not remove the need for a buffer. Early revenue is often unpredictable, and the first few months of a new business usually see a mix of encouraging weeks and quiet ones. The buffer exists to cover the quiet weeks so you do not have to make panicked decisions about pricing or discounts. If the business is genuinely covering its monthly costs from month one, you can consider reducing the buffer to three months and keeping the difference as a separate emergency reserve.

Is my financial data stored or shared?

No. Every cost line, every amount, and every currency choice stays inside your browser tab. The arithmetic runs locally using JavaScript, and nothing is sent to a server. The page is a static file, so there is no database behind it. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the structure is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. The number of one-time lines, the number of monthly lines, the currency code, the chosen buffer months, and the total figures are shared, but the item names and any personal detail are not.

Can I use this calculator for a business outside the United States?

Yes. The arithmetic is the same everywhere because the tool only adds up the numbers you enter. The currency selector lets you work in USD, Ghanaian cedi, Nigerian naira, Kenyan shilling, South African rand, euro, pound, Canadian dollar, Australian dollar, or Indian rupee. What changes between countries is the tax treatment, the registration fees, the insurance requirements, and the typical cost of labour and premises. The one-time costs and monthly expenses you enter should reflect local prices, and a local advisor is the right person to confirm the tax and regulatory line items.
