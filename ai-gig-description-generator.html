What this generator actually does

This tool takes your service notes and turns them into a first draft of a gig description that is suitable for Fiverr, Upwork, or a direct proposal to a client. You describe the service, the client you want to reach, the deliverables you actually provide, the turnaround you can realistically hold, and the way you work. The generator reorganises those notes into a structure that reads clearly from top to bottom, then prints the draft on the same page.

The draft is a starting point, not a finished listing. Every platform has its own character limits, category rules, and tone expectations, and every client has their own reading speed and attention span. The draft gives you a clear structure: an opening line that names the service, a middle section that describes what the buyer gets, and a closing section that sets expectations and invites the next step. You then trim, edit, and adapt it for the specific place you are publishing.

Everything on this page runs inside your browser. When you press the generate button, only an anonymous summary of your form fields, the service, the client, the deliverables, the turnaround, and the process notes, is sent to a serverless function. No names, emails, phone numbers, client identifiers, or account details. Nothing is stored on our side, and the request is not linked back to you later.

Why Nigerian freelancers especially benefit

Nigeria has one of the largest freelance populations on the continent. From Lagos and Abuja to Port Harcourt, Ibadan, and Kano, designers, developers, writers, video editors, and virtual assistants are working with clients in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, and the Gulf. Platforms like Fiverr and Upwork are where many Nigerian freelancers get their first international clients, and the quality of the listing is one of the few things fully within a freelancer's control.

The reality of working from Nigeria also shapes the writing. Buyers abroad sometimes assume slower delivery, patchy connectivity, or communication gaps, and the gig description is the place to quietly correct those assumptions. A line that says "I work on Lagos time and reply within a few hours on business days" does more for trust than any claim about being hardworking. A description that names the specific tools and file formats you use, whether that is Canva, Figma, Notion, or Microsoft Word, removes a small doubt before the buyer has to ask.

Nigerian buyers also matter. Small businesses in Lagos, fashion labels on Instagram, food brands, logistics startups, and content creators increasingly hire freelancers directly. Those buyers have their own reading habits: they scroll quickly, they compare prices openly, and they expect a clear scope before they send a deposit. A gig description or pitch that respects those habits will win more work than one written for a foreign reader and pasted into a local context.

There is also a practical dimension. Payment is often the first friction point in a Nigerian freelance conversation. Platform fees, currency conversion, and naira-to-dollar timing all affect what you actually receive, and those are not things you put in the description. But the description shapes what the buyer expects to pay for, and a clear scope protects you from being asked to add extra work at the same price once the conversation moves to WhatsApp or email.

The six things every good gig description nails

A gig description has a job to do, and it usually does it in six sentences or fewer at the top. Buyers scan. They decide in the first two or three lines whether to read the rest.

The opening line names the service. Not a clever headline. The service. "I write Shopify product descriptions for fashion brands" beats "Turn browsers into buyers with words that convert" every single time on a marketplace where the buyer is comparing ten similar profiles.

The second line names the client. "My clients are usually small brands doing under 200 orders a month" tells the right buyer immediately that you understand their world. It also quietly filters out the wrong buyer, which is a good thing.

The third and fourth lines describe the deliverables. How many items, how long, what format, how many rounds of edits. This is the part that earns trust. Buyers who have been burned by vague scope will read these lines carefully.

The fifth line covers process. What you need from the client to start, what happens in the first day, and how feedback moves back and forth. Short. Concrete. No process theatre.

The sixth line sets the next step. "Send a message with your product details before ordering" or "Message me with the brief and I'll confirm the timeline" gives the buyer a clear action. An open-ended ending lets the buyer drift off to a competitor.

Everything after those six lines is optional. Longer descriptions can work, especially on Upwork proposals, but the first minute of reading is where the decision happens.

Deliverables, scope, and the boundary question

The deliverables section is where a gig either feels professional or feels vague. Specifics matter. "10 product descriptions" is a specific number. "Some descriptions" is not. "Up to 150 words each" is measurable. "Detailed descriptions" is not. "Google Doc plus CSV for bulk upload" is concrete. "Various formats" is not.

Scope boundaries matter just as much, and they protect you. Say how many revision rounds are included and what counts as a revision. A revision is a correction to the work you delivered. A new direction, an added page, or a change of target audience is a new scope, and asking to be paid for it is fair, not difficult. Writing that boundary into the description up front saves an awkward conversation later.

The same applies to what the client needs to provide. A designer needs the logo brief, dimensions, colours, and any reference images. A writer needs the product facts, brand voice, and target audience. A developer needs access details, a test environment, and a description of the acceptance criteria. Listing these in the description makes the service feel organised and gives the buyer a checklist before they place the order.

A useful rule. If a deliverable cannot be counted, weighed, or pointed at, it is not a deliverable. It is a feeling. Rewrite the line until a stranger could say exactly what they would receive.

Writing for a global buyer and a local one

Most Nigerian freelancers on Fiverr and Upwork are writing for buyers who do not share their time zone, their currency, or their cultural references. That does not mean the writing should sound foreign. It means it should be plain. Short sentences. Clear verbs. Concrete nouns. A buyer in Toronto or Dubai should be able to read the description at a glance and understand exactly what happens next.

The same description usually needs adjusting for a Nigerian SME pitching direct. Local buyers respond to slightly warmer language, to references they recognise, and to pricing that is transparent in naira. They also tend to want a phone call or a WhatsApp conversation earlier than international buyers do, so a line like "Happy to jump on a short call if that helps" can move a Nigerian project forward faster than a formal proposal email.

A useful approach is to write one core description and keep three versions: a short marketplace version, a longer proposal version, and a direct-pitch version with slightly warmer tone. The core stays the same. The opening line and the closing line change. That way you are not rewriting from scratch each time you open a new conversation.

Language details are also worth paying attention to. If your client base includes Nigerian small businesses, some descriptions can carry a light Nigerian English cadence without becoming informal. If your client base is international, keep it neutral. Do not force slang, and do not apologise for accent or origin. The description is not the place for either.

Pricing, payment, and platform rules

Fiverr and Upwork both handle their own payments. Their terms do not allow you to move a client off the platform, and putting a bank account number or a direct payment request inside a gig description is one of the fastest ways to get a listing suspended. Keep payment information in the invoice or the platform's own contract, and keep it out of the public listing.

On the platforms themselves, put the price in the package card and the scope in the description. Fiverr buyers compare the three package cards side by side before they read the body text. Upwork proposals give you a section for your rate and a section for your cover letter. Both places have their own logic, and both are where a buyer expects to find those numbers. A price buried in the middle of a description reads as a placeholder and confuses later edits.

For direct pitches to Nigerian businesses, a range in the description is often acceptable. Be explicit that the range depends on scope. A single sentence such as "Projects typically fall between X and Y depending on the number of items and the turnaround, and I confirm the final price after the brief" is honest, protects you, and gives the buyer a starting expectation. It also avoids the situation where the client assumes a fixed price before they have described what they actually want.

The final rule to keep in mind is about claims. Do not promise a specific number of orders, a specific increase in sales, or a specific ranking. Not only do those promises fail quietly in the real world, in Nigeria they can also fall under the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission rules on misleading advertising. Keep the description to what you actually deliver, and let the reviews do the rest.

A worked example

Suppose a Lagos-based freelance writer named Amaka wants to pitch a product description service to fashion brands on Instagram. She writes the following notes into the form.

Service: Shopify product descriptions for fashion brands. Ideal client: Small Lagos and Abuja fashion labels selling on Instagram and Shopify. Deliverables: 10 product descriptions per package, up to 150 words each, in a Google Doc, with a CSV version for bulk upload, and two rounds of edits included. Turnaround: Three business days after receiving the product details and any brand voice notes. Process: I send a short brief template before we start, share a sample of one description within 24 hours of receiving materials, and keep all comments in a single shared document.

A reasonable draft would open with the service, name the client, list the deliverables and format, note the turnaround, describe the process in one or two sentences, and close with a clear next step. The final paragraph would invite the buyer to send product details and confirm the timeline. Nothing about the draft needs to be clever. The point is that a stranger in another country can read it in twenty seconds and know exactly what happens next.

Amaka would then adjust the draft slightly for a local pitch. The opening line might add a mention of Instagram, which is where most of her local clients find her. The closing line might offer a short WhatsApp call. She would keep the pricing out of the description and put a range in the proposal email instead, with a clear note that the final figure depends on the number of items and the timeline.

Common mistakes that cost orders

The same handful of mistakes show up again and again on Nigerian freelance profiles.

Opening with a slogan instead of the service. A buyer scanning ten listings does not have time to decode a tagline. Name what you sell in the first line, then earn the right to be clever later.

Promising unlimited revisions. It is one of the fastest ways to attract the wrong buyer and to end up doing unpaid work in week three. State the number of rounds and what counts as a new scope.

Burying the deliverables in adjectives. "High quality, professional, fast" is not a deliverable. "10 items, up to 150 words each, delivered in Google Doc and CSV" is.

Copying a competitor's listing word for word. Marketplaces notice, buyers notice, and the algorithm treats duplicate text the same way it treats duplicate text anywhere. Write your own description, even if the structure is similar.

Pricing in the description on a marketplace. Fiverr and Upwork both have their own package cards and rate fields. Put the price there and the scope in the description.

Ignoring the platform rules on contact and payment. Asking a client to move off-platform can get an account restricted. Keep the conversation on the platform for the first project at least.

Writing for the wrong reader. A listing aimed at enterprise clients in the US reads differently from one aimed at a Nigerian fashion label. Both are fine. Trying to write for both at once usually produces a listing that fits neither.

Where this tool stops

This generator produces a first draft. It reorganises the notes you give it, it does not invent content, and it does not know your category rules, your pricing strategy, or the specific client you are trying to reach on a particular day. The draft still needs your eye before it goes live. Read it aloud once, cut anything that sounds like it was written by someone else, and check that the deliverables and turnaround match what you actually do.

It also does not check whether a claim is defensible, whether a client name can be used, or whether the description breaks a platform rule. Those are on you. If you are pitching a regulated service such as legal drafting, medical writing, or financial advice, consult the relevant professional body before publishing. If you are using a client name or a case study, get written permission.

What this tool gives you is a faster start and a cleaner structure. It turns scattered notes into a first version that respects the reader. The rest of the work, choosing the right words, testing the listing, and improving it based on real enquiries, is still the freelancer's job, and it should be.

Frequently asked questions

Will this gig description generator get me more orders on Fiverr?

No tool can promise orders. A clear description helps the right client understand what you deliver, which usually improves the quality of enquiries. Orders depend on your category, pricing, portfolio, response speed, reviews, and how well you match what the buyer actually needs. Use the draft as a starting point, keep the wording honest, and put your energy into the parts of the listing that a buyer sees before they read the body text: thumbnail, title, package cards, and reviews.

Can I use the same gig description on Fiverr, Upwork, and other platforms?

You can, but you should not. Fiverr buyers scan quickly and rely on package cards, so the body text usually works best when it is short and tightly scoped. Upwork proposals are longer and often read by someone comparing three or four freelancers, so the description needs a little more context about process and outcomes. Local Nigerian platforms and direct pitches to SMEs may need a different tone again. Write one core description, then adapt the opening and the deliverable list for each place.

Does the generator invent clients, results, or experience?

No. It reorganises the notes you provide. If you type in that you have five years of experience, it will reflect that, but it will not add claims you did not enter. Never paste results you cannot support, and never include a client name without written permission. In Nigeria, advertising claims can also fall under the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission rules, so keeping the copy honest is both a marketplace requirement and a legal one.

How do I handle pricing in the description?

Put the scope in the description and the price in the package card or the proposal. Fiverr and Upwork both give you a structured place to enter price, delivery time, and revisions, and that is where buyers expect to find those numbers. A price written into the middle of the description looks like a placeholder and often creates confusion when you adjust it later. If you are pitching directly to a Nigerian SME, then a range in the description can be fine, but make it explicit that it depends on scope.

Is the description good for buyers who are not native English speakers?

That is exactly the audience you should assume. A large share of global buyers, and a growing share of Nigerian buyers, read English as a second or third language. Short sentences, clear verbs, and concrete nouns travel better than long, layered sentences. The generator defaults to plain English for that reason. Read the output aloud once and cut anything that only makes sense if you already know the market.

Should I mention payment in naira on my gig?

Follow the platform rules first. Fiverr and Upwork handle their own payments and payouts, and their terms do not allow you to move a client off the platform. If you are pitching directly to a Nigerian client, quoting in naira is normal and often expected. Mentioning your bank or payment method in a public gig description usually breaks the platform terms, so keep that for the invoice or the contract once a project is confirmed.

How long should a gig description be?

For Fiverr, aim for a body between 150 and 400 words. That is enough to explain the service, the deliverables, and the process without burying the buyer. For Upwork proposals, the description of your own service can run longer, but the proposal itself should be shorter and tailored to the job post. A long, generic description is one of the fastest ways to lose a buyer who is scanning three other profiles at the same time.

What does the AI actually receive when I press the button?

Only an anonymous summary of your form fields: the service, the ideal client, the deliverables, the turnaround, and the process notes. No names, emails, phone numbers, or client identifiers. Nothing is stored on the server, and the same request is not linked back to you later. If you would rather not send anything, you can still use the page as a structure for writing your own draft.

Is this tool useful outside Nigeria?

Yes. The core writing guidance applies to freelancers anywhere. The Nigerian context in the article is a starting point for readers who want to know which local platforms, regulators, and payment realities apply, but freelancers in Ghana, Kenya, South Africa, Egypt, and beyond will find the same draft structure useful. The marketplace tips on Fiverr and Upwork are global, because those platforms are global.

Does the tool store a history of what I generated?

No. Every request is independent, and closing the tab ends the session. If you want to keep a draft, copy the output while it is on the page and paste it into your own document. Nothing on our side records your service, your client notes, or your drafts.
