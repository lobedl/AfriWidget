What this converter actually does

This tool takes an amount, a source currency, and a destination currency, applies a rate, and returns the converted figure. Two rates are involved: the mid-market reference rate, which is the neutral benchmark used by financial news and by most public rate feeds, and the effective rate you would actually receive after a provider's spread. The results panel shows both, and the breakdown table makes the arithmetic visible so you can see exactly which number was used and why.

The mid-market reference rate is a useful planning number. It is what an amount is worth at the wholesale level before any bank, exchange bureau, or transfer service has taken its cut. For quick mental math, for comparing two currencies against each other, and for estimating roughly what a foreign invoice will cost in local terms, the mid-market rate is what you want. For a specific transaction, the effective rate is what matters, and that is the number the effective estimate line produces.

Everything runs in the browser. The page makes one optional network request, to a public exchange rate feed, and only when the page loads. If that request succeeds, the tool uses live reference rates. If it fails, the tool falls back to a snapshot of rates baked into the file, and the status line tells you which mode is active. Nothing about your conversion is sent anywhere, and no amount you enter is ever stored.

Where exchange rates come from

A foreign exchange rate is a price, and like any price it is set by supply and demand, mediated through thousands of trades that happen every second in a market with no single physical location. Central banks, commercial banks, hedge funds, importers, exporters, and retail travellers all participate. The rate you see quoted on a news site or a rate feed is a summary of where the market is trading at a particular moment, usually the midpoint of the highest bid and the lowest ask.

The most widely cited public references come from a handful of sources. The European Central Bank publishes a daily reference rate for the euro against roughly thirty currencies, set once each working day. The US Federal Reserve publishes its H.10 release weekly, covering the dollar against a similar set. The Bank for International Settlements runs a triennial survey that is the standard reference for total market turnover and for the relative importance of each currency pair. For African currencies, the relevant central banks, such as the Bank of Ghana and the South African Reserve Bank, publish their own daily or periodic reference rates, and those are the ones that matter for local regulation and reporting.

The feed this tool queries on load is one of several free public services that aggregates those references into a single consistent snapshot across more than one hundred and fifty currencies. The service is not the same as an executable market quote, and it is not the same as your bank's rate. It is a reasonable, widely used planning reference, and it is what a public converter should be built on. When the feed is unavailable, the fallback snapshot replaces it, and the fallback is a curated set of reference rates captured at the time the page was last updated.

The gap between mid-market and what you get

The mid-market rate is a theoretical midpoint. Nobody actually transacts at it. Every financial intermediary, from a high street bank to an airport kiosk, buys foreign currency at one price and sells it at a slightly higher price, and the difference between the two is the spread. That spread is how the intermediary is paid. It is not a scam and it is not hidden; it is simply the price of the service.

The width of the spread varies enormously by provider. An online-only remittance service such as Wise or Remitly typically works on spreads below one per cent, sometimes well below. A traditional bank wire typically embeds somewhere between one and a half and two and a half per cent, and it may add a flat transfer fee on top. A bureau de change in a busy city centre sits in the middle of that range, and an airport kiosk at the top of the range can approach four or five per cent. None of these numbers is fixed, and each provider will quote you a different effective rate on the same day for the same pair.

A provider that advertises a great rate and then adds a three per cent commission is not necessarily cheaper than a provider that advertises a mediocre rate but charges nothing extra. The only honest comparison is the bottom line, and that is what the effective estimate line on this page is for.

The African currency pairs that matter most

Four African currencies are supported because they come up most often in real remittance and cross-border commerce flows, and because they each behave differently in ways worth knowing.

The Ghanaian cedi (GHS) has historically been one of the more volatile African currencies, moving sharply with cocoa prices, gold prices, and shifts in the country's external debt position. The spread you pay on a cedi conversion can widen noticeably during periods of pressure, and the difference between the reference rate and the street rate can be meaningful during those windows. The Bank of Ghana publishes its own reference rate daily, and it is the most authoritative number for local reporting.

The Nigerian naira (NGN) went through a significant reform in mid-2023 when the central bank allowed the official rate to move closer to the parallel market rate. Even after the reform, the gap between what different channels quote remains wider than for most currencies, and the reference feed this tool uses may sit slightly apart from what an actual provider quotes on a given day. For any large transaction in naira, confirm the binding rate with the recipient's bank or the transfer service you are using.

The Kenyan shilling (KES) is the most stable of the four under normal conditions and is the dominant currency for mobile money in East Africa. Because mobile money transfers inside Kenya happen at very tight spreads, the shilling behaves more like a developed-market currency for small retail transactions. International transfers in and out still carry a wider spread, but the underlying rate is usually clean.

The South African rand (ZAR) is by far the most liquid African currency and one of the more liquid currencies globally. It trades continuously, and its price reflects commodity cycles, particularly platinum group metals and gold, plus the country's fiscal position and interest rate decisions by the South African Reserve Bank. Because the rand is heavily traded, the reference rate is tighter than for the other three, and the gap between the mid-market and what a bank charges is usually smaller.

What moves exchange rates

Exchange rates move for reasons that can be sorted into a small number of categories. Understanding those categories helps you decide whether to convert today or wait a few days, and it helps you interpret the swings you see in the feed.

Interest rate differentials. If one central bank raises its policy rate and the other does not, capital tends to flow towards the higher-yielding currency, and that currency strengthens. The US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, the Bank of Ghana, the Central Bank of Nigeria, and the South African Reserve Bank all move their rates independently, and the relative movements between them are one of the strongest drivers of major currency pairs over any period longer than a few weeks.

Inflation differentials. A country with persistently higher inflation than its trading partners sees its currency depreciate over time, because the purchasing power of that currency is falling faster. Purchasing power parity is a rough long-run anchor rather than a precise tool, but the direction is reliable. When Nigerian inflation runs meaningfully above US inflation, the naira tends to weaken against the dollar over the following months.

Trade and current account balances. A country that exports more than it imports is receiving net foreign currency, which supports its exchange rate. A country running a persistent current account deficit needs to attract foreign capital to fund it, and if that capital stops flowing, the currency usually weakens. This is why commodity prices matter so much for the currencies of exporting nations, and why the rand, the cedi, and the naira all respond quickly to moves in gold, cocoa, and oil prices.

Geopolitical and policy shocks. Elections, sovereign defaults, capital controls, and sudden shifts in monetary policy all move currencies quickly. These are the events that are hardest to forecast and that tend to produce the largest single-day moves. When they happen, the reference rate the tool uses updates on the feed's next cycle, and the fallback snapshot becomes less reliable until the page is updated.

A worked conversion

Suppose someone in Accra wants to convert one thousand US dollars into Ghanaian cedis. They open the tool, enter 1000, select USD as the source and GHS as the destination, and choose the mid-market reference spread of zero, because they want to see the neutral number first.

The tool applies the current USD to GHS rate, which we will assume is 15.50 for this example. The mid-market result is fifteen thousand five hundred cedis. The inverse rate is one divided by 15.50, which is approximately 0.0645, meaning one cedi is worth about 6.45 US cents at the mid-market reference.

Now suppose the same person looks at the bench panel to see what different providers would actually deliver. At a zero per cent spread, they receive the full fifteen thousand five hundred. At the online remittance service spread of zero point five per cent, the effective amount drops to fifteen thousand four hundred and twenty-two point five. At the bank wire spread of one point five per cent, it drops to fifteen thousand two hundred and sixty-seven point five. At the bureau de change spread of two point five per cent, it drops to fifteen thousand one hundred and twelve point five.

The gap between the mid-market and the bank wire is roughly two hundred and thirty-three cedis, which on this transaction is real money. The gap between the mid-market and the airport kiosk, which is not shown in the bench panel but would sit around four per cent, would be closer to six hundred and twenty cedis. That is the practical cost of convenience, and it is the number worth knowing before you decide where to convert.

Common mistakes with currency conversion

A handful of problems come up again and again when people try to work out what a foreign amount is really worth.

Comparing the headline rate instead of the bottom line. A provider that advertises a rate of fifteen point six and charges a flat twenty-dollar fee is not necessarily cheaper than a provider that advertises fifteen point four with no fee. On a thousand-dollar transfer, the first pays fourteen thousand six hundred and the second pays fifteen thousand four hundred, so the second wins even though the rate looks worse. Always compare what the recipient actually receives.

Forgetting the fixed fee. On small transfers, a flat fee dominates the spread. Sending fifty dollars through a service that charges five dollars flat loses ten per cent before the exchange rate even matters. On small amounts, look for services that charge a percentage rather than a flat fee, or batch small transfers into larger ones when the timing allows.

Using yesterday's rate for today's decision. A feed that has not refreshed since yesterday morning is fine for planning but not for a decision you are about to make. Check the rate source line on the tool, and if it says fallback, remember that the fallback may be several days or weeks old. For a live decision, check the rate again on a page that has re-fetched the feed, or confirm the binding rate with the provider.

Assuming the mid-market rate is achievable. It is not. It is the theoretical midpoint. Any real transaction will sit slightly worse than mid-market, and the only question is how much worse. A provider that quotes exactly mid-market is either absorbing the cost somewhere else or building it into a fee you are not seeing.

Ignoring the parallel market in markets where it exists. In countries where the official rate is managed, the street rate can be materially different. Using this tool for a decision that will actually be executed on the street in such a country is a mistake, because the tool uses the international reference and not the local street price.

Converting more than once unnecessarily. If you are moving money between two currencies that both have thin liquidity, converting through a third currency such as USD twice can cost more than converting directly. This tool routes every pair through a common base internally, which is fine for a reference estimate, but for a real transaction, look for a direct pair.

Where this tool stops

This is a planning calculator. It uses a public reference feed and a baked-in fallback snapshot, and it applies a mid-market or spread-adjusted rate to an amount. It does not know what any specific provider will quote you on a specific day, and it does not model flat fees, card surcharges, or cross-currency routing costs. For a large transaction, the number to rely on is the one your provider actually quotes on the confirmation screen before you authorise the transfer.

It also does not handle tax. Some jurisdictions charge a foreign exchange transaction tax, a stamp duty, or a withholding tax on certain cross-border flows. Those taxes are separate from the exchange rate and are not modelled here. If a transaction involves tax, work with a tax adviser or use the specific tax calculator provided by the jurisdiction in question.

What the tool gives you is a fast, private, transparent estimate, with the mid-market reference and the spread-adjusted effective number both visible so you can see what each component contributes. That is enough for planning, for a first sanity check on a quote, and for understanding roughly how much a provider's spread is really costing you. It is not a substitute for the binding rate on your actual transaction, and it is not a formal accounting record.

Frequently asked questions

Are the exchange rates on this page the same as what my bank will charge me?

No. The rate shown here is a mid-market reference rate, which is the midpoint between the buy and sell prices at the interbank level. Banks, credit card issuers, airport kiosks, and money transfer operators all add a spread or a flat fee on top of that reference, usually somewhere between one and five per cent. The result is that the amount you actually receive is almost always slightly lower than the mid-market conversion.

Does the converter use live rates?

It tries to. On page load the tool fetches a public reference feed that covers more than one hundred and fifty currencies, including the twelve supported here. When the fetch succeeds, the status line shows that live reference rates are loaded. When the fetch fails, whether because of a slow connection, an ad blocker, or a temporary outage at the provider, the tool falls back to a baked-in snapshot of recent rates so the calculator keeps working.

Why do some currencies look approximate?

Because a mid-market reference rate is an average across a market where different participants trade at slightly different prices. For widely traded pairs such as USD to EUR or GBP to USD, the reference is tight. For thinner pairs involving a currency that trades mostly onshore, such as NGN or GHS, the reference is a best-effort estimate and the real tradable price can move more during the day.

What is a spread and why does it matter?

The spread is the difference between the rate a provider buys at and the rate it sells at, or the fee it embeds in the transaction. It is how banks and exchanges make money on foreign exchange. A typical bank wire embeds somewhere around one and a half to two per cent. A bureau de change at an airport can embed four or five. A specialised remittance service can sit below one. The wider the spread, the less of your money arrives on the other end.

What is the difference between an official rate and a parallel market rate?

In some countries where foreign currency is scarce or where the central bank manages the exchange rate closely, an official rate published by the central bank can sit meaningfully apart from the rate you would actually get on the street or from an informal dealer. This tool uses the international reference feed, which generally tracks the more market-driven rate. For a specific transaction, confirm the binding rate with the provider you are actually using.

Which currencies are supported?

Twelve currencies are supported: the US dollar, the euro, the British pound sterling, the Canadian dollar, the Australian dollar, the Indian rupee, the Japanese yen, the UAE dirham, the Ghanaian cedi, the Nigerian naira, the Kenyan shilling, and the South African rand. Every pair among these twelve can be converted, because the tool routes the calculation through a common base currency rather than storing hundreds of direct pairs.

Can I use this converter for accounting or tax purposes?

It is a planning and estimation tool, not an accounting record. For tax filings, audited financial statements, or any figure that has to stand up to scrutiny, use the closing rate published by the relevant central bank or tax authority on the specific date that applies, and keep the source documented. The reference rate here is accurate enough for day-to-day planning and not precise enough for a formal filing.

Why does the same amount convert differently between two apps on my phone?

Because each app embeds a different spread and updates its rate at a different moment. A bank app that shows a rate from this morning and adds a two per cent markup will quote a different number from a remittance app that refreshes every fifteen minutes and adds half a per cent. Neither is wrong. They are simply pricing the same underlying market differently, and the only way to know which is cheaper for your specific transfer is to compare the total amount the recipient receives.

Does the tool send my conversion anywhere?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. The only outbound request the page makes is the optional fetch to the public exchange rate feed on page load. If you press the AI insight button, a small anonymous summary of the currency pair, the rounded amount, the rounded converted amount, the rate source, and the spread percentage is sent to a serverless function that runs once and then stops. No name, no email, no account number, and no history.

Is the tool usable offline?

Yes. Because the fallback rates are baked into the page, the converter continues to work without a network connection once the page itself has loaded. The rates will be the ones recorded at the time the page was last updated by the developer, not the current market, so treat the offline result as a rough planning estimate rather than a live quote.

How often should the fallback rates be updated?

A quarterly refresh keeps the fallback within a reasonable band for most pairs. For currencies that move sharply, such as the Nigerian naira during a period of reform or the Ghanaian cedi during periods of pressure, the fallback can drift further in a shorter window. When the live feed is working, the fallback values do not affect the output, so the refresh cadence mainly matters for users who are offline or blocking the fetch.
