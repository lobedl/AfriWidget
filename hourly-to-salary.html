About This Hourly-to-Salary Converter

This converter translates an hourly rate into annual, monthly, weekly, and daily income estimates. It is especially useful for freelancers, contractors, part-time workers, and employees comparing a contract offer with a salaried role. The result depends on working hours, weeks worked, and the share of time that can actually be billed, so it is a planning comparison rather than a guaranteed income statement.

How to Use the Converter

Enter your hourly rate, hours per week, weeks worked per year, and expected billable utilisation. Add benefits or expenses when the form provides those options, then review the annual equivalent and the utilisation scenarios. Run a second calculation with a lower utilisation rate to see how admin, sales, leave, and unpaid gaps affect the result.

Example: Comparing Two Work Patterns

At $25 per hour for 40 hours across 52 weeks, the theoretical gross equivalent is $52,000 before taxes and expenses. At 75% utilisation, only about three quarters of those hours are billable, so the practical freelance estimate is lower. This comparison shows why an hourly rate should account for non-billable work and benefits before it is compared with a salary.

How to Calculate Your True Freelance Income

Converting an hourly rate to an annual equivalent sounds simple — multiply by 2,080 hours (40 hrs × 52 weeks) and you're done. But for freelancers and independent contractors, this calculation significantly overstates real income because it assumes 100% of all available hours are billable. In practice, time spent on administration, marketing, proposals, and business development is unpaid — and it can easily consume 20–30% of your working time.

The Freelancer's True Cost of Downtime

Every hour a salaried employee spends in a team meeting, on training, or taking a sick day, they still get paid. A freelancer in the same scenario earns nothing. This is why freelance and contractor rates are — and should be — higher than equivalent salaried rates. A common rule of thumb is to multiply your desired equivalent salary by 1.5 to 2x to arrive at a fair freelance rate that accounts for downtime, self-employment taxes, benefits, and the risk premium of variable income.

Freelance Rate vs. Salary: What Benefits Are You Leaving Behind?

When comparing a freelance contract to a salaried offer, remember that a salary often comes packaged with significant non-cash compensation: employer pension contributions (often 5–15% of salary), health insurance premiums (potentially $5,000–$20,000/year in value), paid annual leave (typically 15–25 days), sick pay, professional development budgets, equipment, and software. If your freelance rate's annual equivalent doesn't exceed the total compensation package value — not just the base salary — the salaried role may be the better financial deal.

How to Set Your Hourly Rate as a New Freelancer

If you're setting a rate for the first time, start by calculating your minimum viable rate: divide your required annual income (including business costs, taxes, and savings goals) by your expected billable hours. Add a margin for growth and profit. Research market rates for your skill set and region using platforms like Upwork, Toptal, or local job boards. Your rate should be anchored to the value you deliver, not just your time — as your skills and reputation grow, your rate should reflect that progression.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the annual figure include taxes?

No. The converter shows a gross planning equivalent before income tax, business expenses, insurance, pension contributions, and other deductions. Add those costs when comparing the result with a salaried offer.

Why is utilisation important?

A freelancer may spend part of the week on proposals, administration, sales, training, or leave. Utilisation estimates the share of working time that produces billable income, so it makes the comparison more realistic.

Can I use this for part-time work?

Yes. Enter the hours and weeks you actually expect to work. The result will reflect that schedule rather than assuming a full-time year.
