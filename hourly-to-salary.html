What this converter actually measures

The hourly to salary calculator takes a rate you are paid per hour and turns it into the equivalent income across a day, a week, a month, and a year. It is a straightforward piece of arithmetic on the surface, but the number that matters most is not the annual figure itself. The number that matters is what the annual figure represents once you adjust for unpaid time, working weeks, and billable utilisation.

The tool exists because those three adjustments are the difference between a flattering figure and a useful one. A freelancer charging fifty an hour often hears that this works out to over a hundred thousand a year, and on a forty hour, fifty two week, fully billable schedule, it does. But very few freelancers work forty billable hours a week for fifty two weeks. Proposals, invoicing, marketing, admin, sick days, and holidays all sit outside the hours that a client pays for. Once those are accounted for, the same fifty an hour frequently lands between seventy and eighty five thousand. Both numbers come from the same rate. Only one of them is honest about what the freelancer actually earns.

For a salaried employee, the conversion is simpler. If your contract states that you work thirty five or forty hours a week and you are paid every week of the year including annual leave, the annual figure is close to a direct multiplication. But even here, the tool is useful when you are comparing a salary offer against a contract rate, or working out what a side project at an hourly rate would really add to your income at the end of the year.

The formula, in plain words

The calculator combines four numbers to produce the annual figure. Your hourly rate is multiplied by your hours per day, then by your days per week, then by your weeks per year, and finally by your billable utilisation. Nothing else is involved. Every other figure on the results panel — the daily, weekly, monthly, and effective hourly amounts — is a division or a smaller multiplication of the same input set.

Hours per day times days per week gives you the hours you are nominally at work in a week. Multiplying that by weeks per year gives the total hours you are nominally at work in a year. On a standard full-time schedule of eight hours a day, five days a week, fifty two weeks a year, that total is 2,080 hours. That figure, often called the standard annual hours, is where the well known shorthand of "just multiply your rate by 2,080" comes from.

Multiplying the annual hours by your billable utilisation reduces the figure to the hours you actually get paid for. If your utilisation is seventy five per cent, then only 1,560 of those 2,080 hours are billable, and the annual income is your rate multiplied by 1,560 rather than 2,080. That is the adjustment that turns a nominal figure into a realistic one. The effective hourly rate shown on the results panel is the annual income divided by the full annual hours, which is the rate you are actually earning per hour of your working life, including the unpaid time.

A useful sanity check. If your effective hourly rate is much lower than the rate you quote clients, that gap is the value of your non-billable time. For a freelancer with a seventy per cent utilisation, the gap is nearly a third of the notional rate. It is worth knowing this number before you decide whether to raise your rate or improve your working process.

Every input explained

There are five inputs on this page, and each one changes the output in a specific way. Understanding what each does makes the result easier to interpret and the figures easier to adjust.

Hourly rate. The gross amount paid for one hour of work, before tax, pension, and any business expenses. If you are a salaried employee and you do not know your hourly equivalent, divide your annual salary by 2,080 for a forty hour week or by 1,820 for a thirty five hour week to get a working figure. If you are a contractor, use the rate you quote clients, not the rate after fees or expenses.

Hours per day. The number of hours you are contracted to work or actually work in a day. Full time is usually eight hours, but many countries use a shorter or longer standard. Some companies work a nine day fortnight, in which case nine hours a day across nine working days out of every ten produces the same annual total as eight hours across ten days. Enter what your own schedule actually is rather than a textbook number.

Days per week. The number of working days in a normal week. Full time is usually five. Part time can be two, three, or four. Contractors who work six days a week during a busy season and four days a week in a quiet one may want to run two separate calculations rather than trying to average the two.

Weeks per year. The number of weeks in the year you actually earn money. Salaried employees on a full year contract with paid leave enter fifty two, because leave is paid. A contractor who takes four weeks of unpaid holiday a year enters forty eight. A seasonal worker who only works nine months out of twelve enters thirty nine. Using a lower figure correctly reduces the annual income rather than pretending that unpaid time away from work is somehow compensated.

Billable utilisation. The percentage of your working hours that produce paid work. For a salaried employee, use one hundred, because every hour on the clock is compensated under the salary. For a freelancer or contractor, use a realistic figure between sixty and eighty five per cent. The tool runs scenarios at ninety, eighty, seventy, sixty, and fifty per cent automatically, so you can see the effect without running multiple calculations.

A worked example for a full-time employee

Take a full-time employee on a rate of twenty five an hour, working eight hours a day, five days a week, for fifty two weeks a year, with full paid leave and no unpaid time off. Utilisation is one hundred per cent because the salary covers every working hour, including paid leave. The annual hours are eight multiplied by five multiplied by fifty two, which is 2,080. Multiply by twenty five and the annual gross income is 52,000.

The daily figure is twenty five times eight, which is 200. The weekly figure is 200 times five, which is 1,000. The monthly figure is 52,000 divided by twelve, which is about 4,333. The effective hourly rate is the same twenty five, because every hour is paid at that rate. All of these numbers are consistent with the standard 2,080 hour shorthand, and they are what the salary offer would have to match to be equivalent on gross pay.

Now suppose the same employee takes two weeks of unpaid leave a year on top of the paid leave, which happens in some contract arrangements. The weeks per year drop from fifty two to fifty. The annual hours fall to 2,000, and the annual figure falls to 50,000. The monthly figure falls to about 4,167. Nothing else changes, and the reduction is exactly what the two unpaid weeks represent. That is why the weeks per year field is there. Using fifty two when you actually work fifty overstates the annual income by roughly four per cent, which is a meaningful amount once tax and household expenses are applied.

A worked example for a freelancer

Now take a freelancer charging fifty an hour, working eight hours a day and five days a week, but taking four weeks of unpaid holiday a year and running a realistic utilisation of seventy five per cent. The weeks per year field is forty eight. The annual hours on the schedule are eight times five times forty eight, which is 1,920. Of those, seventy five per cent are billable, which gives 1,440 billable hours. Multiply by fifty and the annual income is 72,000.

At first glance, that looks strong compared to the employee on 52,000. But the comparison changes once you factor in what the freelancer has to pay for out of that 72,000. The freelancer has no paid holiday, no sick pay, no employer pension contribution, no health insurance subsidy, and often has to fund their own software, equipment, continuing education, and professional indemnity cover. In many markets the value of those benefits to an employee is between twenty and thirty five per cent of salary, which means the employee on 52,000 may be closer to 65,000 to 70,000 in total compensation than the base figure suggests.

The freelancer's effective hourly rate, shown on the results panel, is 72,000 divided by 1,920, which is 37.50 an hour. That is the rate they are actually earning per hour of working life, even though clients are paying fifty. The gap exists because a quarter of their working time is unpaid admin and marketing. It is not a moral failing. It is the structural reality of self-employment, and it is why the rate a freelancer quotes needs to be higher than the rate an employee is paid.

Why freelancers usually need a higher rate

The single most common mistake freelancers make when they first go independent is to quote the same hourly rate they earned as an employee. It feels fair, since the work is the same and the skills are the same. In practice it leaves them substantially worse off, because the entire package of benefits and the unpaid admin time have to fit inside the rate. The rule of thumb that independent contractors should charge between 1.5 and 2 times the equivalent employee rate exists precisely to close that gap.

Four items sit inside that multiplier. The first is billable utilisation, which as the previous section showed reduces the effective rate below the quoted one. The second is self-employment tax, which in many countries is higher than the employee rate because the employer's contribution becomes the contractor's responsibility. The third is the direct value of the benefits the employee receives and the contractor does not, including holiday pay, sick pay, pension, health cover, and paid training. The fourth is a risk premium, because variable income, an irregular pipeline, and no notice period all carry a cost that a fixed salary does not.

The exact multiplier depends on where you live, what sector you work in, and how stable your pipeline is. A contractor with a long term retainer at forty billable hours a week may only need a small uplift. A freelancer taking small jobs with unpredictable gaps between them may need the full two times uplift to end up in the same position. Running the numbers through this calculator at two or three different utilisation figures is a quick way to see how sensitive your real income is to the amount of non-billable time you carry.

The benefits you leave behind when you go independent

When comparing an hourly rate against a salaried offer, the base salary is the least interesting part of the comparison. The benefits package is usually where the decision is actually won or lost, and it is almost never visible on a job advertisement headline. Four items deserve particular attention.

Pension or retirement contributions. Employers in most countries make mandatory contributions on top of salary, and many offer a matching scheme as well. In the United Kingdom the employer contribution is typically three per cent minimum and often five or more. In Australia it is eleven and a half per cent. In Canada and the United States the picture varies but the contributions can be substantial. When you are self-employed, that entire amount comes out of your rate, so the equivalent value needs to be added back when comparing.

Health insurance and medical cover. In countries without universal healthcare, employer health insurance can be worth between five and twenty thousand dollars a year to the employee. A freelancer buying equivalent cover privately pays the full premium out of pocket, and the premium is not usually deductible at the same rate.

Paid leave and sick pay. A salaried employee with twenty five days of paid annual leave and ten days of paid sick pay receives thirty five paid days off a year on top of public holidays. A freelancer taking the same days off simply does not earn on them. On a 200 a day rate, that is 7,000 a year of foregone income compared to the salaried equivalent.

Equipment, software, and professional development. Employers typically fund laptops, software licenses, training courses, and conference attendance. A freelancer pays for all of these from the same revenue that has to cover their own living expenses. The amounts vary widely by profession, but for a designer, developer, or consultant, the annual figure is often several thousand dollars.

None of this means freelancing is the wrong choice. It means the comparison has to be done on the total value of each arrangement, not on the headline rate. A 55,000 salary with a good benefits package can be worth more than a 70,000 freelance annual equivalent on a seventy per cent utilisation, once the benefits gap is properly accounted for.

Common mistakes when comparing rates and salaries

Seven mistakes show up repeatedly when people compare hourly rates and salaries, and each one is easy to avoid once you know it is there.

Using fifty two weeks for a freelancer. Paid holiday is a benefit that only applies to employees. A freelancer who takes four weeks off and uses fifty two in the calculation is overstating their annual income by nearly eight per cent. Use the weeks you actually invoice for.

Assuming one hundred per cent utilisation. Nobody bills every hour of the working day. Even a contracted consultant with a full time retainer spends time on invoicing, planning, and internal meetings that no client pays for. A realistic utilisation figure gives a more useful answer than a flattering one.

Forgetting self-employment costs. The annual figure this calculator returns is gross. Self-employment tax, professional insurance, accounting software, and equipment purchases all come out of it before anything reaches your bank account. The salaried comparison does not have those deductions in the same way, which makes the freelance figure look better than it is.

Comparing gross to net. If you have a salaried offer quoted after tax and an hourly rate quoted before tax, the comparison is meaningless until both are on the same basis. Convert to gross or to net, but do not mix them.

Ignoring overtime and penalty rates. The calculator assumes a flat rate for all hours. If your job pays penalty rates for weekends, evenings, or public holidays, the annual figure here understates your real income. Run a separate calculation for those hours at their higher rate and add it to the base.

Treating the annual figure as take-home pay. Every number on this page is gross. Tax, national insurance or social security, and pension contributions all reduce what you actually keep. In most countries an income tax and social security burden of twenty to forty per cent is normal at middle incomes, and the figure varies significantly by country.

Not running the numbers twice. The comparison becomes clearer if you run the calculator twice, once with a conservative utilisation figure and once with an optimistic one. If the outcome flips between the two, the decision is genuinely close and the difference is not large enough to be worth agonising over. If the outcome is the same on both, the decision is more obvious than it first looked.

Who this tool is for, and where it stops

This calculator is for anyone who needs to translate between an hourly rate and an annual income, or between a salaried offer and an independent contract. That includes employees checking whether a contract role is genuinely more lucrative than their current salary, freelancers setting rates for the first time, part-time workers wanting to see their annual equivalent, and contractors comparing a long term retainer against project work at a higher rate.

It is also useful for anyone who has been quoted an annual figure and wants to check what it works out to per hour, especially when comparing offers that quote differently. A salary quoted as "75,000 a year" and a contract quoted as "45 an hour" look far apart until both are converted to the same basis. Converting both through this tool is often the fastest way to see which one is actually higher.

The tool stops short of anything that requires personal tax knowledge. It does not know your marginal tax rate, your country's social security rules, your pension contribution obligations, or your deductible business expenses. Those all sit outside the calculation and have to be worked out separately, usually with the help of an accountant or a tax authority's own calculators. What this page does is give you a clean gross comparison between two ways of being paid. Everything past that comparison is where professional advice becomes worth the cost.

Frequently asked questions

How is an hourly rate converted to an annual salary?

The basic conversion multiplies your hourly rate by the number of paid hours in a year. A full-time employee working eight hours a day, five days a week, for fifty-two weeks works 2,080 hours, so the annual gross figure is simply the hourly rate multiplied by 2,080. The calculator adjusts that base for your own hours per day, days per week, weeks per year, and billable utilisation, which is why the annual figure here can differ from a simple multiplication.

Does the annual figure include tax or superannuation?

No. The figures on this page are gross amounts before income tax, social security, pension contributions, and any business expenses. What you actually take home depends on where you live and what deductions apply to you. If you are comparing an hourly contract against a salaried offer, run your own tax situation through both scenarios before deciding, because the difference often narrows once tax is applied.

What is billable utilisation and why does it matter?

Billable utilisation is the share of your working hours that you can actually charge to a client. Freelancers and contractors spend a large portion of their week on proposals, invoicing, marketing, training, and admin work that produces no direct income. A utilisation rate of 75 percent means only three quarters of your paid time is billable. Lowering the utilisation figure in the calculator gives a more honest picture of real annual income than assuming 100 percent.

Why do freelancers usually need a higher hourly rate than employees?

Because a freelancer has to cover costs an employee never sees. Paid annual leave, sick pay, employer pension contributions, health insurance, software licenses, equipment, and the unpaid admin time all have to fit inside the freelance rate. A common rule of thumb is that an independent contractor needs between 1.5 and 2 times the equivalent salaried hourly rate to end up with the same take-home position over a year. The exact multiplier depends on the country, the sector, and how much of the work is billable.

Should I use 52 weeks or 50 weeks for the calculation?

Use 52 weeks if you are paid for every week of the year, including annual leave. Use a lower number if you take unpaid time off, work seasonally, or are a contractor who only invoices for weeks actually worked. Two weeks of unpaid leave lowers the annual figure by roughly four percent on a full-time schedule, which is worth capturing rather than ignoring.

How does the salary comparison section work?

If you enter an annual salary offer, the calculator subtracts it from the annual equivalent of your hourly rate and shows the difference in both directions. A positive difference means your hourly rate produces more annual income on paper. A negative difference means the salary pays more. Neither result is a recommendation, because the salaried role may also carry paid leave, insurance, pension, and paid training that the calculator cannot value for you.

Can I use this for part-time work?

Yes. Change the hours per day and days per week to match the schedule you actually work. A part-time employee on three days a week enters three in the days field and the annual figure will reflect that reduced schedule. Contractors with irregular weeks can set the weeks per year field to their realistic annual total, or run two calculations and compare.

Does the tool account for overtime or penalty rates?

No. The calculator assumes a single flat hourly rate for every hour worked. If you receive overtime at 1.5 times or 2 times your base rate, or penalty rates for weekends and public holidays, the annual figure here will understate your real income. In that case, run the calculator once for your base hours and once for the overtime hours at their higher rate, then add the two results.

Why is the effective hourly rate different from what I entered?

Because a utilisation rate below 100 percent spreads your rate across non-billable time. If you enter a 50 dollar rate at 75 percent utilisation, the effective hourly rate across all your working hours is closer to 37.50, even though the client still pays 50. That lower figure is what you are actually earning per hour of your life spent working, and it is the number worth comparing against a salaried equivalent.

Does this tool store my figures?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the hourly rate, hours, weeks, utilisation, and computed equivalents is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. No name, no email, no device identifier, and no history.
