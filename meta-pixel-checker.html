What this checker measures

This tool takes a proposed meta title, a proposed meta description, and an optional URL, and reports three things: the approximate pixel width of the title, the approximate pixel width of the description, and a Google-style preview of how the result may appear on a desktop search engine results page. It also flags the likely truncation point when a field exceeds the practical display target.

The measurement runs on a canvas element inside your browser. Each character is drawn at the font size Google uses for the corresponding element, so a string of wide letters such as W and M reports a larger width than a string of narrow letters such as I and L with the same character count. This is why the tool gives a number in pixels as well as a number in characters, and why the two numbers do not always agree.

Everything you type stays in your browser. The canvas measurement is a local operation, no field is transmitted anywhere, and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the title text, description text, pixel widths, and status label is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

Why pixels matter more than character count

The older advice to keep titles under sixty characters and descriptions under one hundred and sixty characters came from an era when font rendering was more predictable. It remains a useful quick check, but it is not accurate for the way modern search results render. Consider two titles of exactly fifty-eight characters. The first uses mostly narrow letters and lowercase text. The second packs in three capital W characters and two long words. The first may fit comfortably within the visible result. The second may already be on the edge of truncation.

Google itself does not publish a fixed character limit for titles or descriptions. It renders each result inside its own layout, and the available width changes with device, browser, font, and whether the result includes extra elements such as sitelinks, ratings, or dates. Google also rewrites titles and descriptions when a different part of the page better matches the query, so no title is guaranteed to display exactly as written.

The practical consequence is that pixel width gives a closer match to reality than character count alone. It is not perfect, because the exact rendering depends on the visitor's device, but it is a much better signal than a raw number of letters.

Use both numbers together. Character count gives you a fast editorial check, and pixel width tells you how the string actually renders. When the two disagree, trust the pixel width and put the most important words at the front so they survive any shortening.

Where the 600px and 960px targets come from

The 600-pixel title target and the 960-pixel description target are widely used working numbers in the SEO community rather than official limits published by Google. They originate from measurements of Google's desktop results page around 2017 to 2018, when the current title and description font sizes were finalised. Google has since adjusted the layout several times, but the two numbers have remained a useful approximation.

Mobile is a different story. A mobile screen displays roughly the same text in a narrower column, so Google wraps the title onto more lines and shows less of the description in a single snippet. Mobile is why the first five to seven words of a title matter so much: on a small screen, those are the words that survive.

How to write a title that survives truncation

A title that survives truncation on every device follows a few simple rules. The first is to put the main subject of the page at the start. If the page is a BMI calculator, the word BMI belongs in the first two words, not in the middle of the string. If the page is a guide to WASSCE registration, the words WASSCE registration belong near the start.

The second rule is to avoid keyword stuffing. A title that repeats the same word three times in the hope of a ranking boost reads badly to human visitors and is more likely to be rewritten by Google. A title that reads naturally and says what the page is about is more likely to be displayed as written.

The third rule is to include one value or differentiator, not five. A title such as Free BMI Calculator with BMR and TDEE Estimates fits comfortably within the working target and tells the visitor what they get. A title such as The Best Free Online BMI Calculator for Everyone, Completely Accurate and Easy to Use in 2026 pushes well past the target and will almost certainly be shortened.

How to write a description that earns the click

The description is not a ranking factor, but it does influence whether a searcher clicks on your result instead of the one below it. A description that answers the question the visitor is asking in the first sentence, and then adds one supporting detail, will perform better than one that fills the space with keywords or promises.

Treat the first sentence as the description's real estate. Google may pull a different sentence from the page if it better matches the query, but when the description is displayed, the first sentence is what the searcher reads first. Put the subject and the value there. Use the second sentence for a supporting detail such as the tool being free, private, or in-browser. Skip adjectives that do not carry information: cutting-edge, world-class, best-in-class, and innovative all consume pixels without adding value.

If you work in a regulated domain, add a compliance-safe qualifier such as for information only, for planning purposes, or not medical advice. This is a small habit that protects the page as well as the visitor.

A worked example

Consider a page about a savings goal calculator. A first draft title might read: The Best Free Online Savings Goal Calculator for Everyone in 2026, Completely Accurate and Easy to Use. This string is 102 characters, well past the sixty-character editorial target, and its pixel width at 20px is well over one thousand. Google will shorten it.

A revised title reads: Savings Goal Calculator – Plan Monthly Targets – AfriWidget. This string is 61 characters and its pixel width at 20px lands comfortably inside the working target. The main subject sits at the front, one value follows, and the brand name appears at the end where shortening cannot hurt.

Now consider the description. A first draft might read: Our free, fast, private, in-browser savings goal calculator helps you work out exactly how much you need to put away each month to hit your target on time, with no signup and no data storage, and no ads before the calculator loads. This string is over 250 characters and its pixel width well over the working target. Google may still show it, but the ending will be replaced with an ellipsis in most layouts.

A revised description reads: Work out how much to save each month to hit a target on time. Free, private, and running entirely in your browser. This string is 118 characters and lands well inside the description target. It answers the visitor's question in the first sentence and adds a differentiator in the second. Nothing about it makes a promise the page cannot keep.

Common mistakes

Counting characters only. Character count is a rough guide, not the whole picture. Two strings with the same character count can render at very different widths, and it is the width that determines what the visitor sees.

Assuming Google will display the exact title you wrote. Google rewrites titles when the original is too long, repetitive, or unclear, and it rewrites descriptions to match the query. Use the preview as a guide, not a promise.

Front-loading the brand name. The brand name at the start of the title consumes premium pixels and pushes the subject further down the string. Put the brand name at the end, where it is the first thing shortened and the last thing a visitor needs to see.

Filling the description with keywords. A description is read by humans, not by the ranking algorithm. Keyword stuffing makes the result look spammy and reduces click-through rate.

Using the same title across multiple pages. Duplicate titles create confusion for Google and for visitors, and they reduce the chance of any of the pages being displayed with the title you wrote.

Ignoring mobile. A title that fits the 600-pixel desktop target may still be shortened on a small screen. Front-load the subject so that even a mobile snippet communicates the point.

Where this tool stops

This checker measures approximate pixel width in a common sans-serif font at the font sizes Google uses for titles and descriptions. It does not know the exact font Google will use on a specific device, the exact width of the column the result will render in, or whether Google will rewrite the title or description in response to a specific query. It also does not check duplicate titles across your site, internal link structure, canonical tags, or any other ranking factor.

What it does give you is a fast, transparent, private way to check that your title and description will fit the working display targets on a desktop result before you publish them. Used together with the character count, the preview, and the front-loaded subject rule, it is a practical writing aid and a useful review step in any content release.

Frequently asked questions

What is a meta title and description pixel checker?

A pixel checker measures how wide your proposed title and description would render on a search engine results page, rather than counting characters alone. Letters have different widths, so a title full of I and L characters fits far more text into the same space than a title full of W and M characters. Measuring in pixels gives a closer match to what Google actually displays, which is why this tool renders your text on a canvas at title and description font sizes and reports the approximate width in pixels.

What is the ideal pixel width for a Google title?

Most SEO teams work to an approximate desktop target of 600 pixels for a title, which is usually between 55 and 60 characters depending on the width of the letters. Google does not publish a fixed limit, and the display width changes with device, font rendering, and whether the result includes extra features. Treat 600 pixels as a practical working ceiling rather than a hard boundary, and put the most important words at the start of the title so they survive any shortening.

What is the ideal pixel width for a meta description?

Most SEO teams work to an approximate desktop target of 960 pixels for a description, which is usually around 150 to 160 characters depending on letter widths and how Google wraps the text. Descriptions are also rewritten often, because Google may pull a different sentence from the page if it better matches the query. Treat the pixel target as a writing guide for a clean first sentence, not as a length that guarantees the full text will be shown.

Why does Google sometimes rewrite my title?

Google rewrites title links when the original title is too long, repetitive, or unclear, when it does not match the search query well, or when another heading or link on the page appears to describe the page more accurately. Google also rewrites when a title looks like keyword stuffing or when the page is missing a title entirely. Keeping the title concise, unique, and closely related to the page content reduces the chance of a rewrite, though it cannot eliminate it.

Do meta descriptions affect rankings?

Google has said for years that meta descriptions are not a direct ranking factor. They matter for a different reason: a clear, accurate description can improve the click-through rate of a result by telling searchers what to expect. A higher click-through rate can have an indirect effect on how a page performs over time, but the description itself is not scored in the ranking algorithm the way titles and page content are.

Should I always keep my title under 60 characters?

Character count is a quick sanity check, not a hard rule. Pixel width and the position of the most important words matter more than the raw character count. A 58-character title with narrow letters may fit comfortably within the display, while a 52-character title with wide letters and two long words may push past the visible limit. Use both measures together, and always front-load the main subject of the page.

Do mobile results show fewer pixels than desktop?

Yes. Mobile screens are narrower, so Google wraps the title onto more lines and often shows less of the description in a single snippet. The working targets of 600 pixels for the title and 960 pixels for the description describe a desktop layout. On mobile, put the most important words in the first five to seven words of the title and the first sentence of the description, so that a shortened snippet still communicates the point of the page.

Is my title and description text sent to a server?

No. The pixel measurement runs on a canvas element inside your browser tab, and the text you type never leaves the page unless you press the AI insight button. If you do press that button, only an anonymous summary of the tool name, the title text, the description text, the pixel widths, and the status label is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. No account, no email, and no history.

Can I use this checker for Bing or other search engines?

Yes, with a caveat. The pixel measurement itself is engine-agnostic; it simply reports how wide your text would render at a given font size. The 600-pixel and 960-pixel targets are drawn from common practice on Google desktop results, and Bing, DuckDuckGo, and other engines render their own layouts with their own font sizes. Use this tool as a general writing aid, and check each engine's own preview tool if you publish widely.

Why do different pixel checkers give different results?

Because they use different fonts, different font sizes, and different measurement methods. Some tools measure at 16 pixels across the board, some measure at the specific font Google uses for titles, and some measure the width of characters in a monospaced font. This tool measures titles at 20 pixels and descriptions at 14 pixels in a common sans-serif font, which is close to what Google uses on desktop results. Small differences between tools are expected, and the relative comparison between two of your own drafts is more useful than the absolute number.
