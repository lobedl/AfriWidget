What this calculator does

This tool takes the raw marks you scored on each A-Level examination paper, combines them using the component weights set out in the exam board specification, and returns the running weighted percentage and the letter grade that percentage currently corresponds to. If you have not yet sat every paper, it also works out what average percentage you would need on the remaining papers to reach a chosen target grade, so you can see before the next exam what a realistic goal looks like.

There is a second mode that totals a three or four-subject A-Level profile and converts the grade combination into UCAS Tariff points. That view is useful when you are comparing your current grades against the typical entry requirements for a university programme, or when you want to understand what a change of one grade in a single subject does to the overall tariff total.

Every calculation runs inside your browser. No mark, no subject name, and no personal detail is uploaded. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the mode, the number of completed components, the weighted percentage, the current grade band, and the target grade is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

Linear UK A-Levels and modular international A-Levels

A-Level qualifications come in two architectural forms, and the way marks are combined differs between them. Understanding which form your qualification follows matters because the forecast and the A* rule change accordingly.

Linear A-Levels are the domestic UK specifications regulated by Ofqual and offered by AQA, Pearson Edexcel, OCR, and WJEC Eduqas. Under the linear structure, all examination papers and any non-examined assessment are sat at the end of Year 13. There is no internal assessment that carries forward from Year 12. The standalone AS-Level taken at the end of Year 12 is a separate qualification and does not contribute marks to the full A-Level. The final subject grade is calculated by summing the weighted contributions of the individual papers, comparing the total to the grade boundaries set for that year, and awarding the letter grade that the total falls into.

Modular A-Levels are the international specifications from Cambridge Assessment International Education and from Pearson Edexcel International. Under the modular structure, the qualification is split into AS units and A2 units. The AS units are typically sat at the end of Year 12 and carry fifty per cent of the final mark, and the A2 units are sat at the end of Year 13 and carry the other fifty per cent. Raw marks from each unit are converted to a uniform mark scale using the grade boundaries for that series, and the UMS totals are aggregated to produce the final grade. The uniform mark scale produces fixed thresholds that do not change between series: an A is eighty per cent of the total UMS, a B is seventy per cent, and so on.

The practical consequence of the difference is that a modular candidate can carry a strong AS mark forward into the A2 year, while a linear candidate has a single set of terminal examinations at the end of Year 13 and no earlier mark to lean on. The forecast calculation in this tool applies equally to both, but the interpretation of the result differs slightly because a modular candidate's running percentage combines unit marks from two assessment points and a linear candidate's running percentage combines terminal papers that are all sat in the same series.

How paper weighting and the composite mark work

The single subject mode of this calculator uses the following method. Each paper or coursework component has a weight, expressed as a percentage of the total subject mark, that the exam board publishes in the specification. For each completed component, the weighted contribution is calculated by taking the raw mark scored, dividing it by the maximum mark available for that paper, multiplying by 100 to convert to a percentage, and then multiplying by the component weight expressed as a decimal.

Component contribution = (Raw mark ÷ Max mark) × 100 × (Weight ÷ 100)
Weighted composite = Sum of all completed component contributions
Running percentage = Weighted composite ÷ (Completed weight ÷ 100)

The weighted composite is the sum of the contributions of every completed paper. The running percentage divides that composite by the fraction of the subject that has been completed so far, which produces a figure out of one hundred that is directly comparable to the grade boundaries. When every paper has been sat, the running percentage equals the composite and is the figure the exam board uses.

Forecasting the mark needed on a remaining paper works in reverse. The target grade has a threshold, such as eighty per cent for a grade A. The points already secured are subtracted from that threshold to give the points still needed. That figure is then divided by the remaining weight expressed as a decimal to give the average percentage required on the remaining papers. If the required percentage is above one hundred, the target is mathematically out of reach with the papers that remain, and if it is below zero, the target has already been secured regardless of the remaining papers.

Grade boundaries move between series. The thresholds shown in this calculator are typical of recent sessions and are close to the standard published boundaries across the four English boards. Actual thresholds for a specific subject and series can differ by a few percentage points in either direction depending on paper difficulty, and the current specification and the results day boundaries for your board are the definitive reference.

The A* rule in modular and linear systems

The A* grade is the highest A-Level award and it follows different rules in the two systems. In a linear UK A-Level, the A* is set by a single composite raw mark threshold for the total across all papers. The threshold is higher than the threshold for a grade A, and the exact boundary is agreed by the awarding committee for each subject and each series after the papers have been marked. There is no separate A2 condition because the whole assessment is terminal.

In a modular international A-Level, the A* requires two conditions at the same time. The candidate must achieve an overall grade A, which means eighty per cent or higher of the total UMS available. The candidate must also achieve at least ninety per cent of the UMS available across the A2 units specifically, which is sometimes expressed as a hundred and eighty out of two hundred UMS if the A2 half totals two hundred. Missing the second condition while still holding the overall grade A results in a grade A rather than an A*.

That structure has a practical implication for modular candidates. A strong AS performance in Year 12 that carries the candidate close to the A boundary can still be undone by a weaker A2 performance, because the ninety per cent A2 condition is separate from the eighty per cent overall condition. A candidate who wants an A* in a modular subject needs to target the A2 papers with the ninety per cent threshold in mind, not just the eighty per cent overall figure.

Grade thresholds and UCAS Tariff points

The A-Level letter grades map to fixed UCAS Tariff points under the current tariff framework. The table below shows the thresholds and the points for each grade. UCAS has periodically reviewed and simplified the tariff, and the framework used by most applicants in the current cycle is the one shown.

GradeTypical thresholdUCAS points
A*90%+ overall, or 80% with 90% on A2 units56
A80%48
B70%40
C60%32
D50%24
E40% (minimum pass)16

A grade U, which is ungraded and below the minimum pass standard, earns no tariff points. A three-subject profile of A*A*A* therefore totals a hundred and sixty-eight UCAS points, while a profile of AAA totals a hundred and forty-four. The tariff is used by some universities as a comparator when the entry requirements are expressed as points rather than grades, though the more selective institutions almost always publish grade-based offers instead.

A worked example

Take a candidate sitting a three-paper science A-Level. The paper weights and the marks scored so far are as follows. Paper 1 is weighted at thirty-five per cent and the candidate scored seventy-eight out of a hundred and five. Paper 2 is weighted at thirty-five per cent and the candidate scored seventy-four out of a hundred and five. Paper 3 is weighted at thirty per cent and has not been sat yet.

The Paper 1 contribution is seventy-eight divided by one hundred and five, which equals approximately zero point seven four two nine, multiplied by thirty-five, which equals approximately twenty-six point zero zero per cent. The Paper 2 contribution is seventy-four divided by one hundred and five, which equals approximately zero point seven zero four eight, multiplied by thirty-five, which equals approximately twenty-four point six seven per cent. The composite so far is fifty point six seven per cent, on a completed weight of seventy per cent. The running percentage is fifty point six seven divided by zero point seven, which equals approximately seventy-two point four per cent. That falls in the grade B band at this stage of the year.

Now suppose the candidate is targeting a grade A, which requires eighty per cent overall. The points still needed are eighty minus fifty point six seven, which equals twenty-nine point three three. The remaining weight is thirty per cent, which is zero point three. The required average on Paper 3 is twenty-nine point three three divided by zero point three, which equals approximately ninety-seven point eight per cent. That is extremely high for a single paper and suggests the grade A target is not realistic on the current trajectory unless the other papers are re-sat at a future series.

If the candidate targets a grade B at seventy per cent instead, the points still needed are seventy minus fifty point six seven, which equals nineteen point three three. Divided by zero point three, the required average on Paper 3 is approximately sixty-four point four per cent. That is a realistic target and gives the candidate a clear working figure for the remaining preparation. The choice of target grade therefore changes the actionable number substantially, and it is worth running both the realistic and the aspirational target to see which is worth pursuing in the weeks before the final paper.

Common mistakes when forecasting a grade

A handful of recurring errors make the forecast less useful than it could be, or lead candidates to the wrong conclusions about what is achievable.

Using the wrong component weights. The weight of each paper is set by the board and the specification, and it does not always match what the school presentation said in class. Before entering weights, check the current specification on the board's website. A five-percentage-point error in the weight of a single paper can move the forecast by several points on the final composite.

Confusing raw marks with UMS. On a modular specification, the marks that count towards the final award are UMS marks, not raw paper marks. If you enter raw marks from a modular paper as if they were UMS marks, the forecast will be wrong. The raw-to-UMS conversion happens after the exam board agrees the grade boundaries for the paper, and it is published alongside the results.

Assuming the boundary is fixed. The thresholds shown in this calculator are typical of recent sessions. The actual boundary for a specific subject and series can be a few points higher or lower depending on how the papers performed in that cohort. A forecast that says eighty-two per cent is comfortable for a grade A can still be wrong if the boundary for that subject moved to eighty-four.

Overlooking the practical endorsement in sciences. In Biology, Chemistry, and Physics, the practical endorsement is reported separately and does not contribute to the letter grade, but a pass on the practical is often a condition of the university offer. A candidate with a strong predicted grade who has not been recorded as passing the practical endorsement can still fail to meet the offer, and the forecast should include the practical as a separate line item even though the maths does not.

Ignoring the AS-Level decoupling in linear specifications. A strong AS-Level in Year 12 does not carry forward to the full linear A-Level in England, and treating it as if it did can give a false sense of security. The linear A-Level grade depends entirely on the terminal examinations at the end of Year 13, and a candidate who relaxed after a strong AS performance has no cushion to fall back on.

Assuming a resit resets the forecast. A resit is a fresh attempt at the terminal papers, and the previous attempt does not carry forward on a linear specification. On a modular specification, unit marks carry forward within the validity window, so a resit may only replace part of the assessment. Confirm the carry-forward rule for your specific qualification before planning a resit strategy.

Choosing three subjects or four

The standard A-Level programme in the UK is three subjects, and almost every university offer is written in terms of three A-Level grades. A fourth A-Level adds UCAS Tariff points and demonstrates breadth of study, but it does not change the standard offer in most cases, and the additional workload can sometimes cost a grade in one of the three subjects that actually matter to the offer.

There are two situations in which a fourth A-Level is worth taking. The first is when a specific course requires or prefers a fourth subject, most commonly Further Mathematics for mathematics, engineering, or physics courses at the more selective universities, or a language for modern languages courses. In those cases the fourth subject is effectively part of the offer and should be planned for from the start. The second is when the candidate is genuinely strong across four subjects and can carry the workload without compromising the quality of preparation for the other three.

For most candidates, the more useful strategic decision is which three subjects to take rather than whether to take four. A profile of A*AA in three well-chosen subjects opens more doors than a profile of AABB across four, because the offer thresholds at the more selective universities are usually expressed as three grades. The tariff total is a useful comparator at the mid-tier universities that use points-based offers, but it is not the primary instrument at the top end of the sector.

Where this calculator stops

This tool estimates the weighted subject mark, the running grade, the target forecast, and the UCAS Tariff total from the figures you enter. It does not know the exact grade boundaries that your exam board will apply in any given series, because those are agreed after the papers have been marked and are not published in advance. It does not know whether your specific qualification is linear or modular, so it cannot automatically distinguish between a raw mark that counts directly and a raw mark that is first converted to UMS. It also does not know the specific entry requirements for any particular university programme, since those change year on year and are published in the current prospectus for each course.

The threshold table used here matches the typical published boundaries across the four English boards in recent sessions and the standard tariff points under the current UCAS framework. Use this calculator to see where you stand, to test how much a change in one paper would move the composite, and to plan the preparation effort on the papers that remain. Where the running percentage is close to a grade boundary, a few marks on the next paper can shift the final grade, and this calculator makes the boundary visible so the decision to push can be made deliberately.

Frequently asked questions

How is an A-Level weighted paper score calculated?

Each examination paper or coursework component carries a percentage weight that is set by the exam board in the specification. The weighted contribution of each completed paper is worked out by taking the raw mark scored, dividing it by the maximum mark available on that paper, multiplying by 100 to get a percentage, and then multiplying by the component weight divided by 100. The sum of those weighted contributions across every completed component gives the running composite percentage. When all papers are completed, that composite is the overall subject mark that the exam board compares against the grade boundaries for the year.

What is the difference between raw marks and UMS in A-Levels?

Raw marks are the actual points scored on an exam paper, such as 62 out of 80. Because paper difficulty varies slightly between sessions, exam boards convert raw marks into a scaled or uniform mark scale using statistical grade boundaries. On linear UK specifications the scaled mark is used only internally by the board, and the candidate sees a letter grade. On modular international specifications from Cambridge CAIE and Pearson IAL, the uniform mark scale is published and the candidate sees a UMS mark out of a fixed maximum for each unit.

How is an A* awarded in modular A-Levels compared with linear A-Levels?

In modular international A-Levels from Cambridge CAIE and Pearson IAL, the A* grade requires two conditions at the same time: an overall grade A, meaning eighty per cent or higher of the total UMS, and at least ninety per cent of the UMS available across the A2 units specifically. In linear UK A-Levels from AQA, Edexcel, OCR, and Eduqas, the A* is determined by a single composite raw mark threshold set by the board for the combined total across all papers, and no separate A2 condition applies because the whole assessment is taken at the end of Year 13.

Do AS-Level marks count towards the final A-Level in England?

In England, under the linear specifications introduced from 2015 onward, standalone AS-Levels are decoupled from the full A-Level and do not contribute marks to the final A-Level grade. The full A-Level is assessed entirely at the end of Year 13 through terminal examinations and, where applicable, non-examined assessment. In Cambridge CAIE and Pearson IAL, the AS-Level is not decoupled and continues to carry fifty per cent of the final A-Level mark under the modular structure.

What happens if my final mark is one or two marks below a grade boundary?

The school's examinations officer can request an official Review of Marking from the exam board within the published window after results are released. A clerical check verifies that every mark was recorded correctly, and a review of marking examines whether the original marking applied the mark scheme correctly. If the review results in a higher mark that crosses a grade boundary, the board issues an updated certificate and refunds the review fee. Reviews do not normally result in a lower mark unless the board finds a substantive error.

How do universities view four A-Levels compared with three?

Almost all UK universities, including Oxford and Cambridge, base standard conditional offers on three A-Levels. A fourth A-Level adds UCAS Tariff points and demonstrates breadth, but it does not usually change the standard offer. Universities generally prefer three top grades over four slightly lower grades, so a candidate choosing between spreading effort across four subjects or concentrating on three should weight the decision accordingly. A small number of courses in mathematics and engineering give a lower offer for candidates taking Further Mathematics as a fourth subject.

Can I combine marks from two different exam sittings to improve my grade?

On linear UK specifications the whole A-Level is assessed at the end of Year 13, so there is no option to combine component marks across sittings. A resit means re-sitting the full terminal papers and using the new marks. On modular specifications from Cambridge CAIE and Pearson IAL, unit marks are held on the candidate's record and can be carried forward within the validity window that the board applies. Most boards allow unit marks to be reused for around thirteen months, which typically covers two examination series.

Does this calculator store my marks or share my results?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the mode being used, the number of completed components, the weighted percentage, the grade band, and the target grade is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Your paper names, raw marks, subject names, and any personal identifiers never leave the browser.
