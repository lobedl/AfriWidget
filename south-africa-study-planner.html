What this planner does

Every South African student has felt the same thing at the start of a semester. You register for a full load, you look at your timetable, and the week looks manageable. Then week four arrives, the first assignments are due, and the hours you thought you had are suddenly gone. The problem is almost never laziness. The problem is that nobody has shown you the actual workload behind the credits on your registration form.

This planner turns those credits into a real number. Enter the NQF credits you are registered for, the number of teaching weeks in the semester, and the hours you already spend on lectures, work, and commuting. The planner tells you the weekly self-study target you need, the daily figure that breaks down to, and how heavy the overall week is once you add everything up. It also lays out a weekly time-block blueprint and a milestone list aligned with the South African academic year.

Everything runs inside your browser. No student number, no institution name, and no study hours are uploaded anywhere. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the calculation is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

How South African higher education counts study time

The South African higher education system is built on the National Qualifications Framework, usually shortened to the NQF. Every qualification on the framework has a total credit value, and every module you register for has its own credit value inside that qualification. A three-year bachelor degree is 360 credits. A four-year professional degree is 480. An honours year on top of a bachelor is 120. A National Senior Certificate from matric is 120 credits at NQF level 4.

What makes the framework useful for planning is that credits are not just labels. They are attached to a fixed number of notional learning hours. Once you know how many credits you have registered for, you can work out how many hours the semester is supposed to take, and from there you can see what the week needs to look like. That is the whole method behind this planner.

The same principle applies outside South Africa. Ghanaian universities running on the Ghana Tertiary Education Commission credit framework, Nigerian universities running on the National Universities Commission credit unit system, Kenyan and Ugandan universities on the East African credit transfer framework, and international universities on ECTS all work with the same idea. A credit represents a fixed amount of student effort, and the weeks in a semester tell you how that effort has to be spread.

The NQF credit and notional hour standard

One NQF credit is defined as ten notional hours of learning. Notional hours cover everything the average student is expected to do in order to achieve the learning outcomes of a module. That includes scheduled lectures, tutorials, laboratory sessions, practicals, studio time, and field trips. It also includes independent reading, assignment writing, group work, revision, and examination preparation.

The standard full-time semester is 60 credits, which works out to 600 notional hours. Spread across a fourteen-week teaching semester, that is about forty-three hours per week. Subtract the fourteen to eighteen hours most students spend in scheduled lectures and tutorials, and the remaining figure is between twenty-five and twenty-nine hours of independent study per week. That is the number most students do not plan for, and it is the number that quietly decides whether the semester goes well or badly.

Why the number feels high. Most students compare their university week to their school week, where the timetable filled the day and study happened in the evening. At university, the timetable is lighter by design. The extra hours are meant to be used for reading, problem sets, and revision, and the credits on the registration form assume you are doing exactly that. The number is not a punishment. It is the workload the qualification is built on.

Why commuting and part-time work change the plan

South African students rarely have the luxury of an eight-hour study day. Commuting alone can add one to three hours per day for students who live off campus. A student travelling by minibus taxi from Soweto to Wits, or from Khayelitsha to UCT, or from Mamelodi to UP, is losing a large block of time before a single lecture starts. When you add a part-time job to pay for transport, data, and food, the real working week quickly stretches past what a full-time university schedule assumes.

The planner handles this by counting every hour you already commit to work and commute, and showing you the total. If the total is too high, the answer is not to push through and hope. The answer is to adjust the plan. Common adjustments that work in practice include dropping to a 45-credit semester while working, moving shifts to the weekend, using campus library time between lectures so you do not travel home mid-day, or splitting one or two modules into the next semester so the weekly total stays inside a manageable range.

A useful rule of thumb in the South African context is that a student can handle a full 60-credit load without paid work, or a 45-credit load with ten to fifteen hours of paid work, or a 30-credit load with twenty hours of paid work and long commutes. Anything more than that requires a serious conversation with an academic adviser, a faculty student support office, or a funding office such as NSFAS.

How the weekly plan is built

The planner uses four simple steps.

Step 1. Total semester hours = NQF credits × 10 Step 2. Weekly academic hours = Total semester hours ÷ Teaching weeks Step 3. Self-study hours = Weekly academic hours − Scheduled contact hours Step 4. Total weekly load = Weekly academic hours + Work hours + Commute hours

Once those four figures are in place, the load status falls into one of five bands. Under 35 hours per week is a light pace, usually a part-time semester or a reduced load. Between 35 and 45 hours is a standard full-time pace for a student with no paid work and a short commute. Between 46 and 55 is a high-intensity load, common when a student carries a full credit load and works or commutes long distances. Between 56 and 65 is a heavy load with a real risk of burnout. Above 65 is an overload that almost always needs adjusting.

The planner also checks whether your self-study hours cover at least half of the notional workload, whether your commute is under ten hours per week, and whether your paid work is under fifteen hours per week. Those three checks catch the most common structural problems in a South African student week, and they are the ones most worth taking seriously before you settle on a plan.

A worked example for a South African student

Take a second-year Bachelor of Commerce student at a large urban university. She is registered for four modules at fifteen credits each, so the semester totals 60 NQF credits. The teaching semester is fourteen weeks. Her timetable includes twelve hours of lectures and one tutorial per module, which comes to sixteen contact hours per week. She works twelve hours per week at a retail store on weekends. Her commute to campus takes an hour each way, four days per week, so eight hours per week.

The total weekly load of almost sixty-three hours puts her in the heavy band, with a real risk of burnout. The planner's advice for this student is to either reduce the paid work to eight hours per week, drop to a 45-credit semester, or restructure the week so that two of her study days are spent entirely on campus from morning to evening, cutting the number of commute trips. Any one of those changes brings the total weekly load down to a workable range without dropping the full credit load.

The self-study figure of almost twenty-seven hours looks heavy on its own, but it breaks down to about five and a half hours per weekday if she studies five days a week, which is realistic when you have a library between lectures. The problem is not the self-study hours. The problem is the total. Adding twelve hours of work and eight hours of commute on top of a forty-three hour academic week is what pushes the schedule over the edge.

Common mistakes South African students make

Treating the timetable as the workload. The timetable is the scheduled part. The full workload is the timetable plus the reading, problem sets, and revision the credits assume. Students who plan only around lectures and tutorials consistently underestimate their week by twenty to thirty hours, and then wonder why the semester feels out of control by week six.

Underestimating commute time. A short taxi ride from a nearby township to a city campus can easily take an hour each way once you include waiting, transfers, and traffic. Eight hours of commute per week is a real cost, and it needs to be built into the plan, not treated as free time.

Overloading the semester to finish faster. Registering for 90 credits instead of 60 to finish a degree in two and a half years is tempting, especially when funding is tight. The problem is that the notional hours scale with the credits, so a 90-credit semester is around sixty-five hours per week of academic work alone. Very few students can sustain that for fourteen weeks without a drop in marks.

Ignoring the second-semester squeeze. The South African academic year has a long mid-year break that most students treat as holiday. Students who plan properly use that break to clear deferred work, get ahead on reading, or make up a module that did not go well in the first semester. Students who do not end up in a compressed second semester that is much harder than the first.

Assuming every university week is the same. Weeks one to four are usually lighter, with orientation, new content, and few assignments. Weeks five to ten are the heavy teaching weeks with the first major tests. Weeks eleven to fourteen are dominated by assignment deadlines and final revision. A realistic plan assumes the workload is not flat, and shifts study hours forward into the earlier weeks so the back half of the semester does not collapse.

Not checking the faculty calendar. Every university publishes an academic calendar with test weeks, mid-semester breaks, public holidays, and the final examination period. A plan that ignores the calendar will collide with it in week seven. Check the calendar in the first week of the semester and mark the heavy weeks before you plan anything else.

Skipping the mentoring and support sessions. Most South African universities run free academic support: writing centres, maths help desks, tutor sessions, and student counselling. These are not emergency services. They are the fastest way to close a gap between where you are and where the credits assume you should be. Students who use them early rarely need them late.

Who this planner is for

The planner is built for undergraduate and postgraduate students at South African universities, universities of technology, and TVET colleges. It works for a first-year student coming straight out of matric, a mature student returning to study after years away from the classroom, a student on a NSFAS bursary working out how many credits to register for, and a postgraduate student balancing a thesis with a part-time job.

It also works for the people around them. A parent trying to understand why their child is stressed in week seven can use the planner to see the actual weekly load. A faculty academic adviser can run through it with a student who is about to register for a heavy semester. A student support office at a TVET college can use it during orientation to explain why full-time study is a full-time commitment.

Outside South Africa, the same method works for students in Ghana, Nigeria, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and any other country that expresses study load in credits or credit units. The numbers change. The principle does not.

Where the planner stops

This planner estimates the weekly workload from a credit value, a semester length, and the hours you enter yourself. It does not know the exact number of notional hours your specific institution applies to each module, because some faculties adjust the notional hours slightly for practical or studio modules. It does not know your personal study pace, your existing academic base, or how well you retain material from one week to the next.

It also does not model the way the workload actually moves across a semester. Weeks one to three are almost always lighter than weeks ten to fourteen, and the planner spreads the load evenly because that is the only honest way to give you a weekly figure. In practice, most students front-load reading in the early weeks so the later weeks have more room for test preparation and assignment writing.

What the planner does give you is a fast, transparent, private estimate of the weekly hours a semester actually requires, so you can see the workload before you commit to it, and adjust the plan while there is still time. Use it alongside your faculty handbook, your academic calendar, and your institution's student support office. The final plan should come from you and your adviser, not from a calculator on its own.

Frequently asked questions

What is a credit in South African higher education?

In the South African National Qualifications Framework, one credit represents ten notional hours of learning. Notional hours include lectures, tutorials, practicals, laboratory work, reading, assignment writing, revision, and exam preparation. A standard three-year bachelor degree is 360 credits, which is roughly 3,600 notional hours across the degree. A full-time semester of 60 credits is therefore about 600 notional hours of learning before you account for anything else in your week.

How many hours per week should a full-time South African student study?

A 60-credit full-time semester over fourteen weeks works out to about forty-three notional hours per week. Subtract scheduled lectures, tutorials, and practicals, and the remaining figure is your weekly self-study target. Most South African universities expect this to land somewhere between twenty-five and thirty-five hours of independent study per week, which is more than most first-year students plan for. The calculator shows you the exact number based on your own registered credits and semester length.

Does the planner work for Ghana, Nigeria, and other African countries?

Yes. The planner is built around the South African NQF credit standard, but the underlying method applies to any system that converts credit values into study hours. Ghanaian universities running on the GTEC credit framework, Nigerian universities running on the NUC credit unit system, Kenyan and Ugandan universities on the East African credit transfer system, and international universities on ECTS all use the same principle: credits times notional hours divided by weeks equals weekly workload. Enter your credit value from your handbook and the planner produces a realistic weekly target.

How do I fit part-time work and commuting around a full credit load?

The honest answer is that a full load plus fifteen hours of paid work plus eight hours of commuting is a heavy week that leaves little room for anything else. The planner shows the total figure so you can see exactly how heavy the load is before you commit to it. Common adjustments are dropping to a 45-credit semester while working, moving shifts to weekends, using campus library time between lectures to cut commuting, or switching one or two courses to a later semester so the total load stays manageable.

What is the standard semester length at a South African university?

Most South African universities run two semesters of roughly fourteen weeks each, with a short mid-semester break and a separate examination period. The first semester runs from late January or early February to June, and the second runs from July to November. Some universities of technology and TVET colleges use trimesters instead, with shorter teaching blocks of twelve weeks. Select the semester length that matches your own academic calendar, because the weekly study target rises sharply when the number of teaching weeks falls.

Is this planner an official DHET or university tool?

No. It is an independent academic planning aid built to match the published NQF credit and notional hour standard used across South African higher education. The official workload, assessment calendar, and pass requirements are set by your institution and by DHET. Use this planner to build a realistic weekly routine, and treat your faculty handbook and your own timetable as the definitive source.

Does the planner store my study hours or share my results?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored on any server. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the calculation is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. The credit load, semester length, contact hours band, work hours band, commute hours band, and computed weekly targets are shared, but no names, student numbers, institution names, or personal details leave the browser.

Can I use this planner for a TVET college programme in South Africa?

Yes. TVET programmes under the National Certificate (Vocational) framework and the NATED Report 191 framework both carry NQF credit values, and the same notional hour standard applies. A full-time NC(V) or NATED semester usually sits between forty and sixty credits depending on the number of subjects. Enter the credit value from your college programme guide and the planner will produce the same weekly target a university student would see for the same credit load.
