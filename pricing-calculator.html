What this calculator does

This tool works out the price a product or service needs to be sold at in order to hit a target profit. You give it three numbers, and it returns the rest. The three inputs are the total cost of one unit, the profit target expressed either as a margin or as a markup, and, if you sell online and want to account for it, the fee that your payment processor charges. The output is the selling price, the net profit per unit, the effective margin, the effective markup, and, if the fee is enabled, the fee amount and the net amount you actually receive per sale.

The interesting part of the calculation is the fee. Payment processors take a percentage of the total transaction plus a small fixed amount, which means the fee grows when you raise the price. A business that ignores this ends up with a margin below what it thought it was targeting. This tool reverses the fee out of the price first, so the amount you keep after the processor takes its cut matches the margin you asked for. That is the difference between a calculator that guesses and one that plans.

Everything runs inside the browser. Every input, every intermediate value, and every output stays in the page. If the AI insight button is pressed, only anonymous counts are sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. The mode, the cost band, the target percentage, the fee flags, and the computed figures are shared, and nothing else.

Margin versus markup

Margin and markup are two different ways of expressing the same profit, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes in small business pricing. The difference is the base that the profit is compared against.

TermBaseFormulaExample on cost 10, price 15
MarginSelling price(Profit ÷ Price) × 10033.3%
MarkupCost(Profit ÷ Cost) × 10050.0%

Both figures describe the same five units of profit, but they land on very different percentages. Accountants, lenders, and investors almost always work in margin, because it lines up with the revenue and profit lines on a financial statement and because margins across products can be compared on the same basis. Retailers often work in markup, because markup maps directly onto the cost of the goods on the shelf and is easy to apply on the shop floor.

This calculator lets you work in either mode. If you enter a target margin, the tool treats the profit as a percentage of the selling price. If you enter a target markup, the tool treats the profit as a percentage added directly to the cost. The results panel always shows both figures so you can see the corresponding value in the other mode.

The formulas behind the price

Four formulas power the whole tool. Knowing them makes the output easier to check.

Margin mode (no fee)
Price = Cost ÷ (1 − Target Margin)

Margin mode (with fee)
Price = (Cost + Fixed Fee) ÷ (1 − Target Margin − Fee Rate)

Markup mode (no fee)
Price = Cost × (1 + Target Markup)

Markup mode (with fee)
Price = (Cost × (1 + Target Markup) + Fixed Fee) ÷ (1 − Fee Rate)

Take a product that costs ten and a target margin of forty per cent. Without a fee, the price is ten divided by zero point six, which is sixteen point six seven. Add a payment processor fee of three point four nine per cent plus forty-nine cents, and the denominator shrinks to zero point five six five one, while the numerator becomes ten point four nine. The new price is eighteen point five six, which is almost two pounds more than the no-fee price. The extra two pounds is not extra profit; it is the amount needed to cover the fee and still leave a forty per cent margin on the transaction.

A useful sanity check. If the fee rate plus the target margin adds up to one hundred per cent or more, the calculation has no solution, because the price would have to be infinite. The tool flags this and asks you to reduce one of the two numbers. In practice this never happens with realistic margins and realistic processor fees, but it is worth knowing why the guard is there.

Why merchant fees matter

A payment processor is not a free service, and the fee it charges is a real cost that has to be paid from the sale. The fee is almost always expressed as a percentage of the transaction amount plus a fixed per-transaction amount, which means it grows when the price grows. That behaviour is what makes the fee different from a normal cost: raising the price to protect the margin also raises the fee, and the two effects partially cancel out.

The size of the fee varies by provider and by market. In the United States a typical card-acquiring fee is around two point nine per cent plus thirty cents. Stripe advertises a similar structure for online card payments in most countries, and PayPal sits slightly higher at around three point four nine per cent plus forty-nine cents for standard transactions. In African markets, mobile money providers and local card acquirers often charge between one and three per cent of the transaction amount, sometimes with no fixed component.

The practical effect of ignoring fees is that the business thinks it is earning one margin and is actually earning a smaller one. A product priced at one hundred to earn a thirty per cent margin loses eight or nine percentage points of margin once a typical card fee and a small fixed amount are deducted. That gap is the difference between a business that pays its tax bill comfortably and one that struggles in the same quarter. The right move is to treat the fee as part of the pricing model rather than as a line item discovered later.

Choosing a realistic target margin

The right margin depends on the industry, the cost base, and the volume of sales. The table below gives the typical gross margin ranges for common product and service categories as a starting point.

CategoryTypical gross marginWhy
Grocery and packaged food retail20 to 30%High volume, thin margins, low differentiation
Restaurants and catering60 to 70%Food cost is a small share of the final price
Fashion and general retail40 to 60%Seasonality and markdowns are priced in
Software and digital products70 to 90%Marginal cost per user is close to zero
Consulting and agency services40 to 60%Labour cost sits directly against the fee
Manufacturing of physical goods25 to 45%Material, labour, and overhead all sit in the cost base

Two rules of thumb help when the market does not fall neatly into a category. First, the gross margin has to be large enough to cover fixed overheads (rent, salaries, software, insurance, marketing) and still leave a real profit after tax. A forty per cent gross margin in a business with thirty-five per cent fixed overheads leaves only five per cent net, which is not enough to fund growth. Second, the margin should reflect the value the customer sees, not just the cost the seller incurs. A product that saves a customer ten hours of work per month can be priced well above the labour cost of making it, and the customer will still see it as fair.

A worked example

Consider a handmade candle business. Each candle uses two pounds of wax, a wick, a jar, a label, and a box. The materials cost four pounds fifty. Pouring and finishing takes twenty minutes at a notional labour rate of ten pounds per hour, which is three pounds thirty-three. Shipping materials add another forty pence. The total cost per candle is eight pounds twenty-three.

LineAmount

Labour at £10/hour for 20 min£3.33

Total cost per unit£8.23
Target margin45%
Payment processor fee2.90% + £0.30

Without the fee, the price would have been fourteen point nine six. With the fee reversed out of the price, the price rises to seventeen point six seven, and the seller keeps seven pounds ninety-five per candle after paying the cost of goods and the transaction fee. That is the target forty-five per cent margin on the transaction amount.

Now suppose the seller expects to sell one hundred candles a month. The monthly revenue is one thousand seven hundred and sixty-seven pounds, and the monthly net profit before fixed overheads is seven hundred and ninety-five pounds. Over a year that is nine thousand five hundred and forty pounds, which is the number the seller needs to compare against rent, marketing, insurance, and any salary they intend to pay themselves. The pricing calculator produces all of this without the seller having to touch a spreadsheet.

Common mistakes

Confusing margin and markup. A fifty per cent markup is not the same as a fifty per cent margin. Marking up by fifty per cent on a ten-pound cost gives a fifteen-pound price and a thirty-three per cent margin. If the goal was a fifty per cent margin, the price should have been twenty pounds. Choosing the wrong mode in a pricing tool, or leaving the mode set incorrectly in a spreadsheet, is the single most common cause of underpriced products.

Ignoring payment processor fees. Every online sale usually has a fee attached to it. A business that prices as if it were paid in cash will see its margin quietly disappear once the processor takes its cut. The fee is small per transaction but it adds up over a year and it is entirely predictable, so there is no reason not to include it.

Forgetting founder labour. If the founder is making the product, the labour is a real cost whether or not it appears in the bank statement. Pricing without counting founder hours makes the business look more profitable than it is, and the founder ends up subsidising the product with unpaid time.

Setting a margin based on the wrong cost base. The margin calculation should use the direct cost of producing or delivering one unit. If the cost includes allocated fixed overhead, the margin is inflated and the price will be higher than the market expects. If the cost excludes packaging, shipping, or transaction fees, the margin is deflated and the price will be lower than it should be. Decide what is inside the cost base and keep it consistent.

Raising the price without checking the market. The calculator produces a price that hits the target margin. Whether that price is sellable is a separate question. If the calculated price is well above what the market will pay, the answer is usually to reduce the cost base, accept a lower margin for that product, or differentiate on something the customer values. Discounting the price without changing any of those things just shrinks the margin further.

Applying the same margin to every product. Different products play different roles in a catalogue. A high-volume, low-margin product brings customers in; a low-volume, high-margin product pays the bills. Setting every product to the same margin ignores the way the catalogue actually works.

Forgetting sales tax and VAT. The margin calculation uses the amount the business actually receives, not the amount the customer pays. If the business is registered for VAT or sales tax, the tax portion passes straight through to the government and is not part of the revenue on which the margin is calculated.

Where this tool stops

This calculator works out the price that hits a target margin or markup given the cost and, optionally, a payment processor fee. It does not know whether the target margin is appropriate for the market, whether the cost base is complete, or whether the resulting price is competitive. It does not model volume discounts, tiered pricing, subscription pricing, or the difference between one-time sales and recurring revenue. It does not include tax, which has to be handled outside the margin calculation.

It also does not provide financial advice or business advice. The decision to set a price, to launch a product at a loss to gain market share, or to hold a price in the face of a competitor's discount is a decision for the business owner and any advisor they choose to work with. The tool produces a number; the judgement about what to do with it belongs to the person using the tool.

What the tool does give you is a fast, transparent, private way to work out a selling price, see the corresponding margin and markup, add merchant fees into the calculation, and copy the summary for a quote, a pitch deck, or a conversation with an accountant. Used as one input among several, it is a practical starting point for anyone pricing a product or a service.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between margin and markup?

Margin is profit expressed as a percentage of the selling price. Markup is profit expressed as a percentage of the cost. A product that costs ten and sells for fifteen has a five profit either way, but that is a fifty per cent markup on cost and a thirty-three point three per cent margin on the selling price. Accountants, lenders, and investors usually work in margin because it lines up with the revenue and profit lines on a financial statement. Retailers often work in markup because it maps directly to the cost of the goods on the shelf.

Why does adding payment processor fees change the price?

Payment processors such as Stripe, PayPal, and card acquirers charge a percentage of the total transaction plus a small fixed fee. When you raise the price, the processor fee grows with it, so you cannot simply add the fee on top of the price you would have charged in cash. This calculator reverses the fee out of the price first, so that the amount you actually receive after the processor takes its cut matches your target profit. Without that adjustment, a business that thinks it is earning a thirty per cent margin can end up closer to twenty-five per cent once the fees clear.

How do I pick a realistic target margin?

The right margin depends on the industry and the cost base. Physical retailers of commodity goods often run on twenty to forty per cent gross margin. Restaurants run on sixty to seventy per cent on food and beverage, because the labour and rent sit below the gross profit line. Software and digital products often run above eighty per cent because the marginal cost of one more user is close to zero. Consulting and agency services typically sit between forty and sixty per cent. The rule of thumb is that the margin has to be high enough to cover fixed overheads and leave a real net profit after tax.

Should I include my own labour in the cost?

Yes, if you are a sole trader or a small business. Founders often price products without counting the hours they spend making them, and the result is that the business appears profitable on paper while the founder is effectively working for free. The clean way to handle this is to add a line for your own labour at a realistic hourly rate, or to add a founder draw to your fixed overheads and make sure the total margin covers it. If you would not pay an employee that rate for the same work, the number is too low.

How does psychological pricing affect the numbers?

Charm pricing ends a price in ninety-nine, ninety-five, or ninety. The effect on revenue is real but small, and it works best on low-value consumer goods where the price is under one hundred. On higher-ticket items, round numbers often read as more confident and premium. Whatever you choose, the arithmetic has to work: subtracting one cent to end in ninety-nine is fine, subtracting ten per cent to end in ninety-nine can destroy the margin. Use the calculator first to find a price that covers costs, then decide whether a psychological tweak is worth the small change in margin.

What if my calculated price is higher than the competition?

You have three honest options. First, reduce the direct cost, either by sourcing cheaper materials, negotiating with a supplier, or reworking the design so it uses less. Second, accept a lower margin for that product and make the money back on a different product or on volume. Third, differentiate on something the customer values, such as quality, warranty, delivery speed, or packaging, so that the higher price is justified in the customer's mind. What does not work is undercutting the competition while keeping the same cost base, because that only accelerates the cash burn.

Does the calculator include VAT, sales tax, or other indirect taxes?

No. The figures are net of indirect taxes because VAT and sales tax rules vary by country, business structure, and product category, and the tax is usually collected on behalf of the government rather than earned by the business. If your market requires tax-inclusive pricing, add the local tax rate to the calculated price after you have settled on the margin. If you are VAT-registered, remember that the VAT portion is not your revenue, and the margin calculation should use the tax-exclusive amount.

How do I price a service rather than a product?

The formula is the same, but the cost side looks different. Work out the cost of delivering the service: the time you spend, the direct expenses such as travel and software licences, and any subcontractor fees. Add up those numbers and treat the total as the cost per unit, where the unit is one client engagement or one hour of work. Then apply the same margin calculation. Service businesses often forget that non-billable time such as admin, sales, and travel has to be covered inside the billable rate, which is why a freelancer aiming for a forty per cent margin usually has to set the visible hourly rate noticeably higher than the target.

Is my pricing data stored or shared?

No. Every figure you enter stays inside your browser tab, and the arithmetic runs locally with JavaScript. Nothing is transmitted to a server, and nothing is saved. The page is a static file, so there is no database behind it. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the structure is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. The mode, the cost band, the target percentage, the fee flags, and the computed figures are shared, but no item names, product descriptions, or personal details are included.

Can I use this calculator for a business outside the United States?

Yes. The arithmetic is the same everywhere because the tool only works with the numbers you enter. The currency selector lets you work in USD, Ghanaian cedi, Nigerian naira, Kenyan shilling, South African rand, euro, pound, Canadian dollar, Australian dollar, or Indian rupee. What changes between countries is the typical margin for a given product category, the rate charged by local payment processors, and the indirect taxes that sit outside the margin calculation. The tool handles the maths; the market context belongs to you and to any advisor who knows your sector.
