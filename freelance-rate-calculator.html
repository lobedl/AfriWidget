What this calculator actually does

This tool takes the annual income you want to keep and works backwards to the minimum hourly rate your freelance business needs to charge. It adds back four layers that sit between gross revenue and personal take-home: monthly business overhead, an income tax allowance, a profit and risk buffer, and the reality that you cannot bill every hour you work. Once those layers are applied, the calculator divides the resulting revenue target by the hours you can realistically sell in a year.

The output shows the recommended minimum hourly rate, the equivalent day rate for short engagements, the monthly retainer figure if you want to offer subscription-style arrangements, and the total gross revenue the business must generate to hit your number. A small bench panel shows what the rate would be at different billable loads, which is usually the most revealing part of the whole tool: if your recommended rate feels high, the bench almost always shows that the hours assumption was too optimistic rather than the income target being unreasonable.

The default currency is the Nigerian naira, with South African rand next, because those are the two largest English-speaking freelance markets in Africa and the numbers most readers of this page will be working in. The currency selector also covers the US dollar, the euro, the pound, the Ghanaian cedi, the Kenyan shilling, the Canadian and Australian dollars, and the Indian rupee, so freelancers billing international clients can see the same rate in the currency they actually invoice in.

Why a freelance rate is not a salaried rate

The single biggest mistake new freelancers make is taking the hourly equivalent of their old salaried job and charging that to clients. A salaried package includes a lot more than the gross figure printed on the offer letter. The employer pays payroll taxes, contributes to pension or provident funds, covers paid leave, funds sick pay, provides a workspace, supplies equipment, and often subsidises medical aid and training. On the freelance side, every one of those costs moves onto the freelancer, and the bill for them arrives in the same month the client invoice was supposed to.

The second mistake is assuming the working week can be entirely billable. A salaried employee is paid for every hour they are on the clock, including meetings, internal admin, and the training sessions their employer sends them on. A freelancer is paid only for the hours a client agrees to pay for, and everything else, from proposals to invoicing to learning a new tool, happens on unpaid time. That gap between hours worked and hours billed is the reason freelance rates look high to people outside the profession.

A sanity check worth doing once. Take your recommended hourly rate and multiply it by the number of billable hours you entered. If the result is not at least one and a half times the hourly equivalent of a comparable salaried job, one of the inputs is probably too optimistic. The most common culprits are too many billable hours assumed, too few weeks off, and overhead estimated at zero.

The four cost layers

Four layers sit between gross revenue and the money that reaches your personal account, and the calculator asks for each of them individually so the effect of any one of them is visible.

Business overhead. Everything the business spends to operate. For a Nigerian freelancer working from home in Lagos, that usually includes data bundles, a portion of the electricity bill or the monthly cost of fuelling a generator, a contribution to rent for the workspace, laptop and phone depreciation, cloud software subscriptions, cloud storage, banking fees, and professional fees for accounting or tax compliance. For a South African freelancer in Johannesburg or Cape Town, similar lines apply with an emphasis on fibre internet, load-shedding backup power, cloud tools, medical aid contributions if self-funded, and the annual cost of provisional tax filings and CIPC compliance. A solo freelancer's overhead typically runs between five and twenty per cent of revenue depending on the service and the location.

Tax allowance. Self-employment income is taxed differently in almost every country, and the correct treatment is too complex to bake into a single field. In Nigeria, freelancers are taxed under Personal Income Tax, administered by the Federal Inland Revenue Service for individuals earning above the statutory threshold or by the relevant state internal revenue service below it. In South Africa, freelancers register as provisional taxpayers with the South African Revenue Service and pay income tax on taxable profit through two provisional returns and a final annual assessment. Rates and thresholds move from year to year, and the treatment of foreign-currency income adds a further layer. The calculator's tax field is a planning allowance so the rate you derive is not wildly under-provisioned, and it should be confirmed against current rules before you rely on it.

Profit and risk buffer. This is money the business keeps so that a slow month does not become a personal cash crisis. It is not savings and it is not profit distribution. It is the reserve that absorbs the gap between invoice and payment, the cost of an unexpected equipment failure, and the unpaid weeks when a project gets cancelled or delayed. A buffer of five to fifteen per cent of revenue is a common planning range.

Billable time versus total time. The final layer is not a cost but a constraint. Every working hour that does not produce a client invoice is an hour the business has to fund from elsewhere. The calculator handles this by asking for billable hours per week rather than hours worked per week, and by reducing the working weeks to account for holidays, sick days, and the slow periods that every freelancer experiences at least once a year.

Billable hours are the number most freelancers get wrong

The billable hours field is where the calculation usually fails, and it fails in the same direction every time. Freelancers enter the hours they would like to bill rather than the hours they realistically can, and the resulting rate looks comfortable while the bank balance tells a very different story.

Consider what a solo freelance week actually contains. Client work is maybe half of it. Proposals, discovery calls, and meetings with prospects who never convert take an hour or two. Marketing, portfolio maintenance, and social posting take another hour or two. Invoicing, chasing payment, bookkeeping, and general admin take an hour or two more. Learning new software, reading about the industry, and staying current is another hour or two. Equipment maintenance, backups, and IT housekeeping eat into the rest. A freelancer who sits at their desk for forty hours a week is doing well if twenty-five of those hours are billable.

The practical takeaway is that if you are not sure what your true billable percentage is, start with twenty to twenty-five hours a week and treat that as the upper bound. If after a couple of months your actual invoices show a higher figure, adjust upward and the recommended rate will fall. If they show lower, adjust downward and the rate will rise. Either way, the number should be driven by your invoices, not by your intention.

The Nigerian and South African context

Freelancing in Nigeria and South Africa comes with a handful of cost realities that freelancers in London or Toronto simply do not face, and they belong in your rate even if they feel invisible.

Power and connectivity. Both markets carry meaningful costs for electricity, backup power, and internet bandwidth. A Nigerian freelancer often runs on a mix of grid power and generator or inverter, with fuel and battery replacement as real monthly lines. A South African freelancer budgets for load-shedding backup, additional mobile data during outages, and frequently for a home UPS to protect equipment. These are not optional overheads in the sense that a designer might skip a software subscription for a month. Without them, client work stops.

Currency and payment rails. Many freelancers in both countries bill international clients in dollars or pounds and receive payment through platforms such as Payoneer, Wise, or a bank's domiciliary account. Each of those channels has its own spread, withdrawal timing, and conversion cost. The revenue that arrives in the local account is almost always smaller than the gross invoice suggests, and the difference belongs in the overhead line rather than being treated as a surprise.

Compliance and paperwork. Nigeria requires self-employed individuals earning above a statutory threshold to register for a Tax Identification Number and to file annual returns, with additional registration obligations under Value Added Tax for some service businesses. South Africa requires provisional tax registration and two submissions per year plus an annual return, and depending on turnover a freelancer may qualify for the simplified Turnover Tax regime. Both countries have updated their rules in recent years, and the cost of getting compliance wrong is not just the tax: it is the penalties, the interest, and the time spent sorting out the mess.

Client mix and pricing expectations. Local clients in both markets often anchor to a rate that reflects the local cost of living, while international clients anchor to a rate that reflects the client's own market. Freelancers who serve both types of clients need to decide whether they are running a local business at local rates or an international service at international rates, and the answer has consequences for every part of the pricing strategy. There is no right or wrong answer, but there is a wrong way to run the numbers: charging international clients at local rates because the freelancer never built the case for anything higher.

A worked example

Take a Nigerian freelance UX designer based in Lagos who wants to take home twelve million naira a year. She budgets eighty thousand naira a month for overhead covering data, inverter and generator fuel, cloud software, banking fees, and accounting. She allows twenty-five per cent for tax and ten per cent for the profit buffer. She plans four weeks off and twenty-five billable hours a week.

Working weeks are fifty-two minus four, which is forty-eight. Billable hours per year are twenty-five times forty-eight, which is one thousand two hundred. The three multiplication factors are 0.75 for the remaining share after tax, 0.90 for the remaining share after the buffer, and the overhead is handled as an addition to income rather than a percentage because it was entered as a fixed monthly figure. The required gross revenue before tax and buffer is the take-home income plus annual overhead, which is twelve million plus nine hundred and sixty thousand, or twelve point nine six million naira. Dividing by zero point seven five gives seventeen point two eight million naira after tax and before the buffer, and multiplying by one point one for the buffer gives roughly nineteen million naira of required gross revenue.

Dividing nineteen million naira by one thousand two hundred billable hours gives a minimum hourly rate of about fifteen thousand eight hundred naira. The day rate at eight hours is about one hundred and twenty-six thousand naira. If she takes on a monthly retainer client who prepays for a fixed number of hours each month, the equivalent retainer figure is the annual revenue divided by twelve, roughly one point five eight million naira a month. Now look at what happens if she can only bill twenty hours a week rather than twenty-five. Billable hours per year drop to nine hundred and sixty, and the required rate rises to about nineteen thousand eight hundred naira per hour. Same income target, same overhead, same tax rate, same buffer. The only input that changed was the billable load, and the effect on the required rate is thirty per cent. That is why the billable hours field is the most sensitive input in the whole calculator.

Swap the numbers to South African rand and the arithmetic is identical, with the rand amounts scaled to whatever take-home figure a Johannesburg or Cape Town freelancer is targeting. The currency label is not the point. The point is the shape of the calculation, and the fact that the rate is a function of four independent inputs rather than a single market-accepted guess.

Pricing a fixed project or retainer

Hourly rates are a planning tool, not a commercial strategy. Most clients prefer fixed quotes because they can budget against a number rather than watch a meter, and most freelancers eventually prefer them too, because they reward efficiency rather than penalise it. A fixed quote is built from four components.

Estimated hours. Multiply the hours the project will genuinely take, including research, client communication, revisions, testing, and delivery, by your minimum hourly rate. The first estimate is almost always too low, because it counts only the work the client can see and forgets everything that supports it.

Direct project costs. Any expense you have to incur to complete this specific project, such as printing, travel, licences, subcontracted work, or specialised equipment. These are pass-through costs and they belong in the quote in full rather than being absorbed into the hourly rate.

Contingency. Add ten to twenty per cent on top of the hours to cover the scope changes that will arrive once the client sees an early draft. Without a contingency, every change becomes either an argument or a personal cost.

Value adjustment. Finally, look at what the project is worth to the client. If it is a small internal document, the price the hours produce is probably right. If it is a launch campaign for a product the client expects to sell for a large multiple of the project fee, the value of the outcome is much higher than the hours, and a percentage of that value is fair. This is the hardest adjustment to make because it requires confidence, but it is standard practice in consulting and high-end creative work.

Monthly retainers work differently. A retainer is a prepaid commitment for a fixed allocation of hours or a fixed set of deliverables each month. It gives the freelancer predictable cash flow and gives the client priority access. Retainers are usually priced slightly below the hourly rate because the volume and the predictability are worth something. A common structure is a ten to fifteen per cent discount on the hourly rate in exchange for a commitment of at least three months and payment in advance.

Common mistakes when setting a rate

Six mistakes come up repeatedly when freelancers work out what to charge, and each one is worth checking against your own numbers.

Anchoring to the lowest local rate. A rate based on the cheapest competitor's price is not a rate, it is a race to the bottom. The correct starting point is what the business needs, and the market conversation happens after that floor is known.

Ignoring time off entirely. Freelancers who plan to work fifty-two weeks a year are planning for burnout by month eight. Four to six weeks off is not generous; it is the minimum needed to keep working at a professional level across a decade.

Forgetting the non-billable admin time. Every invoice, every quote, every email to a supplier, every minute spent updating accounting software is time the business pays for. If the billable hours assumption does not account for it, the rate will be too low.

Charging the same rate forever. Rates should rise with experience, with portfolio strength, and with market movement. A rate that was right in year one is almost never right in year four.

Not writing down scope. Most money disputes in freelance work come from a mismatch between what the freelancer thought the project included and what the client thought. A written scope is boring and takes an hour to prepare, and it prevents the argument that ends the relationship.

Treating the buffer as optional. The buffer feels like profit that could be taken home instead. It is not. It is the reserve that keeps the business solvent during a slow month, and skipping it is exactly what sends freelancers back to full-time employment in year two.

Where this calculator stops

This tool estimates the minimum rate your inputs require. It does not tell you what a specific client will pay, and it does not account for the fact that different markets support very different rates for the same work. It does not model multiple service lines, productised offerings, or the mix of hourly, fixed-price, and retainer work that most mature freelancers eventually use.

It also does not model tax correctly for any specific jurisdiction. Tax rules, thresholds, registration requirements, VAT or sales tax obligations, and self-employment contributions vary widely between Nigeria, South Africa, and every other country, and the calculator's single tax percentage is a planning allowance rather than a precise figure. If your income is significant enough that the difference between twenty-five per cent and thirty-five per cent matters, speak with a qualified tax adviser in your own country, ideally before your first filing deadline rather than after it.

Finally, the calculator is not a substitute for the commercial judgement that pricing actually requires. The rate it returns is the floor the business needs. What you charge above that floor is a decision about positioning, client relationships, market conditions, and the value you deliver. Use the tool to make sure you are not inadvertently funding your clients' businesses out of your own savings, and use your own judgement for everything above that line.

Frequently asked questions

Why is a freelance rate so much higher than the hourly equivalent of a salary?

A salary package includes far more than the gross figure. Employers cover paid leave, sick pay, pension or provident contributions, medical aid, equipment, workspace, and payroll taxes. When you go freelance, every one of those costs lands on your side of the ledger. On top of that, you cannot bill every hour you work, because marketing, admin, pitching, invoicing, and learning all take up time. The result is that matching a salaried take-home figure requires an hourly rate that is typically one and a half to two times the naive hourly equivalent.

How many billable hours can a freelancer realistically sell per week?

Most full-time solo freelancers bill between twenty and twenty-eight hours a week. The remaining ten to twenty hours go to non-billable work: proposals, invoicing, client calls, admin, bookkeeping, learning new tools, and the unpaid gaps between paid projects. A freelancer who thinks they can bill thirty-five or forty hours a week every week is almost certainly overestimating. Start with twenty-five as an upper bound, then adjust based on the invoices you actually issue.

What does this calculator mean by business overhead?

Overhead is everything the business spends to exist, separate from what the freelancer personally keeps. For a Nigerian or South African freelancer working from home, it includes internet and data costs, backup power or generator fuel, a portion of rent and electricity for the workspace, laptop and phone depreciation, cloud software, cloud storage, banking fees, professional accounting, tax compliance, and any insurance. Add a line for the tools specific to your trade: design software, video editing licences, development environments, or equipment. Depending on the service and location, monthly overhead for a solo freelancer commonly runs between five and twenty per cent of revenue.

How do I handle tax as a Nigerian freelancer?

Nigeria taxes self-employment income through Personal Income Tax, administered by the Federal Inland Revenue Service for individuals earning above the statutory threshold or by the relevant state internal revenue service. Freelancers typically register for a Tax Identification Number, file annual returns, and may be required to register for Value Added Tax if turnover exceeds the statutory threshold. The exact rates, bands, and registration rules change from year to year, and the treatment of foreign-currency income adds further complexity. Use this calculator's tax field as a rough planning allowance and confirm your actual obligations with a qualified Nigerian tax adviser.

How do I handle tax as a South African freelancer?

South Africa taxes sole proprietors under the standard personal income tax system administered by the South African Revenue Service. Freelancers register as provisional taxpayers, submit two provisional tax returns each year, and file a final ITR12 return. If annual turnover falls below the small business threshold, a freelancer may also be able to register for Turnover Tax, which simplifies the compliance process. Rules change frequently, and the treatment of foreign-currency income earned from overseas clients has specific requirements. Treat the calculator's tax field as a planning allowance and confirm your actual obligations with a registered South African tax practitioner.

Can I price in a foreign currency while living in Nigeria or South Africa?

Yes, and many freelancers do. International clients on platforms like Upwork, Fiverr, Toptal, or direct retainers usually pay in US dollars, euros, or pounds, which often makes budgeting easier in an environment of local currency volatility. Whether you display a dollar rate, a rand rate, or a naira rate is a commercial decision. What matters is that the underlying hourly figure you charge is the same in real value regardless of the currency label. Use the currency selector to see the rate in the local currency for your own planning, and consider invoicing international clients in their currency for smoother payment.

Should I publish my hourly rate on my website?

It depends on your market and your client mix. Publishing a minimum rate on your portfolio page filters out enquiries that were never going to convert and saves both sides time. It can also anchor the negotiation in your favour. The downside is that it removes the ability to price flexibly for high-value clients or unusual project scopes. A common middle ground is to publish a starting-from figure or a project minimum rather than a fixed hourly rate, which signals your positioning without locking you into a number for every engagement.

How do I price a fixed project rather than an hourly rate?

Estimate the hours the project will actually take, including research, client communication, revisions, testing, and delivery, then multiply those hours by your minimum hourly rate. Add any direct project costs such as travel, printing, subcontractors, or licences. Then add a contingency of ten to twenty per cent, because projects almost always take longer than the first estimate, and scope tends to grow once the client sees an early draft. The total is your fixed quote. Clients appreciate the certainty, and you retain a margin for the changes that will inevitably appear.

How do I raise my rates with clients I already have?

Give at least thirty days of notice in writing, keep the message short and courteous, and state the new rate plainly without over-explaining. You can mention a specific reason if it helps, such as expanded skills, higher market rates for your specialism, or rising operational costs, but a single sentence is enough. Expect to lose one or two clients when the new rate takes effect, and price the increase so that the extra revenue more than covers the loss. Clients who value the work will usually accept a reasonable increase; those who do not were probably not the right fit for the rate you actually need.

Does the tool store my income figures or client data?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the currency, rounded income target, monthly overhead, tax rate, buffer rate, weeks off, billable hours per week, and the computed hourly rate is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. No name, no client information, no bank details, and no history.

Why does the recommended rate often look higher than I expected?

Because most freelancers, especially in their first two years, start from a rate they think the market will accept rather than a rate the business needs. The calculator does not care what the market will accept; it tells you the minimum rate that your income goal, overhead, tax, and profit buffer require. If the market for your specific service genuinely cannot support that number, the honest conclusion is that the current business model does not fund the lifestyle it is meant to fund, and one or more of the inputs has to change: overhead has to come down, billable hours have to go up, or the service itself has to be repositioned.
