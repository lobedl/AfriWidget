What this paraphrasing tool actually does

This tool takes a block of text and returns a cleaner, reworded version of it. You paste the original, pick a tone, and describe the reader you are writing for. The tool rephrases the passage in that direction and prints the draft on the same page. Nothing about the process is magic, and nothing about it replaces your own judgement. It is a fast, private first pass on a rewrite, so you can spend your time editing instead of staring at a blank page.

The draft is a starting point. It is not a finished document and it is not a decision about meaning. Every paraphrase still has to be checked against the original to confirm that the important qualifiers, numbers, names, and technical terms are still in place. If the original text says that something may reduce a risk, the rewrite should not say that it will reduce the risk. That difference is easy to lose in a reword, and it changes the substance of the sentence.

Everything on this page runs inside your browser. When you press the button, only an anonymous summary of your request is sent to a small serverless function that generates the draft and then stops. No account, no login, no history. Nothing on our side records the text you pasted, the tone you chose, or the draft you received.

Why California writers and students especially benefit

California holds some of the largest and most active writing communities in the world. The University of California system, the Cal State system, Stanford, and the California community colleges together enrol more than three million students, and a large share of them write in English as a second language. Beyond the campuses, Los Angeles and the San Francisco Bay Area sustain some of the biggest concentrations of screenwriters, technical writers, content teams, and independent journalists anywhere. A paraphrasing tool that produces a clean first draft is useful in all of those settings, but only if the person using it understands the rules that apply to their context.

Academic integrity is the first thing to sort out. UC campuses, Cal State campuses, Stanford, and the community colleges each publish their own policies, and instructors inside the same department often take different positions on assisted writing tools. Some allow grammar, clarity, and paraphrase tools as long as you edit the result. Some require you to disclose the tool you used. Some prohibit generative tools on certain assignments entirely, especially in courses where the point is to see your own reasoning. The syllabus is the document that governs your specific course, and the safest move is always to ask the instructor before you submit.

California also sits at the centre of the modern privacy conversation. CCPA and CPRA give California residents specific rights over personal information, and while this tool is not a personal-information processor in the sense those laws regulate, the practical rule is the same everywhere. Strip names, IDs, phone numbers, client details, and anything under a non-disclosure agreement before pasting. If you are paraphrasing a client's internal document, paraphrase the language, not their confidential data.

For professional writers, the California context runs through the entertainment and tech industries. A screenwriter in Burbank reworking a scene, a technical writer in San Jose cleaning up API documentation, a product marketer in San Francisco smoothing a launch page, and a journalist in Los Angeles rewriting a press release for a local audience all benefit from the same three-step workflow: paste the draft, choose tone and reader, review the result against the source. The tool does not replace their voice, and it should not try to.

Meaning first, wording second

Paraphrasing means expressing an idea in new words and sentence structures while keeping the original meaning accurate. That sounds simple until you try it on a careful sentence. The interesting work is in the small words that carry the argument: may, often, only, unless, approximately, at least, on average. A rewrite that quietly removes one of those qualifiers can invert the meaning of a whole paragraph, even when the grammar looks clean.

The safe approach is to read the original twice before you paste it. On the first pass, identify the main claim and the subject performing the action. On the second, note any conditions, caveats, or ranges. If the sentence says "studies suggest", keep the word suggest. If it says "for most users", keep most. If it says "under normal operating conditions", keep that condition. Meaning is not just the verb and the noun; it is the entire scaffolding that holds the claim up.

Once the rewrite comes back, place the two versions side by side. Read them sentence by sentence and check that no qualification has been silently dropped. Compare any figure, unit, date, name, and technical term character by character. Restore quotation marks around direct quotations. Restore citation markers if the tool has moved them. The paraphrase is only useful if it still says what the original said, and the review step is where that gets decided.

A practical rule. If you cannot point to the sentence in the original that justifies each sentence in the rewrite, the rewrite has drifted. Go back, read the source again, and adjust the wording rather than the meaning.

Audience, tone, and the reader test

The same idea can be written in several different ways depending on who is going to read it. A customer service email, a graduate seminar paper, a pitch deck slide, and a technical specification all draw on the same raw information but present it in very different registers. The audience and tone fields on this page help the tool move in the right direction, but the final decision about register belongs to you.

A clear and professional tone works for most business writing. It uses measured language, avoids jargon where plain words are available, and keeps sentences reasonably short. A plain and friendly tone suits customer communication, internal team notes, and social copy. An academic and precise tone preserves hedged language and technical terms; it should not be flattened into conversational shorthand. A concise and direct tone strips filler and puts the action early. A warm and conversational tone is useful for newsletters, blog posts, and community updates, where a slightly personal register earns the reader's attention.

Once you have the draft, read it aloud. If a sentence is hard to say, it will be hard to read. If a paragraph opens with a long subordinate clause, the reader will have to hold the meaning in their head before they reach the main verb. Small edits to sentence order and length often do more for clarity than swapping individual words. The reader test is simple: could someone who does not already know the subject follow the sentence on a single reading? If not, the rewrite has not finished its job.

Structure changes that improve clarity

Weak paraphrasing often swaps individual words while keeping the same sentence shape. The result sounds different without reading differently, and it can still be too close to the source to be useful. Stronger rewrites change the structure because the new audience or purpose actually calls for it. There are a handful of moves that improve readability in almost any context.

Switching between active and passive voice is one. Active voice puts the actor at the front of the sentence and usually reads more directly: "The team reviewed the report." Passive voice moves the actor to the end or removes them entirely, which is sometimes what you want: "The report was reviewed before publication." The right choice depends on whether the reader needs to know who did the thing or only what happened.

Splitting a long sentence into two shorter ones is another. English readers hold meaning in working memory, and a sentence that runs past about twenty-five words starts to strain that memory. Two short sentences often land better than one long one, even when the information is identical. The reverse move, combining two very short sentences, can also help when the second is a natural consequence of the first.

Moving a condition to the front of the sentence is a third. "If the server is unavailable, the system retries the request" reads more cleanly than "The system retries the request if the server is unavailable," because the reader knows the condition before they hear the action. Reordering the supporting points inside a paragraph, so that the strongest point comes first, is a fourth. None of these moves change the underlying meaning. They change how the meaning lands.

Ethical use: citation, fair use, and disclosure

Paraphrasing does not remove the obligation to credit the source. If the underlying idea belongs to someone else, and you present it as your own without a citation, that is still plagiarism even when the wording is entirely new. The rule is the same in academic work, journalism, and business writing, though the specific consequences differ. In a university context, the faculty handbook will say exactly what counts and what does not. In publishing, the rules are set by professional norms and, for certain kinds of use, by copyright law.

On the copyright side, the U.S. Copyright Office maintains a fair use index that lists the four factors courts weigh when deciding whether an unlicensed use is permissible: the purpose of the use, the nature of the copyrighted work, the amount used, and the effect on the market for the original. Paraphrasing a short passage for commentary or criticism is more likely to fall inside fair use than paraphrasing an entire book for republication. The safe move, when the stakes are high, is to consult a lawyer or the rights holder before you publish.

Disclosure is the third piece. In academic settings, some instructors now require students to disclose any assisted writing tools they used, including grammar checkers, paraphrasing tools, and AI drafts. In journalism, many outlets have written policies about whether a paraphrase tool can be used at all and how the use must be documented. In business, contracts and client agreements sometimes say whether any part of a deliverable can be created with an assistant tool. Read the applicable policy, follow it exactly, and note the tool in your own records.

The final ethical point is about truth. A paraphrase should make a sentence easier to understand, not obscure a risk, soften a warning, or hide a commitment that the original text made clear. If a policy says that a service has limits, the rewrite should keep the limits. If a contract says a deadline is firm, the rewrite should not turn it into an aspiration. Clarity that helps readers understand the truth is a service. Clarity that hides the truth is the opposite, and no amount of polish makes it acceptable.

A worked example

Suppose a graduate student in a public health programme at a California university is summarising a paper for a seminar. The original sentence reads: "Regular physical activity may reduce the risk of developing type 2 diabetes in adults over 45, although the effect varies by baseline fitness and diet quality."

The student pastes the sentence into the tool and selects academic and precise tone, with graduate students as the audience. A reasonable draft would keep the qualifier may, keep the age group, keep the two named variables of baseline fitness and diet quality, and keep the connection between the activity and the outcome. Nothing about the sentence should become more confident than the original. Nothing should be dropped for the sake of brevity.

Now suppose a product marketer in San Francisco is rewriting a product brief for a launch page aimed at small business owners. The original brief reads: "The update is designed to reduce the average onboarding time by approximately 20 per cent for teams with fewer than ten seats, based on internal testing in the first quarter."

With a plain and friendly tone and small business owners as the audience, a reasonable draft would keep approximately, keep the seat limit, keep the internal-testing caveat, and keep the first-quarter timeframe. The rewrite might turn the sentence around for readability, but the numbers and the qualifiers do not change. A launch page that quietly drops the caveat is a bigger problem than a launch page that reads slightly less punchy.

The two examples point to the same rule. The rewrite serves the reader. It does not change what the original said.

Common mistakes when paraphrasing

A handful of problems come up again and again, and recognising them early saves a lot of editing.

Chasing synonyms rather than rethinking the sentence. A paraphrase that swaps "important" for "significant" and stops there is barely a paraphrase. It usually reads worse than the original and can still be too close to the source to count as new writing.

Dropping qualifiers. The words may, often, only, unless, and approximately are load-bearing. Remove them and a cautious statement turns into a confident one, which is a change of meaning, not a change of wording.

Losing the citation. If the original text referenced a source, the rewrite should keep the reference. In academic writing, the citation travels with the idea, not with the sentence.

Turning every sentence the same length. A paragraph of uniform sentences reads mechanically. Varying length is part of what makes prose feel human, and a rewrite that flattens the rhythm is usually a step backwards.

Assuming the tool has preserved nuance. It has not, unless you check. The draft is a hypothesis about what a clean rewrite looks like. The verification step is yours.

Using the tool on text you do not have permission to edit. This includes client documents covered by an NDA, unpublished manuscripts from other authors, and copyrighted material you intend to republish. If you are unsure, ask before you paste.

Publishing without a final pass in your own voice. The rewrite is clean, but it is not yours. Read it aloud, adjust the vocabulary to your natural register, and take responsibility for the last pass.

Where this tool stops

This tool produces a first draft. It does not check whether the rewrite preserves the meaning of the source, whether a claim is accurate, whether a citation is correctly formatted, or whether the passage you pasted was covered by an agreement you should not have breached. Those checks belong to the writer, and no tool can take them over without also taking over the responsibility that goes with them.

It also does not decide academic integrity questions for you. Whether an assisted paraphrase is allowed on a specific assignment is a policy question for your instructor and your institution, not a technical question for the tool. Some courses allow this kind of work, some require disclosure, and some prohibit it entirely. Read the syllabus, ask if you are unsure, and follow the rule your course has set.

What the tool gives you is a fast, private first pass, so that the expensive part of the work, judgement, editing, and voice, has a clean starting point. The last pass is still yours, and it should be. A paraphrase that has been read out loud, checked against the source, and adjusted in your own words is a much better sentence than one that came straight out of a text box.

Frequently asked questions

Will paraphrasing with this tool get me in trouble at a University of California or Cal State campus?

That depends entirely on your instructor and your course syllabus. UC, Cal State, Stanford, and the California community colleges each set their own academic integrity rules, and instructors inside the same department often take different positions on assisted writing tools. Some allow grammar and clarity tools, some require you to disclose any assistant you used, and some prohibit the use of generative tools entirely on certain assignments. Read the syllabus, ask the instructor before you submit anything, and never present a rewritten passage as your own original prose without following the rules your institution has set.

Does paraphrasing avoid a plagiarism or copyright problem?

No. Changing the wording of someone else's idea does not remove the obligation to credit the source. In academic writing, that means a citation. In business, editorial, or publishing work, it means checking whether the underlying text is protected by copyright and whether your use falls under fair use or requires a licence. The U.S. Copyright Office's fair use index is a good starting point for the U.S. side, and the rules change again once you publish outside the country.

What personal information should I keep out of the paraphrase box?

Anything you would not want to see a month later in a stranger's hands. In California, CCPA and CPRA give residents specific rights over personal information, but the practical rule is the same everywhere: do not paste passwords, government ID numbers, medical details, private client data, unreleased contracts, or anything under a non-disclosure agreement. Strip names and identifiers before paraphrasing, and paste only what is needed to fix the wording.

Can the tool preserve citations, quotes, and technical terms?

It can keep what you paste, but it cannot know which parts of the text are load-bearing. Always review the draft side by side with the source. Restore every citation marker, keep direct quotations inside quotation marks, and confirm that figures, units, names, dates, and technical terms are unchanged. A paraphrase that quietly drops a qualification like may, often, only, or unless can invert the meaning of a sentence even when the grammar looks clean.

Is the rewrite generated locally or on a server?

Only an anonymous summary of your request leaves your browser. The form and the output panel sit on your device, and the paraphrase is generated by a small serverless function that runs only when you press the button. No account, no email, no session identifier, and no history. If you would rather not send anything at all, you can still use the page as a structured way to draft the rewrite yourself.

Does the tool work for non-English text or mixed-language drafts?

It works best with English, and reasonably well with languages that share a similar sentence structure. For heavily mixed text, or for languages with very different grammar such as Mandarin or Arabic, the draft will need more editing. Treat it as a starting point rather than a final. If your work is bilingual by design, you may find it faster to paraphrase each language separately rather than mixing them in one request.

How long should the text I paste be?

One to three paragraphs is the sweet spot. Long enough that the tool has real context, short enough that the rewrite stays close to the source. If you need to paraphrase a long essay or report, paste it section by section rather than in one block. Paragraph-by-paragraph rewrites are easier to review, easier to keep faithful to the original, and easier to adjust tone if the first attempt drifts.

Will the rewritten text sound like me?

Only after you edit it. The tool produces a clean draft, not your voice. Read the result aloud, cut anything that sounds stiff, and replace generic phrasing with the vocabulary you actually use. For client work, keep a short style note beside the draft with three or four words or phrases your brand prefers, and make those adjustments manually. The last pass belongs to you, and that pass is what turns a draft into your writing.

Does the tool store a history or share what I generate?

No. Every request is independent, and closing the tab ends the session. Nothing on our side records the text you pasted, the draft you received, or the tone and audience you chose. If you want to keep a draft, copy it while it is on the page and paste it into your own document, notes app, or writing tool of choice.

Is this tool useful outside California?

Yes. The paraphrasing guidance applies to writers anywhere, and the workflow of paste, choose a tone and audience, review the draft, edit in your own voice is the same in Lagos, London, Toronto, and Berlin. The California context in the article is a starting point for readers who want to know which academic integrity rules, privacy regulations, and copyright frameworks apply locally, but the same principles of honesty, citation, and review travel with you.
