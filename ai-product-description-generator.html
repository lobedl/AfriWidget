What this generator actually does

This tool takes the facts you provide about a product and turns them into a first draft of a description. You supply a product name, an audience, the features and materials, the main customer benefit, and a preferred tone. The generator rearranges those inputs into a description a shopper can read and act on, then prints it on the same page.

The draft is a first pass, not a finished listing. Every marketplace applies its own rules to titles, claims, images, and prohibited categories. Every brand has its own voice. Every product has its own specifics that only the seller knows. The tool gives you a structured starting point so you spend your time editing for those specifics instead of staring at a blank box.

Everything on this page runs inside your browser. When you press the button, only an anonymous summary of your form fields is sent to a small serverless function that generates the draft and stops. No account, no login, no history. Nothing on our side records your product name, your features list, or the draft you received.

Feature versus benefit, and why the difference matters

The single most useful distinction in product copy is the difference between a feature and a benefit. A feature is something the product has. A benefit is why the customer cares. A stainless steel bottle is a feature. A bottle that keeps water cold through a long commute is a benefit. Both belong in a description, but they do different work, and confusing them is the most common reason product pages read as dry lists of specifications.

The features section is where the buyer goes to verify. Capacity, materials, dimensions, colours, care instructions, and included parts are the details that answer the questions a shopper has after the photos have done their job. Put the specifics there and keep them accurate. "750ml capacity" and "double-wall stainless steel" mean something. "High quality materials" does not.

The benefit field is where the buyer decides whether the product is for them. It should describe a change the product actually supports, not a fantasy outcome. A desk organiser can keep frequently used items within reach. It should not promise to transform the way a person works. A skincare product can describe its texture and how it fits into a routine. It should not claim medical results unless those claims are supported by evidence and reviewed against the advertising rules that apply where you sell.

A useful test. Read the features list and ask whether a stranger could verify each line by looking at the product. If they could, the feature is doing its job. If they could not, the line is marketing and belongs in the benefit section, described carefully and honestly.

The facts the copy needs from you

The quality of the output depends entirely on the quality of the input. Feed the tool vague notes and you will get a vague description. Feed it real numbers, materials, and use cases and you will get a draft that a shopper could act on. There is no shortcut around that, and no amount of AI can invent specifics a seller has not provided.

For a physical product, the essentials are the name, the material construction, the dimensions or capacity, the colour and finish options, any included accessories or parts, the care instructions, and any certifications that matter to the buyer. For a digital product, the essentials are the format, the file size, the licence terms, the software or platform it is designed for, and any update or support policy. For a service, the essentials are the scope, the deliverable, the timeline, and the boundary of what is not included.

Keep the notes short and factual. You do not need to write full sentences. A comma-separated list of specifics gives the tool more to work with than a padded paragraph. If a product has a warranty, a return window, or a delivery promise, check the exact policy before you type it, because whatever you put in the form may end up in the draft and later on the page.

Writing for the marketplace and the brand page

A product description is not one document. It is at least two, and sometimes three or four. Marketplace listings reward brevity because buyers compare quickly and the format is consistent across sellers. A brand's own product page can be longer because the reader has already chosen to look. A physical product insert or a package label has yet another register, because the reader already owns the product and is looking for care instructions rather than a pitch.

On a marketplace, the first two lines are the pitch. The rest is verification. A buyer scanning four similar listings will decide in about three seconds whether to read on. Put the clearest fact or benefit first, then the top two or three features, then close with the practical details. The tone should be plain. Clever headlines belong in advertising, not in a listing that has to survive translation into five languages and a text-only display on a mobile phone.

On a brand's own product page, you have more room. You can open with a short paragraph that sets the context, follow with a benefit-led bullet list, close with specifications and care instructions, and add an FAQ for the questions that come up most often. The tone can carry more of the brand's voice, because you control the environment. The same copy that reads well on Shopify may need to be trimmed for Amazon and expanded for a retail catalogue.

Keep a small notes file with the tone guidelines your brand uses. Two or three sentences about vocabulary, sentence length, and the words you avoid is enough. That file matters more than any single description, because it keeps the brand consistent across every product you sell.

Search, honesty, and platform rules

Search engines and marketplaces both reward useful content, and both punish filler. The honest move is to use the vocabulary a real buyer would type: the product name, the material, the use case, the size, the colour, and the compatible model where relevant. Put those terms where they belong, in a natural sentence, and do not repeat them in every paragraph hoping for a ranking bump.

Keyword stuffing does two things at once. It makes the description harder to read, which reduces conversion, and it can trigger marketplace penalties, especially on Amazon, where the listing rules on repeated words and prohibited claims are enforced. A description that reads as if a person wrote it, that answers a shopper's question, and that uses the right category and attribute tags, will usually outperform a longer description written for a robot.

The other honesty concern is the claim. Modern advertising rules in most countries, from the Federal Trade Commission in the United States to the Advertising Standards Authority in the United Kingdom, require that claims be truthful, not misleading, and supported by evidence. If you say a product lasts ten years, you need to be able to justify that. If you say it is the best on the market, you need to define what you are measuring. A careful paraphrase is one that would not embarrass you in front of a regulator, and the description draft is only the first of several steps toward that standard.

A worked example

Suppose a seller in Lagos is listing a reusable insulated water bottle aimed at commuters and gym members. She types the following into the form.

Product: reusable insulated water bottle. Audience: commuters, students, gym members in Lagos. Features: 750ml capacity, double-wall stainless steel, matte finish, screw-on lid with silicone seal, BPA-free, hand-wash recommended, comes in black and sand. Benefit: keeps drinks cold through a long commute. Tone: clear and persuasive.

A reasonable draft would open with the bottle and the commuter use case, follow with the capacity and construction, note the colours and included lid seal, mention the BPA-free material, close with a care note, and invite the buyer to check the size against their bag or cup holder. Nothing exaggerated. Nothing invented. Every claim traceable back to the form.

Now suppose the same seller is listing for a premium retail catalogue rather than a marketplace card. She would choose the premium tone and slightly longer sentences, and she might add a line about the matte finish catching the light. She would keep the same facts and the same honesty. The register shifts, the substance does not.

Common mistakes that cost sales

The same handful of mistakes show up on product pages everywhere, and they cost more than most sellers realise.

Leading with adjectives instead of facts. "Premium quality, high performance, best in class" tells a shopper nothing. A specific material, size, or use case gives them something to compare against.

Hiding the practical details. If the bottle is not dishwasher safe, say so. If the dress runs small, say so. If assembly is required, say so. The buyer finds out eventually, and the review they leave will be honest about it.

Overpromising on the benefit. Claims that cannot be supported are a legal risk and a customer trust risk at the same time. Keep the benefit to a realistic change, and let the features carry the weight.

Copying a competitor's description. Marketplaces notice duplicate text, search engines do too, and the copy rarely fits a different product as well as it fits the original.

Ignoring the mobile layout. Most shoppers read on a phone. Long paragraphs scroll off the screen, bullet lists read better, and the first two lines carry the whole pitch.

Forgetting the call to action. A description should end with something the buyer can do: check the size chart, message a question, add to cart, or read the FAQ. An open-ended ending lets the shopper drift to the next listing.

Assuming the draft is finished. It never is. The generator gives you a clean first pass. The product facts, the brand voice, and the compliance check all come from you, and they are what turns a draft into a listing that sells.

Where this tool stops

This tool produces a first draft. It does not check whether your product description complies with marketplace rules, advertising standards, or consumer protection law. It does not know the exact listing format the platform you sell on requires, and it does not verify the facts you supply. Those checks belong to the seller, and no tool can take them over without also taking over the responsibility that goes with them.

It also does not know your brand voice, your competitors, or the specific audience you are trying to reach on a given platform. It works from the notes you give it. If those notes are incomplete, the draft will be incomplete, and no amount of polish will fix a description that is missing the specifics a buyer needs.

What the tool gives you is a faster start. A structured draft that respects the reader, so you spend your time on the two things that only the seller can do: getting the facts right and taking responsibility for the claim. The last pass is yours, and it should be. A product description that has been read aloud, checked against the specifications, and adjusted for the specific marketplace it lives on is a better listing than one that came straight out of a text box.

Frequently asked questions

Can I paste the AI description straight into my Shopify or Amazon store?

Treat the draft as a first pass, not a finished listing. Read it against the facts you typed in, check every number, material, measurement, and claim, and remove anything you cannot support. Amazon and Shopify each have their own rules on restricted words, sizing, and prohibited claims, and a strong draft still has to clear those rules before you publish. The last edit is where you take responsibility for the description, so do not skip it.

Does the tool invent product features or performance claims?

No. It rearranges the notes you give it. If you do not type that the bottle keeps drinks cold for 24 hours, the draft will not claim that. If you type it as a marketing claim you cannot prove, the draft may repeat it, which is exactly why you should not paste unverified claims into the form in the first place. Keep the input to facts the product can support, and the output will stay on safe ground.

How is this different from just using ChatGPT for the same job?

The structure is different. This tool is built around the specific fields a product page needs: name, audience, features and materials, main benefit, and tone. That shape nudges you to supply the concrete details the copy will use, which is the part most people skip when they write a quick prompt. It is also private by default and does not require an account, which matters if you are working on a client product or an unreleased launch.

Should my description be short or long?

Long enough to answer the questions a real shopper has, and no longer. Marketplace cards usually work best with a short, scannable paragraph because buyers compare quickly. A dedicated product page on your own store can carry more detail because the reader has already committed to looking. Either way, put the clearest value near the top, follow with practical specifications, and close with a clear next step. Padding the middle with adjectives rarely helps anyone.

Is the description optimized for search engines?

The draft uses the vocabulary you supply, which means the keywords you care about are usually present, but it is not a keyword-stuffing tool and it should not be used like one. Search engines reward pages that answer a shopper's question clearly. The best SEO move on a product page is an honest title, a specific description, correct category and attribute tags, and clean structured data, not a sentence repeated in five different ways.

Can I use this for Jumia, Konga, or other African marketplaces?

Yes. The tool is not tied to a specific platform. The fields work the same way whether you are writing for Jumia, Konga, Kilimall, Takealot, or a store you run yourself on WooCommerce or Shopify. The rules each marketplace applies to claims, images, and prohibited categories are different, so check the seller handbook for the platform you are listing on before you publish the description.

What if my product is handmade or has natural variation?

Say so, and say it early. A short line like small variations in colour or grain are normal in this handmade item prevents a bad surprise later and protects you from returns that were not really about quality. Use the features and materials field to note the material, the process, and the natural range the buyer should expect. Buyers who understand the variation are usually the happiest customers.

Does the tool store what I generate?

No. The form and the output sit on your device, and the draft is generated by a small serverless function that runs only when you press the button. No account, no email, no session identifier, and no history. If you want to keep a description, copy it while it is on the page and paste it into your product catalogue or a notes app.

Will the description sound like my brand?

Only after you edit it. The tool produces a clean draft, not your voice. Pick the tone closest to your brand, generate the first version, then read it aloud and replace anything that sounds generic. If you sell premium products, keep sentences slightly longer and more measured. If you sell practical everyday goods, keep them short and direct. The last pass is what makes the copy sound like the store it belongs to.

Is this tool useful outside e-commerce?

Yes, with a small adjustment. The same structure works for service listings, catalogue entries, product inserts, and pitch decks, because all of them need to explain a thing to a specific reader in a way that is honest and clear. If you are describing a service rather than a physical product, use the features field for what the service actually does and the benefit field for the change the customer experiences. The rest of the workflow is the same.
