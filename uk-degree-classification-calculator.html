What this calculator does

This tool takes the marks you have entered for your Year 2 and Year 3 modules, applies the credit weighting inside each year, then combines the two year averages using your university's year weighting ratio, and returns three things: your composite degree mark, your honours classification band, and the marks you need across your remaining final year credits to reach the next classification. If the composite lands in the borderline zone below a classification threshold, the tool also checks whether the majority-credit condition that most universities apply would elevate you to the higher band.

There are two parts to the calculation that catch students out. The first is the year weighting ratio, which sets how much of the final mark depends on Year 2 versus Year 3. The second is the credit weighting inside each year, which decides how much a thirty-credit dissertation matters compared with a fifteen-credit elective. Getting either of those wrong produces a number that looks plausible but is off by several points, and the classification outcome can be different at the boundary. This calculator keeps both layers explicit, so the running total matches what the exam board will produce.

Every calculation runs inside your browser. No mark, no module name, and no personal detail is uploaded. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the weighting model, the number of modules entered, the Year 2 and Year 3 averages, the composite mark, the classification band, and the borderline status is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

The UK honours classification bands

UK undergraduate degrees are awarded with a classification that summarises the composite mark across the final years of the programme. The bands are the same across almost every university in England, Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland, and the same pattern is used in most British-pattern institutions overseas. What varies between institutions is the year weighting ratio and the borderline rules, not the classification bands themselves.

ClassificationComposite markCommon short form
First-Class Honours70.00 to 1001st
Upper Second-Class Honours60.00 to 69.992:1
Lower Second-Class Honours50.00 to 59.992:2
Third-Class Honours40.00 to 49.993rd
Ordinary Degree (no honours)35.00 to 39.99Pass
Fail (below honours)Below 35.00Fail

The First-Class threshold at seventy per cent is a national convention rather than a fixed rule. A very small number of institutions have moved the boundary slightly in either direction in particular years, and some professional programmes apply a different internal threshold for accreditation reasons. The classification bands shown here are the standard bands used at almost every UK university, and the academic regulations for your specific programme are the definitive source.

The bands matter beyond the certificate. Most competitive graduate schemes, postgraduate Master's programmes, and professional training routes in law, accountancy, and consulting set a 2:1 as a minimum entry requirement. A 2:2 keeps many options open but closes a meaningful number of doors. The gap between a 68 and a 70 is one classification band, and the gap between a 58 and a 60 is another, which is why the borderline rules deserve careful attention in the final year of study.

How year weighting ratios work

The year weighting ratio sets how much of the final composite mark is contributed by Year 2 and how much by Year 3. Universities publish a single ratio per programme, and the ratio applies to the year averages, not to the individual module marks. That means a module mark in Year 2 is first averaged with the rest of the Year 2 modules using credit weighting, and only then does the Year 2 average get multiplied by the year weighting.

The most common ratio in the UK is 1:2, which gives Year 2 a third of the final mark and Year 3 two thirds. A smaller group of universities uses 1:3, which reduces the Year 2 contribution to a quarter and increases the Year 3 contribution to three quarters. Some institutions use 1:1 and give both years equal weight. Integrated master's programmes such as MEng and MSci typically use a three-stage ratio where Year 2 contributes around twenty per cent and the two final years contribute around forty per cent each.

Year 2 average = Sum(module mark × module credit) ÷ Total Year 2 credits
Year 3 average = Sum(module mark × module credit) ÷ Total Year 3 credits
Composite mark = (Year 2 average × Year 2 weight) + (Year 3 average × Year 3 weight)

The practical effect of the year weighting is that a strong final year moves the composite further than an equally strong second year. A student who improves by ten percentage points between Year 2 and Year 3 gains more from that improvement on a 1:2 ratio than on a 1:1 ratio. Conversely, a student whose Year 2 was weaker than Year 3 can recover more ground if the university uses a 1:3 ratio, because the Year 2 average carries less weight in that model.

Credit weighting inside each year

Inside each year, every module mark is multiplied by the credit value of the module before the average is taken. A twenty-credit module therefore has twice the influence of a ten-credit module on the year average, and a forty-credit dissertation has four times the influence of a ten-credit option. This is where most of the strategic leverage in the final year sits, because a dissertation mark that moves by a few percentage points can shift the year average by more than a small optional module ever could.

A typical three-year UK honours degree totals three hundred and sixty credits, divided into three levels of one hundred and twenty credits each. The Level 5 year (usually the second year of study) and the Level 6 year (usually the third year) contribute to the classification under the year weighting ratio. The Level 4 year (first year) is normally qualifying only, meaning that the candidate must pass the required number of credits to progress, but the marks themselves do not enter the final average.

A candidate sitting six twenty-credit modules across the year has a straightforward credit distribution, and each module carries equal weight inside the year. A candidate with a forty-credit dissertation plus four twenty-credit taught modules is in a different position, because the dissertation alone accounts for a third of the year and the margin for error in the other modules is correspondingly tighter. The calculator's credit-weighted average handles both structures without any special setup, but the strategic implication of the dissertation weighting is worth knowing before the final year begins.

The borderline and majority-credit rules

A candidate whose composite mark sits just below a classification threshold enters the borderline zone, and most UK universities apply a discretionary rule that can elevate the candidate to the higher class. The borderline zone is usually defined as the one or two percentage points immediately below the threshold. For the First-Class threshold at seventy per cent, the zone typically runs from sixty-eight per cent to sixty-nine point nine per cent. For the 2:1 threshold at sixty per cent, the zone typically runs from fifty-eight to fifty-nine point nine per cent.

The most common borderline condition is the majority-credit rule. Under this rule, the candidate is elevated if at least half, and often sixty credits, of the final year credits carry marks at or above the higher classification band. A candidate sitting at sixty-nine per cent who has sixty of their one hundred and twenty final year credits at seventy per cent or above will usually be elevated to a First. A candidate at the same composite mark with only forty credits at First level will usually receive a 2:1.

Other borderline conditions vary between institutions. Some universities elevate candidates whose dissertation mark is at the higher classification band regardless of the credit distribution elsewhere. Some apply an exit velocity rule that considers the improvement between Year 2 and Year 3, especially where the improvement is large. A small number of institutions use a weighted combination of these conditions. The exam board has the final say in every borderline case, and the academic regulations for the programme list the specific rules that apply.

Borderline rules are discretionary, not automatic. A candidate who satisfies the majority-credit condition is normally elevated, but the exam board retains the discretion to decide each case on its full academic record. The calculator applies the majority-credit rule as a check, not as a guarantee of the outcome.

A worked example

Take a BSc Economics candidate with four Year 2 modules of thirty credits each and five Year 3 modules totalling one hundred and twenty credits, including a forty-credit dissertation. The weighting model used by the university is the 1:2 ratio. The marks are as follows.

ModuleCreditsMarkWeighted points
Year 2 Microeconomic Theory30641,920
Year 2 Macroeconomic Theory30621,860
Year 2 Econometrics30581,740
Year 2 Financial Markets30682,040
Year 3 Applied Econometrics30722,160
Year 3 International Trade30742,220
Year 3 Behavioural Economics20711,420
Year 3 Senior Dissertation40763,040

The Year 2 average is the sum of the Year 2 weighted points divided by the Year 2 credits. The sum is seven thousand five hundred and sixty, and the credits total one hundred and twenty, so the Year 2 average is sixty-three point zero zero per cent. The Year 3 average is the sum of the Year 3 weighted points divided by the Year 3 credits. The sum is eight thousand eight hundred and forty, and the credits total one hundred and twenty, so the Year 3 average is seventy-three point six seven per cent.

Applying the 1:2 weighting, the Year 2 contribution is sixty-three point zero zero multiplied by zero point three three three three, which is twenty-one point zero zero. The Year 3 contribution is seventy-three point six seven multiplied by zero point six six six seven, which is forty-nine point one one. The composite mark is seventy point one one per cent, which lands just above the First-Class threshold. The classification is First-Class Honours.

The margin is thin, and it is worth noting what produced it. The dissertation mark of seventy-six contributed three thousand and forty weighted points on its own, which is nearly a third of the Year 3 total. If the dissertation had come out at seventy-two instead, the Year 3 average would have dropped to about seventy-two point three three, the composite would have fallen to about sixty-nine point five six, and the outcome would have moved into the borderline zone at the lower edge of the First-Class band. The credit weighting on the dissertation is what decides the outcome in this case.

Now suppose the candidate had used the drop-lowest-module rule, which is available at some universities. The Year 2 lowest mark is Econometrics at fifty-eight, worth thirty credits, which exceeds the twenty-credit ceiling that most drop rules apply. Under a strict twenty-credit drop rule, the Econometrics module cannot be fully dropped, and the rule would not change the Year 2 average. A different candidate with a weaker ten or twenty-credit module in the mix would see a larger effect, which is why the rule is worth checking against the specific credit distribution in each year.

Common mistakes when reading your standing

A handful of recurring errors make a student's own estimate diverge from what the exam board will actually produce, and some of those errors have real consequences in the final year.

Using the wrong year weighting. The ratio is fixed in the academic regulations for the programme, and it does not always match what a lecturer quoted in class. A student who assumes a 1:2 ratio at a university that uses 1:3 will overestimate the impact of the second year and underestimate the importance of the final year. The difference at the boundary is often a full classification band, so the regulations should be read before using this calculator to plan.

Ignoring the credit value of the dissertation. The dissertation usually carries thirty or forty credits and therefore accounts for a third of the final year mark. A small change in the dissertation mark moves the year average by more than a similar change in a ten or twenty-credit taught module. Candidates who treat the dissertation as just another module can be surprised at the end of the year when a weaker dissertation pulls the composite well below the target.

Assuming Year 1 counts. At most universities in the UK, Year 1 is qualifying only. A student who invests heavily in Year 1 at the expense of the later years has spent effort on marks that do not appear on the final classification. The reverse mistake also happens: a student who assumes Year 1 does not count without checking can be caught out by the small number of programmes that apply a weighting to it.

Treating the borderline zone as a safety net. The borderline rules help some candidates, but they are discretionary and the conditions are specific. A candidate who assumes the exam board will elevate them at sixty-eight per cent without checking the majority-credit condition may find that the elevation does not apply. The tool checks the condition for the current inputs, but the exam board always retains the final decision, and the regulations list the exact conditions.

Neglecting the difference between capped resits and fresh marks. A resit mark is often capped at forty per cent, which means a successful resit still contributes only forty per cent to the weighted average for that module. A candidate planning their final year around the possibility of a resit should account for the cap when estimating the year average, because the resit pass cannot recover the original mark.

Reading the composite as a final certificate. The composite mark shown here is the calculation the exam board will perform on the marks they have recorded. The final classification that appears on the certificate is the exam board's decision after applying their own borderline rules, and in the very small number of cases where the candidate has extenuating circumstances or where the exam board finds a marking discrepancy, the recorded classification can differ from the calculated one. The certificate is the definitive document.

UK classifications and US GPA

When a UK graduate applies to a US graduate school or to an employer that uses a four-point GPA scale, the UK classification is converted to an approximate GPA. The conversion is not standardised and varies slightly between receiving institutions, but the pattern below is close to the one most graduate admissions offices apply.

UK classificationComposite markUS GPA (approx.)
First-Class Honours70% and above3.80 to 4.00
Upper Second (2:1)60% to 69.99%3.30 to 3.70
Lower Second (2:2)50% to 59.99%2.70 to 3.20
Third-Class Honours40% to 49.99%2.00 to 2.60

The conversion is not symmetric because the UK marking system compresses most of the useful range into the upper part of the scale. A UK mark of seventy per cent is a strong performance at the top of the national scale, whereas a US mark of seventy per cent is often a mid-range result. Graduate admissions offices that receive UK transcripts apply their own conversion tables, and the exact figure depends on which table they use. The bands here are guides for orientation rather than official equivalencies, and the receiving institution's conversion is the one that matters for the application.

Where this calculator stops

This tool estimates the composite degree mark and the classification band from the module marks and weighting ratio you enter, and it applies the majority-credit borderline rule as a check on the current inputs. It does not know your university's specific borderline conditions beyond the majority-credit rule, does not know whether your programme applies a small Year 1 weighting, and does not know whether your dissertation carries a particular moderation rule that is separate from the rest of the marks. It also does not model the effect of extenuating circumstances, deferred assessments, or the internal review process that some institutions apply at the classification stage.

The classification bands and the credit weighting inside each year match the standard UK framework as published by the Quality Assurance Agency and applied by almost every university in the sector. The exam board of your university produces the official composite mark and the official classification, and the transcript they issue is the document that governs. Use this calculator to see where you stand, to understand how much a single module or a single percentage point on the dissertation would move the composite, and to plan the final year effort with a clear view of what needs to improve.

Frequently asked questions

Does Year 1 count towards my final degree classification?

At most UK universities, Year 1 marks are qualifying only and do not contribute to the final degree classification. You must pass the required number of credits to progress to Year 2, but the average that appears on your final transcript is built from Year 2 and Year 3 marks only. A small number of institutions apply a small weighting to Year 1 in specific programmes, so the academic regulations for your specific course are the definitive source.

What are the UK honours degree classification bands?

The standard bands are First-Class Honours at seventy per cent and above, Upper Second-Class Honours at sixty to sixty-nine point nine per cent, Lower Second-Class Honours at fifty to fifty-nine point nine per cent, and Third-Class Honours at forty to forty-nine point nine per cent. Below forty per cent, the award is normally an ordinary degree without honours, and below thirty-five per cent the award is a fail with the option of a Certificate or Diploma of Higher Education where the credits allow.

How do the year weighting ratios work?

Universities apply a weighting ratio between Year 2 and Year 3 to produce the final composite mark. The most common ratios are one-to-two, meaning Year 2 counts for a third and Year 3 counts for two thirds, and one-to-three, meaning Year 2 counts for a quarter and Year 3 counts for three quarters. Some institutions use a fifty-fifty split, and integrated master's degrees usually weight the final two years more heavily than the second year. The academic regulations for your university state the exact ratio for your programme.

What is the majority-credit borderline rule?

When your composite average falls in the borderline zone just below a classification threshold, most UK universities apply a discretionary uplift if certain conditions are met. The most common condition is that at least half, and often sixty credits, of your final year credits carry marks at or above the higher classification band. Other conditions include a strong dissertation mark at the higher level or clear upward progression between Year 2 and Year 3. The exact borderline rule varies between institutions, and the exam board's decision is final in each case.

Can I drop my lowest module from the calculation?

Some universities apply a drop-lowest-module rule that removes the weakest mark from the credit-weighted average, usually up to twenty credits per year. The rule is most common in programmes where students take six or more modules per year, and it is written into the academic regulations rather than applied automatically. If your university uses it, tick the corresponding option in the calculator, and the Year 2 and Year 3 averages will be recomputed with the lowest credit load removed. Check the regulations to confirm the exact rule for your programme.

How does the classification convert to a US GPA?

The conversion is approximate and varies by institution. A UK First at seventy per cent or higher typically maps to a US GPA of around three point eight to four point zero. An Upper Second at sixty to sixty-nine per cent maps to around three point three to three point seven. A Lower Second at fifty to fifty-nine per cent maps to around two point seven to three point two. A Third at forty to forty-nine per cent maps to around two point zero to two point six. The receiving institution performs its own conversion in most cases, so the bands shown here are guides rather than official equivalencies.

What happens if I fail a module in Year 2 or Year 3?

A fail is usually a mark below forty per cent on a module. The university normally offers a resit attempt, and the resit mark is capped at forty per cent in most cases, which means the resit pass cannot improve the weighted average beyond the bare pass mark. Some universities award compensated credit if the overall year average is strong and the failed module is not a core or prerequisite module. Where compensation is not available and the resit is capped, the credit still counts but at the minimum passing mark.

Does this calculator store my marks or share my results?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the weighting model, the number of modules entered, the Year 2 and Year 3 averages, the final composite mark, the classification band, and the borderline status is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Your module names, individual marks, and any personal identifiers never leave the browser.
