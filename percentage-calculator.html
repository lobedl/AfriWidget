What this calculator does

This tool works out three of the most common percentage questions anyone can face. The first is what a given percentage of a number actually is, which is the calculation behind discounts, VAT, tips, sales tax, commission, and interest on a simple loan. The second is what percentage one number is of another, which is the calculation behind exam scores, savings goals, survey results, and progress tracking. The third is the percentage change between two values, which is the calculation behind price rises, salary increases, investment returns, and month-on-month growth.

The reason a dedicated percentage calculator is worth using rather than mental arithmetic or a phone's built-in tool is that percentages are the source of an enormous number of small, systematic mistakes. The most common one is choosing the wrong baseline, which turns a correct calculation into a misleading number. The second most common is confusing percentage points with percentage change, which turns a small movement into an apparently large one. This tool shows the working so both mistakes are easier to spot before they end up in a budget, a price list, or a business report.

Every calculation runs inside your browser. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored, and nothing is tracked. If the AI insight button is pressed, only an anonymous summary of the calculation type, the amount entered, the percentage entered, and the result is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

What a percentage actually is

The word percentage comes from the Latin per centum, meaning by the hundred. A percentage is therefore just a fraction whose denominator has been fixed at one hundred. Fifty per cent means fifty out of every hundred, which is the same as the fraction one half, and the same as the decimal zero point five. Twenty five per cent means twenty five out of every hundred, which is the same as one quarter, and the same as zero point two five.

Fixing the denominator at one hundred is what makes percentages useful. It turns every ratio into a common scale, so a discount on a small purchase and a discount on a large purchase can be compared directly. A shop offering twenty per cent off a low-priced item and a shop offering ten per cent off a high-priced item may still deliver very different total savings, and the percentage alone does not tell you which is the better deal. That is why the amount matters as much as the rate.

Percentages also make comparisons across time and place easier. A bank in Nigeria quoting a fourteen per cent annual interest rate, a bank in Kenya quoting a twelve per cent rate, and a bank in the United Kingdom quoting a six per cent rate are all describing the same kind of cost in a form that can be lined up side by side, even though the currencies, the tax treatments, and the regulations are different.

The three core percentage formulas

Three formulas cover almost every percentage question that comes up in everyday life.

Finding a percentage of a number. Multiply the number by the percentage, then divide by one hundred.

Percentage of a number = (Number × Percentage) ÷ 100

For a fifteen per cent tip on a forty thousand naira meal, multiply forty thousand by fifteen to get six hundred thousand, then divide by one hundred to get six thousand naira. That is the tip amount, not the total bill. To find the total, add the tip to the meal price.

Finding what percentage one number is of another. Divide the part by the whole, then multiply by one hundred.

Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100

If you completed thirty tasks out of a hundred and twenty, divide thirty by one hundred and twenty to get zero point two five, then multiply by one hundred to get twenty five per cent. The whole here must be the total reference amount, not a sub-part, or the answer will be wrong in a way that is not immediately obvious.

Finding the percentage change between two values. Subtract the original value from the new value, divide by the original value, then multiply by one hundred.

Percentage change = ((New value − Original value) ÷ Original value) × 100

Moving from eighty to one hundred is a twenty five per cent increase. Moving from one hundred back to eighty is a twenty per cent decrease. The two movements cover the same absolute distance of twenty, but the percentages differ because the baselines differ. This asymmetry surprises people, but it is a direct consequence of the formula and not a mistake.

Discounts, VAT, and final prices

Discounts and taxes are where percentages most often end up on a receipt, and they are also where the calculations most often go wrong. The discount amount is calculated on the original price, but the final price is what remains after the discount is subtracted. A twenty five per cent discount on eighty thousand shillings is twenty thousand shillings off, leaving sixty thousand shillings before any additional charges. The percentage rate is the same whether the price is high or low, but the cash saved changes with the amount.

Value Added Tax works the other way round. The VAT amount is calculated on the price before tax, and the price the customer pays is the sum of the net price and the VAT. On a net price of ten thousand naira with Nigeria's seven point five per cent VAT, the VAT amount is seven hundred and fifty naira, and the total the customer pays is ten thousand seven hundred and fifty naira. To work backwards from a VAT-inclusive price, divide by one plus the VAT rate as a decimal, which gives the net price, and then subtract to get the VAT amount.

Discounts and markups are not symmetrical. A ten per cent discount followed by a ten per cent markup does not return the original price. The discount is calculated on the higher starting price and the markup is calculated on the lower discounted price, so the ending figure is slightly lower than where it began. Anyone who prices by applying a percentage up or down should be aware of this.

VAT rates by country

Different countries apply different VAT rates to the same kind of transaction, and knowing the local rate is essential for anyone pricing a product for a foreign market or comparing prices across borders.

CountryStandard VATNotes
Nigeria7.5%Standard rate on most goods and services; basic food and medical items are exempt.
Kenya16%Standard rate; a lower rate applies to some essentials and zero rate to exports.
Ghana15%Standard rate; plus small levies on specific categories.
South Africa15%Standard rate; zero-rated on basics such as brown bread and paraffin.
United Kingdom20%Standard rate; reduced rates of 5% and 0% apply to certain goods.
India5% to 28%GST slab system with multiple rates depending on category.
United States0% to 10%+No federal VAT; state and local sales taxes vary widely.

The rate matters, but so does the base it is applied to. Some countries apply VAT to the full retail price, and some apply it to a narrower base such as the wholesale price. Some goods are exempt, which means no VAT is charged at all, and some are zero-rated, which means VAT is charged at zero per cent but the supplier can still reclaim input VAT. These distinctions sit outside the calculator, but they matter for anyone running a business across a border.

Percentage points versus percentage change

Percentage points and percentage change describe the same movement in two different ways, and choosing the wrong one can make a small change look large or a large one look small.

Percentage points are the direct arithmetic difference between two percentages. If a savings rate moves from forty per cent to fifty per cent, it has risen by ten percentage points. The number itself has not changed scale; it has simply moved ten units along the same percentage line.

Percentage change describes the same movement relative to the starting value. A movement from forty per cent to fifty per cent is a ten point rise, but it is also a twenty five per cent increase, because ten divided by the original forty is zero point two five. Both numbers are correct, but they answer different questions. If you are comparing two interest rates, you probably want percentage points. If you are comparing growth over time, you probably want percentage change.

Newspapers and marketing material often switch between the two without warning, because percentage change tends to sound more dramatic than percentage points. A rate that rises from two per cent to three per cent is a one point change, but it is a fifty per cent increase. Both figures describe the same event, and both can be true at the same time.

Common mistakes with percentages

Using the wrong baseline. The single most common source of percentage error is applying a rate to the wrong starting figure. A discount applies to the original price, not the discounted price. A tax applies to the net amount, not the gross. A growth rate applies to the earlier value, not the later one. Writing a one-sentence description of the calculation before starting makes this mistake much less likely.

Confusing percentage points with percentage change. A rise from three per cent to four per cent is one percentage point, but it is a thirty three per cent increase. Both are correct; they answer different questions. Mixing the two in a single report makes the numbers hard to interpret.

Assuming percentage changes are symmetric. A fall from one hundred to fifty is a fifty per cent decrease, but a recovery from fifty back to one hundred is a one hundred per cent increase. The baseline has changed, and the percentages reflect that. Investors who lose fifty per cent of their portfolio need a one hundred per cent return to get back to where they started.

Forgetting to convert percentages to decimals in formulas. When a percentage is multiplied by another number, it must first be converted to its decimal form by dividing by one hundred. Forgetting this step, or multiplying by the percentage directly, produces answers that are off by a factor of a hundred.

Compounding multiple percentage changes additively. A ten per cent increase followed by another ten per cent increase is not a twenty per cent increase. It is a twenty one per cent increase, because the second increase applies to the already-increased figure. Compounding always produces a slightly larger total than the simple sum of the rates.

Rounding too early. Rounding intermediate results to whole numbers before finishing a multi-step calculation can shift the final answer by a meaningful amount. Keeping full precision through the calculation and rounding only at the end gives a cleaner result, especially when the numbers are large or the rates are small.

Where this calculator stops

This tool produces a correct mathematical result from the numbers you enter. It does not know the rules of a specific lender, the rounding conventions of a specific tax authority, the exemptions that apply to a specific category of goods, or the way a specific shop applies a discount before or after VAT. In cross-border pricing, the same nominal percentage can produce different final amounts depending on which of these rules applies.

It also does not provide financial, tax, or legal advice. Whether a particular discount is worthwhile, whether a VAT rate applies to a specific product, and whether a percentage change is significant in a business context are all questions for a qualified adviser who understands the full picture. The tool is a calculator, and the rules of the jurisdiction and the agreement of the parties are what determine the true final number.

What the tool does give you is a fast, transparent, private answer to the three percentage questions that come up most often, along with the working behind each one. That working is what makes it easy to check the answer against the real-world context and spot any mistake before it matters.

Frequently asked questions

What is a percentage?

A percentage is a way of writing a number as a fraction of one hundred. The word comes from the Latin per centum, meaning by the hundred. Fifty per cent means fifty out of every hundred, which is the same as the fraction one half and the same as the decimal zero point five. Percentages are useful because they turn ratios into a common scale, so a discount on a small purchase and a discount on a large purchase can be compared directly.

How do I calculate a percentage of a number?

Multiply the number by the percentage and then divide by one hundred. For a fifteen per cent discount on a forty thousand naira purchase, multiply forty thousand by fifteen to get six hundred thousand, then divide by one hundred to get six thousand naira. That figure is the discount amount, not the final price. To find the final price, subtract the discount from the original amount. The same method works for tips, tax, commission, and any other percentage of a base figure.

What is the difference between percentage change and percentage points?

Percentage points measure the direct difference between two percentages. If a completion rate moves from forty per cent to fifty per cent, it has risen by ten percentage points. Percentage change compares the same movement to the starting value, so a rise from forty per cent to fifty per cent is a twenty five per cent increase relative to the original forty per cent. Both figures describe the same movement, but they answer different questions. Percentage points are used for rate comparisons, and percentage change is used for growth relative to a baseline.

How do I work out a discount?

Calculate the discount amount first by multiplying the original price by the discount percentage and dividing by one hundred. Then subtract the discount from the original price to get the final price. A twenty per cent discount on fifty thousand shillings is ten thousand shillings off, leaving forty thousand shillings before any tax or delivery charges. To find the original price from a discounted price, divide the discounted price by one minus the discount percentage as a decimal.

How do I calculate VAT?

VAT is calculated by multiplying the price before tax by the VAT rate and dividing by one hundred. Nigeria charges seven point five per cent VAT, Kenya charges sixteen per cent, Ghana charges fifteen per cent, South Africa charges fifteen per cent, and the United Kingdom charges twenty per cent as the standard rate. To find the VAT amount, multiply the net price by the rate and divide by one hundred. To find the VAT-inclusive price, add the VAT amount to the net price, or equivalently multiply the net price by one plus the rate as a decimal.

Can a percentage change be negative?

Yes. A negative percentage change means the new value is lower than the original value, which is a percentage decrease. Moving from one hundred to eighty is a twenty per cent decrease. Moving from eighty to one hundred is a twenty five per cent increase. The two directions are not symmetrical because they use different baselines. A fall from one hundred to fifty is a fifty per cent decrease, but a rise from fifty back to one hundred is a one hundred per cent increase.

How do I convert a percentage to a decimal or a fraction?

Divide the percentage by one hundred to get a decimal. Fifty per cent becomes zero point five, and seven point five per cent becomes zero point zero seven five. To convert a percentage to a fraction, write it over one hundred and simplify. Fifty per cent is fifty over one hundred, which simplifies to one half. Twenty five per cent is twenty five over one hundred, which simplifies to one quarter. To go the other way, multiply a decimal by one hundred to get a percentage.
