How to Use the Percentage Calculator

Percent means “per hundred.” A percentage turns a ratio into a number that is easier to compare. Enter the values in the calculation that matches your question, then select Calculate. All three calculators work independently, so you can compare a discount with the final price, measure a change in income, or check how much of a target has been completed.

Finding a Percentage of a Number

The first calculator uses the formula percentage ÷ 100 × number. For example, 15% of 240 is 36. This is useful when calculating a 15% discount, estimating a 15% tip, or setting aside 15% of income. The result is the portion represented by the percentage, not automatically the final price. To find a discounted price, subtract the result from the original amount; to add a markup, add it instead.

Comparing a Part with a Whole

The second calculator divides the part by the whole and multiplies by 100. If you completed 30 tasks out of 120, the result is 25%. The whole should be the complete reference amount. Using a different denominator can change the answer significantly, so decide what “100%” means before entering numbers. This method works for exam scores, savings goals, sales conversion rates, survey results, and budget categories.

Percentage Increase and Decrease

Percentage change is calculated as new value minus original value, divided by the original value, multiplied by 100. A positive result indicates an increase and a negative result indicates a decrease. Moving from 80 to 100 is a 25% increase, while moving from 100 to 80 is a 20% decrease. The two directions are not symmetrical because they use different original values as the baseline.

Practical Uses and Limitations

Percentage calculations appear in shopping, finance, business, education, health tracking, and everyday planning. They do not account for fees, taxes, rounding rules, inflation, or changing baselines unless you include those values yourself. A store discount may be applied before tax, and a bank rate may be quoted annually while interest compounds monthly. Use the result as a transparent mathematical estimate and check the terms of the real transaction.

Discounts, Markups, and Final Prices

To calculate a sale price, first find the percentage amount and then subtract it from the original price. A 25% discount on 80 is 20, leaving a price of 60 before any taxes or delivery charges. A markup works in the opposite direction: a 25% markup on a cost of 80 adds 20, producing a selling price of 100. The same percentage applied in reverse does not always return the starting figure, because the second calculation uses a different base.

Percentages in Budgets and Business

A budget can use percentages to compare spending categories even when monthly income changes. For example, housing that costs 300 from an income of 1,500 uses 20% of that income. If income rises but rent remains unchanged, the housing percentage falls. Businesses use similar calculations to review gross margins, conversion rates, commission costs, and month-over-month growth. Keep the period and denominator clear so two reports are genuinely comparable.

Percentage Change Is Not the Same as Percentage Points

Percentage points describe the direct difference between two percentages. If a completion rate moves from 40% to 50%, it rises by 10 percentage points. Relative percentage change compares that difference with the starting 40%, so the increase is 25%. Both descriptions can be correct, but they answer different questions. Use percentage points for direct rate comparisons and percentage change when you want to describe growth relative to the original baseline.

Use a Clear Baseline

Most percentage mistakes come from choosing the wrong “whole.” Before calculating, write a short sentence: “This part is compared with this total,” or “This new value is compared with the original value.” For a tip, the meal amount is usually the base; for a tax, the taxable amount is the base; for growth, the earlier value is the base. If a fee is calculated on a subtotal but displayed on a final bill, confirm which number the provider uses.

Percentage Questions

How do I calculate a discount?

Find the discount amount using the first calculator, then subtract it from the original price. A 20% discount on 50 is 10, so the final price before tax is 40.

Can a percentage change be negative?

Yes. A negative result means the new value is lower than the original value and represents a percentage decrease.

Why are percentage points different from percent?

A change from 20% to 25% is five percentage points, but it is a 25% relative increase compared with the original 20%.
