What this calculator does

This tool takes a cost price and a selling price and returns four numbers that every business owner needs to know about a product: the gross profit per unit, the gross margin percentage, the markup rate, and the cost ratio. If you also enter the monthly units sold, it projects monthly revenue, monthly profit, and annual profit. A pricing sensitivity table shows what happens to the margin and markup when the selling price moves up or down by ten or twenty per cent, which is the fastest way to see how much room the product actually has.

The point of the tool is not to do arithmetic you could do on a napkin. It is to make the difference between margin and markup impossible to confuse. Those two numbers are calculated from the same underlying profit figure, but they use different baselines, and the difference between a forty per cent margin and a forty per cent markup on the same product is roughly eight pounds on a fifty pound cost. Getting that wrong on a single high-volume product line can cost a business thousands of pounds across a year without anyone realising the price was ever set incorrectly.

Every calculation runs inside your browser. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored, and nothing is tracked. If the AI insight button is pressed, only an anonymous summary of the cost, the price, the margin, the markup, the cost ratio, and the units sold is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

The three profitability metrics

Three numbers describe how profitable a product is, and each one answers a slightly different question.

Gross profit is the absolute cash amount left over after subtracting the direct cost of the product from the selling price. On a fifty pound cost and a ninety pound selling price, the gross profit is forty pounds. This is the number that pays for everything else in the business, from rent to salaries to marketing.

Gross margin expresses the same profit as a percentage of the selling price. Forty pounds of profit on a ninety pound sale is a 44.4 per cent margin. This is the number to compare against industry benchmarks and against other products in the range, because it describes the efficiency of the pricing rather than the absolute amount of money.

Markup expresses the same profit as a percentage of the cost. Forty pounds of profit on a fifty pound cost is an eighty per cent markup. Markup is the number most commonly used when setting prices in the first place, because a supplier or manufacturer usually thinks in terms of how much to add to the cost, not how much of the final price should be profit.

Both margin and markup describe the same underlying profit. The confusion comes from the fact that they use different denominators. Margin divides by the selling price, which is always larger than the cost, so the resulting percentage is always smaller. Markup divides by the cost, which is always smaller, so the resulting percentage is always larger. On any profitable product, markup is a bigger number than margin, and the gap widens as the margin rises.

Margin versus markup

The confusion between margin and markup is the single most common pricing error in small business, and it costs money in a specific direction. A business owner who wants a fifty per cent margin and applies a fifty per cent markup ends up with a thirty-three per cent margin, which is a full seventeen points short of the target.

The reason is straightforward once you see the formulas next to each other.

Margin = (Selling price − Cost) ÷ Selling price × 100
Markup = (Selling price − Cost) ÷ Cost × 100

The numerator is identical in both. Only the denominator changes. Selling price is always larger than cost for any profitable product, so dividing the same numerator by the larger denominator always produces a smaller percentage. That is why markup is always higher than margin on the same item, and why the two can never be used interchangeably.

The conversion between the two is worth memorising. To convert markup to margin, divide the markup by one plus the markup. A fifty per cent markup divided by one point five is thirty-three point three three per cent margin. To convert margin to markup, divide the margin by one minus the margin. A fifty per cent margin divided by zero point five is a one hundred per cent markup. The maths is short, and it saves a surprising amount of rework when pricing a product range.

How to price from a target margin

When the goal is to hit a specific margin rather than a specific markup, the pricing formula inverts. Instead of adding a markup to the cost, divide the cost by one minus the target margin expressed as a decimal.

Selling price = Cost ÷ (1 − Target margin)

If the cost is fifty pounds and the target margin is forty per cent, divide fifty by zero point six, which gives a selling price of eighty-three pounds and thirty-three pence. Running the calculation back confirms the margin: gross profit is thirty-three point three three pounds, and that divided by the selling price of eighty-three point three three is exactly forty per cent.

The same formula works for any margin target. A sixty per cent margin on the same fifty pound cost requires a selling price of one hundred and twenty-five pounds. A twenty per cent margin requires sixty-two pounds and fifty pence. The table between these two values is the pricing ladder the business owner works from, and it is worth keeping on hand rather than recalculating it every time a product is added to the range.

What counts as cost of goods sold

The cost figure that goes into the calculator matters as much as the price itself, and small businesses routinely undercount it. Cost of goods sold includes every direct cost that is incurred to acquire, manufacture, or deliver a single unit of the product, and nothing else.

For a retailer, the cost of goods sold is the wholesale price paid to the supplier, plus inbound freight, plus any import duties or customs charges incurred on the shipment. If the product is stored in a warehouse and picked and packed specifically for a customer, the direct pick-and-pack cost is arguably part of the cost of sale too, though most small retailers leave it out.

For a manufacturer, the cost of goods sold is the raw materials used in the product, the direct labour used to produce it, and the factory overhead allocated to that production run. Factory overhead includes the electricity, the machinery depreciation, and the factory supervisor's salary to the extent that those costs are consumed by making the product.

For a service business, the cost of goods sold is the direct cost of delivering the service. For a web designer, that might be hosting fees and third-party licences. For an accountant, it might be the software subscriptions that get charged to each client engagement. For a consultant, it might be the subcontracted specialist hours billed through the consultant's firm.

What does not belong in the cost of goods sold: office rent, salaried admin staff, marketing spend, founder drawings, and any other cost that is not directly attached to producing or delivering a specific unit of the product. Those are operating expenses and they sit below the gross margin line. Including them in the cost of goods sold would overstate the cost and understate the margin, which distorts every pricing decision that follows.

Industry margin benchmarks

There is no universal good margin. The right number depends heavily on the sector, because the cost structure and the competitive dynamics are different in each one.

SectorTypical gross marginWhy
Software and digital products70% to 90%Near-zero incremental cost to serve one more customer, but high R&D and customer acquisition spend.
Professional services50% to 70%High margins on billable labour, offset by salaries and admin overhead below the gross margin line.
Retail and e-commerce30% to 60%Moderate margins to cover inventory holding, shipping, platform fees, and seasonal markdowns.
Manufacturing25% to 40%Heavy raw material, direct labour, and factory overhead costs per unit produced.
Food and beverage15% to 30%Food spoilage, high kitchen labour, and intense price competition compress margins.

A margin that sits in the typical range for the sector is a reasonable starting point, but the benchmark is not a target. A business with a higher cost structure, a stronger brand, or a more differentiated product can sustain a higher margin than the sector average. A business competing on price will usually operate below the average and make up the difference on volume. The benchmark is a sanity check, not a decision.

A worked pricing example

Take an e-commerce business selling a jacket that costs sixty pounds to acquire, including inbound freight. The founder wants to set a price that produces a forty per cent margin.

Using the pricing formula, the selling price is sixty divided by one minus zero point four, which is sixty divided by zero point six, which equals one hundred pounds. At that price, the gross profit is forty pounds, the gross margin is forty per cent, the markup is sixty-six point six seven per cent, and the cost ratio is sixty per cent.

Now suppose the founder prices the jacket at ninety pounds instead, on the mistaken belief that a fifty per cent markup is equivalent to a fifty per cent margin. At ninety pounds, the gross profit is thirty pounds, the margin is thirty-three point three three per cent, and the markup is fifty per cent. The business is seven percentage points short of its target margin on every jacket sold, and at a hundred jackets per month, that is seven hundred pounds of profit given up each month.

The same product at a hundred and ten pounds produces a gross profit of fifty pounds, a margin of forty-five point four five per cent, and a markup of eighty-three point three three per cent. That is above the target margin and demonstrates the leverage price has on profitability. A ten per cent price increase on a product with a sixty pound cost pushes the margin up by five full percentage points, which is often more than the business could achieve through any realistic cost reduction programme.

Payment processor fees and hidden cost

The gross margin shown by this calculator is a correct calculation from the cost and price the user enters, but the number the seller actually keeps can be meaningfully lower once payment processing fees are accounted for. On an e-commerce sale, a card processor typically charges between one point five and three point five per cent of the transaction value plus a small fixed fee per transaction.

On a thirty pound sale with a three point five per cent plus thirty pence processor fee, the charge is one pound thirty-five. The cost of goods is twenty pounds, the gross profit before fees is ten pounds, and the gross profit after fees is eight pounds and sixty-five pence. The margin before fees is thirty-three point three three per cent, and after fees it is twenty-eight point eight three per cent. That is a four-and-a-half point drop from a single fee line, and it does not account for shipping subsidies, returns, or customer service time.

The practical implication is that a business running on tight margins should include expected processor fees in the cost figure used for pricing, not in a separate calculation performed after the fact. Including them at the pricing stage ensures the selling price actually covers them, rather than leaving the business with a margin shortfall that is discovered only at the monthly reconciliation.

Where this calculator stops

This tool produces a gross margin from the cost and price you enter. It does not know your operating expenses, your tax position, your financing costs, or your owner drawings, and it does not compute net margin. Net margin subtracts every expense from revenue, not just the direct cost of the product, and it is the number that matters for profitability at the company level. Gross margin and net margin measure different things, and confusing them is a second common error that this calculator does not resolve.

It also does not provide financial or tax advice. Whether a specific margin is appropriate for a specific business, how to allocate overhead between products, and how to treat unusual costs are questions for a qualified accountant. The calculator is a pricing aid, and the authoritative documents are the business's financial statements, its tax filings, and the advice of a certified professional.

What the tool does give you is a fast, transparent, private way to see the four numbers that determine whether a product makes money. The margin, the markup, the cost ratio, and the gross profit per unit are the four figures any business owner should be able to quote from memory for their top-selling products. This calculator puts them on one page in under a second.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between gross margin and markup?

Gross margin expresses gross profit as a percentage of the selling price. Markup expresses the same gross profit as a percentage of the cost price. Because the selling price is always larger than the cost for any profitable product, margin is always a smaller number than markup on the same item. On a product costing sixty pounds that sells for one hundred, the gross profit is forty pounds, the margin is forty per cent, and the markup is sixty-six point six seven per cent. Confusing the two is the single most common pricing error in small business.

What is a good profit margin for a business?

There is no single good margin that applies to every business. The right benchmark depends on the sector. Software and digital products typically operate at seventy to ninety per cent gross margin because the incremental cost of serving one more customer is close to zero. Professional services run at fifty to seventy per cent. Retail and e-commerce run at thirty to sixty per cent. Manufacturing sits at twenty-five to forty per cent. Restaurants and grocery retailers operate on fifteen to thirty per cent because of food spoilage and intense price competition. A healthy margin is one that covers all fixed operating costs and leaves a reasonable net profit after everything else is paid.

How do I set a selling price if I know my cost and the margin I want?

Divide the cost by one minus the target margin expressed as a decimal. If the cost is fifty pounds and the target margin is forty per cent, divide fifty by one minus zero point four, which is fifty divided by zero point six, giving a selling price of eighty-three pounds and thirty-three pence. To hit a fifty per cent margin on the same cost, divide fifty by zero point five, which gives a selling price of one hundred pounds. The formula is worth memorising because it is the only correct way to back out a price from a margin target.

How do I convert a markup into a margin?

Divide the markup by one plus the markup. If the markup is fifty per cent, expressed as zero point five, divide zero point five by one point five, which gives zero point three three three, or thirty-three point three three per cent margin. The conversion goes the other way as well. Divide the margin by one minus the margin to get the markup. A forty per cent margin divided by zero point six gives a sixty-six point six seven per cent markup.

Can gross margin be negative?

Yes. If the selling price falls below the cost of goods sold, the gross profit becomes negative, and the gross margin percentage becomes negative with it. This happens during liquidation sales, clearance events, and end-of-season markdowns where the goal is to recover cash and free up shelf space rather than to make a profit on the item. A negative gross margin on some items is not automatically a problem if it is part of a deliberate clearance strategy and other products in the range carry healthy margins.

What costs should be included in the cost of goods sold?

Cost of goods sold includes every direct cost incurred to acquire, manufacture, or deliver a single unit of the product. For a retailer, that is the wholesale price plus inbound freight plus any import duties. For a manufacturer, it is the raw materials plus the direct labour used to produce the item plus the factory overhead allocated to that item. For a service business, it is the direct cost of delivering the service, such as subcontracted work, hosting, or third-party licences. It does not include office rent, salaried admin staff, marketing, or owner drawings. Those are operating expenses and sit below the gross margin line.

Should payment processing fees be part of the cost of goods sold?

There is no universal rule, but most accountants treat payment processing fees as an operating expense rather than a cost of goods sold, because they are charged by a service provider rather than incurred in producing the product itself. For a small e-commerce business, though, it is often worth treating processor fees as a cost of sale for pricing purposes, because on a thirty pound sale a fee of one pound thirty-five is a real reduction in the cash the seller actually receives. A margin of thirty-three per cent before processor fees can drop to twenty-eight or twenty-nine per cent after, and that difference matters when the business is running on tight margins.

Does this calculator store my pricing data?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored on any server. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the cost price, the selling price, the gross margin, the markup, the cost ratio, and the units sold is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Your product names, supplier details, and customer information are never collected because they are never asked for.
