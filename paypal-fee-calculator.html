What this payment processor fee calculator models

Digital payment gateways quote their pricing as a compound rate—a percentage of the gross transaction plus a flat per-order charge—such as 3.49% + $0.49 for PayPal Goods and Services or 2.90% + $0.30 for Stripe and Square Online. When freelancers, e-commerce merchants, consultants, and SaaS founders price products mentally using only the headline percentage, the flat per-transaction charge quietly erodes unit profit on low-ticket orders, while naive fee markups on client invoices leave a recurring payout shortfall.

This calculator solves both operational problems in one place. In Standard Mode ("I charge X, I receive Y"), you enter the checkout or invoice total paid by the customer and select a processor tier to see the exact dollar fee deducted, the net settlement landing in your balance, and the real effective fee rate expressed as a percentage of gross revenue. In Reverse Gross-Up Mode ("I receive Y, I charge X"), you enter the exact net payout you need to clear after fees, and the tool solves the inverse equation so your gross invoice covers both the percentage deduction and the fee charged on the fee adjustment itself.

Beneath the per-transaction breakdown, the volume projection table multiplies your chosen ticket size across 10, 50, 100, 500, and 1,000 monthly orders so you can audit how per-order fixed fees accumulate over a twelve-month operating cycle. Every calculation runs locally inside your browser tab with client-side JavaScript—zero invoice figures, client records, or merchant numbers are stored or transmitted unless you explicitly press the optional AI commentary button.

How payment processor fees work: interchange, assessments, and markup

When a buyer clicks "Pay" with a debit card, credit card, or digital wallet, the money does not move directly from their bank account to yours. Instead, every card-backed transaction travels across a four-party settlement rail involving the cardholder's issuing bank (which extends credit or holds consumer deposits, funds card reward programs, and absorbs issuer-side fraud rules), the card network (such as Visa, Mastercard, American Express, or Discover, which maintains authorization routing and clearing rails), the acquiring bank or payment facilitator (which underwrites your merchant account and settles funds), and the payment gateway or processor (PayPal, Stripe, or Square, which provides checkout software, tokenization, fraud screening, and dispute management).

Modern payment facilitators such as PayPal, Stripe, and Square bundle all three underlying wholesale cost layers into a single predictable blended flat-rate schedule:

  • Issuing bank interchange (~1.15% to 2.40% + $0.05 to $0.10): The largest component of any card fee goes to the buyer's bank, not to Stripe or PayPal. Regulated debit cards carry much lower wholesale interchange than corporate travel rewards credit cards or cross-border cards, and card-not-present online checkouts carry higher interchange than physical EMV chip taps because online fraud rates are higher.
  • Card network scheme assessments (~0.13% to 0.15%): Visa and Mastercard levy non-negotiable network assessments on gross transaction volume, plus separate cross-border assessment surcharges (often 0.80% to 1.00% at the wholesale rail level) whenever the card issuer's country differs from the merchant's domicile.
  • Processor margin and fixed gateway authorization fee: The remainder of the percentage rate plus the flat per-transaction fee ($0.10, $0.30, or $0.49) compensates the payment processor for PCI-DSS Level 1 vaulting, 3D Secure authentication, machine-learning fraud filters, payout settlement, and blending volatile card-tier costs into one transparent rate.
Processor Fee = (Gross Charge Amount x Percentage Rate) + Fixed Per-Transaction Fee
Net Payout Received = Gross Charge Amount - Processor Fee
Effective Fee Rate (%) = (Processor Fee / Gross Charge Amount) x 100

Understanding this wholesale structure explains why flat-rate processors seldom discount rates for early-stage stores, yet willingly switch merchants processing above $80,000 to $100,000 per month onto interchange-plus pricing (for example, wholesale interchange + 0.30% + $0.10). On a blended flat rate, a customer paying with a regulated domestic debit card subsidizes another customer paying with a premium corporate rewards card; on interchange-plus, your business captures the wholesale savings directly whenever customers pay with lower-cost debit cards.

PayPal merchant fees: Goods and Services, invoicing, QR codes, and micropayments

PayPal operates a dual-rail network: buyers can fund purchases either from an existing PayPal balance or linked bank account inside PayPal's closed-loop wallet, or from an external Visa, Mastercard, or American Express card routed through PayPal Checkout. For standard US commercial checkouts, PayPal Invoicing, and PayPal.Me commercial links, the published PayPal Goods and Services rate is 3.49% + $0.49 per transaction.

Not every PayPal product carries that exact 3.49% + $0.49 rate, so auditing which checkout button your customers click is critical:

  • PayPal Checkout & PayPal Invoicing (3.49% + $0.49): Applies when customers pay through the branded PayPal wallet button, Pay Later options, or commercial invoices. It includes PayPal Purchase Protection for buyers and PayPal Seller Protection on eligible physical and intangible deliveries.
  • Standard Credit and Debit Card Fields on PayPal (2.99% + $0.49): When a merchant embeds PayPal's unbranded card fields so buyers type card numbers directly without logging into a PayPal wallet, US domestic card rates sit slightly lower at 2.99% + $0.49 (or 2.59% + $0.49 under Advanced Credit and Debit Card processing, subject to underwriting).
  • In-Person PayPal QR Code & Zettle Point-of-Sale: Physical QR code and terminal transactions carry lower card-present rates (for example, 2.29% + $0.09 on Zettle card-present readers) because physical proximity lowers unauthorized-transaction disputes.
  • PayPal Micropayments Schedule (4.99% + $0.09 US reference tier): Sellers whose average order value sits below $10.00—such as single-track audio stores, game item vendors, or pay-per-article publishers—can apply for a dedicated micropayment merchant profile. Trading a higher percentage (4.99%) for a $0.09 fixed charge instead of $0.49 saves money on every order below $26.67. For instance, on a $4.00 digital purchase, standard Goods and Services deducts $0.63 (15.74% effective), whereas a 4.99% + $0.09 micropayment rate deducts $0.29 (7.24% effective)—cutting fees by more than half.

Sellers occasionally ask whether they can ask clients to select Friends and Family to avoid commercial deductions. PayPal's User Agreement explicitly prohibits receiving Friends and Family transfers for goods, freelance milestones, consulting retainers, or digital downloads. Accounts flagged by PayPal's velocity and memo-scanning rules lose Seller Protection, face 180-day reserve holds, and risk permanent suspension.

Stripe processing fees: domestic cards, international cards, and ACH

Stripe is the default developer-first payment infrastructure for SaaS subscriptions, custom e-commerce storefronts, and global B2B invoicing. Its standard published US Pay-As-You-Go card rate is 2.90% + $0.30 per successful card charge across Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Discover, Apple Pay, and Google Pay—saving 59 basis points plus $0.19 per transaction compared with PayPal Goods and Services ($3.20 vs. $3.98 on a $100.00 order).

However, Stripe's modular architecture adds transparent add-on percentages whenever a transaction crosses borders or uses billing automation:

  • International cards (+1.50%, totaling 4.40% + $0.30): If your Stripe account is domiciled in the United States and a customer pays with a card issued in the United Kingdom, Nigeria, Canada, or Germany—even when the checkout price is denominated in US dollars—Stripe adds a 1.50% cross-border card surcharge.
  • Currency conversion (+1.00%, totaling 5.40% + $0.30 on international FX orders): If the customer pays in Euros or British Pounds and Stripe converts the settlement into your US dollar bank account, an additional 1.00% FX conversion fee applies on top of the 4.40% + $0.30 international card rate.
  • Manually keyed cards (+0.50%) and Billing / Invoicing add-ons (+0.40% to +0.70%): Typing a customer's card number manually into the Stripe Dashboard adds 0.50% for elevated card-not-present fraud risk, while automated Stripe Invoicing or recurring subscription dunning tools carry small percentage add-ons above raw Payment Intents API charges.
  • ACH Direct Debit (0.80%, capped at $5.00): For domestic US B2B agencies, wholesalers, and SaaS annual contracts, enabling ACH Direct Debit inside Stripe Checkout is one of the highest-ROI fee optimizations available. Any invoice of $625.00 or higher hits the $5.00 maximum fee cap—meaning a $5,000.00 consulting invoice costs just $5.00 (0.10% effective rate) via ACH instead of $145.30 via Stripe card or $174.99 via PayPal Goods and Services.

Square processing fees: card-present vs. online checkout

Square built its ecosystem around omnichannel retail, restaurants, clinics, market stalls, and field-service contractors that accept both physical point-of-sale payments and online orders. Because cryptographic EMV chip dips and NFC contactless taps ("Tap to Pay", Apple Pay, physical cards) prove the physical card was present at the counter, card networks assign substantially lower interchange rates—and Square passes that spread along:

  • Square In-Person Tap, Dip, or Swipe (2.60% + $0.10): On a $25.00 coffee-and-merchandise ticket, Square In-Person charges just $0.75 (3.00% effective rate), compared with $1.03 on Stripe/Square Online (4.10%) and $1.36 on PayPal Goods and Services (5.45%).
  • Square Online Checkout & eCommerce API (2.90% + $0.30): Matches Stripe's standard domestic online rate dollar-for-dollar on digital storefront checkouts and payment links.
  • Square Invoices (3.30% + $0.30 card) and Manually Keyed-In Cards (3.50% + $0.15): Reading card digits over the phone and typing them into the Square app triggers the highest domestic tier (3.50% + $0.15) because keyed transactions lack EMV cryptograms or 3D Secure verification and carry steep chargeback exposure.

Worked volume example: three businesses earning $10,000 per month

To see how order structure shapes annual net income even when top-line revenue is identical, compare three online businesses that each generate $10,000.00 per month ($120,000.00 per year) through PayPal Goods and Services (3.49% + $0.49):

  • Business A — Micro-ticket creator store (1,000 orders/month at $10.00): Pays $349.00 in percentage fees plus $490.00 in fixed $0.49 fees every month, totaling $839.00/month or $10,068.00/year (8.39% of gross revenue).
  • Business B — Mid-ticket apparel brand (200 orders/month at $50.00): Pays $349.00 in percentage fees plus $98.00 in fixed fees every month, totaling $447.00/month or $5,364.00/year (4.47% of gross revenue).
  • Business C — High-ticket consulting practice (20 invoices/month at $500.00): Pays $349.00 in percentage fees plus just $9.80 in fixed fees every month, totaling $358.80/month or $4,305.60/year (3.59% of gross revenue).

Key takeaway for product bundling: Business A and Business C collect the exact same $120,000.00 in annual sales on the exact same PayPal rate schedule, yet Business A surrenders $5,762.40 more per year solely because its revenue arrives in $10.00 increments instead of $500.00 increments. Bundling three $10.00 templates into a $27.00 pack cuts three $0.49 fixed charges down to one and immediately recovers over four percentage points of net margin.

Reverse fee mode: how to gross up client invoices accurately

Freelancers, agencies, and wholesale suppliers frequently agree with clients that the buyer will cover electronic payment gateway fees if paying by credit card or PayPal instead of bank transfer. The single most common billing error is adding the percentage rate directly onto the desired net payout. Because the processor applies its percentage fee to the entire gross invoice—including the extra dollars you added to cover the fee—naive addition always comes up short.

To solve for the exact gross invoice amount G that leaves a desired net payout N after percentage rate r (expressed as a decimal, such as 0.0349) and fixed charge F, start from the net settlement identity and isolate G:

N = G - (G x r + F) = G x (1 - r) - F
G x (1 - r) = N + F
Required Gross Invoice (G) = (Target Net Payout + Fixed Fee) / (1 - Percentage Rate)

Worked Example 1: Grossing up a $1,200.00 freelance milestone

Suppose a web developer completes a project milestone with a contracted net fee of $1,200.00, and the client asks to pay via PayPal Goods and Services (3.49% + $0.49):

  1. Incorrect naive markup: Computing $1,200.00 x 1.0349 + $0.49 produces an invoice of $1,242.37. When the client pays $1,242.37, PayPal deducts ($1,242.37 x 0.0349) + $0.49 = $43.36 + $0.49 = $43.85, depositing $1,198.52—leaving a $1.48 reconciliation discrepancy in your accounting ledger.
  2. Exact reverse gross-up: Computing ($1,200.00 + $0.49) / (1 - 0.0349) = $1,200.49 / 0.9651 = $1,243.90. When the client pays $1,243.90, PayPal deducts ($1,243.90 x 0.0349) + $0.49 = $43.41 + $0.49 = $43.90, depositing exactly $1,200.00.

Worked Example 2: Combining unit cost, target margin, and gateway fees

Suppose an e-commerce seller imports a specialty kitchen scale with a landed unit cost of $32.00 and wants a net contribution of $18.00 per unit after Stripe domestic card fees (2.90% + $0.30), meaning the store must clear $50.00 net from Stripe per order before paying fixed overhead. Entering $50.00 in Reverse Mode for Stripe Standard computes ($50.00 + $0.30) / (1 - 0.029) = $50.30 / 0.971 = $51.80 required list price. Stripe deducts $1.80 ($1.50 + $0.30), leaving $50.00 net—covering the $32.00 landed COGS and preserving the exact $18.00 unit profit. Cross-border payments, FX spreads, and African freelance corridors

For remote engineers, digital agencies, exporters, and SaaS founders across Africa—including Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, South Africa, Rwanda, and Egypt—as well as international sellers billing US or European clients, the domestic gateway fee shown in standard US tables is only the first layer of cost. Cross-border digital payouts typically pass through up to three sequential deductions: (1) a cross-border commercial receiving surcharge, (2) a foreign exchange (FX) retail conversion spread below the interbank mid-market rate, and (3) a local bank or mobile-money withdrawal fee.

Consider what happens to a $1,000.00 USD invoice paid by an overseas client under three common settlement paths:

  • PayPal Cross-Border + Automatic Currency Conversion (8.36% total cost): When an international commercial payment incurs a 1.50% cross-border surcharge on top of 3.49% + $0.49 (totaling 4.99% + $0.49 = $50.39), your USD wallet receives $949.61. If PayPal then converts that $949.61 into local currency at a typical 3.50% retail FX spread below the wholesale mid-market exchange rate (worth $33.24), you receive local currency worth $916.37—surrendering $83.63 of the $1,000.00 invoice. (You can benchmark mid-market exchange rates using our Real-Time Currency Converter.)
  • Stripe International Card + 1.00% FX Conversion (5.43% total cost): An overseas card processed with automatic settlement currency conversion incurs 2.90% base + 1.50% international card + 1.00% FX conversion + $0.30 = $54.30, netting $945.70.
  • African Regional Gateways & Mobile-Money Corridors: In Kenya, linking PayPal to M-Pesa (or withdrawing to a USD domiciliary bank account at Equity Bank or KCB before converting at tighter treasury spreads) avoids double currency conversion when billing in USD. Across Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, and South Africa, regional gateways such as Paystack and Flutterwave typically charge 1.50% to 2.90% on domestic local-currency cards/mobile money (often with local fee caps on domestic NGN transfers) and roughly 3.80% to 3.90% on international cards, though settlement currency rules and central-bank domiciliary payout rules vary by jurisdiction.

Refunds, chargebacks, and rolling reserves: hidden post-checkout costs

Published checkout percentages assume every order settles cleanly and stays settled. In real-world e-commerce and digital services, customer refunds and cardholder disputes create asymmetric fee losses that must be factored into your unit economics and break-even volume:

  • Non-refundable processing fees on voluntary refunds: PayPal, Stripe, and Square do not refund the original transaction fee when you issue a full or partial refund to a customer. If a customer buys a $100.00 jacket via PayPal Goods and Services ($3.98 fee, $96.02 net payout) and returns it unworn for a full $100.00 refund, PayPal pulls the remaining $3.98 out of your merchant balance so the buyer receives $100.00 back. Even with undamaged inventory returned to your shelf, that single return costs $3.98 in sunk gateway fees plus outbound and return shipping.
  • Chargeback and dispute fees ($15.00 to $20.00+ per dispute): When a buyer files a card chargeback with their issuing bank instead of requesting a refund from you directly, Stripe and PayPal freeze the disputed transaction amount and assess a separate dispute fee—typically $15.00 on Stripe (with optional additional counter-dispute fees if contested) and $15.00 to $20.00 on PayPal depending on dispute tier and seller volume—unless you qualify for Chargeback Protection programs (which themselves add 0.40% to 0.60% per checkout) or win an eligible Seller Protection claim.
  • Network dispute monitoring thresholds (0.65% to 0.90%): Visa and Mastercard track disputes as a ratio of monthly transaction count, not dollar volume. Crossing 0.65% to 0.90% triggers early-warning or excessive-chargeback programs, rolling reserves (where 10% to 25% of payouts are held for 90 to 180 days), or merchant account termination. Issuing prompt voluntary refunds on dissatisfied low-ticket orders—even at the cost of forfeiting a $1.36 processing fee—is almost always cheaper than absorbing a $15.00 dispute fee and damaging your dispute ratio.

Practical strategies to lower payment processing fees legally

You rarely need to switch platforms completely to cut annual processing expenses by 20% to 50%. Applying channel-routing discipline across your existing payment stack delivers immediate margin gains:

  1. Route B2B invoices above $500 to ACH, SEPA, or bank transfer: Keep card and PayPal buttons enabled for fast sub-$300 checkouts where impulse conversion matters, but configure invoicing software to default to ACH Direct Debit, SEPA, or local bank transfer on retainers and wholesale orders over $500.
  2. Enforce minimum cart values or multi-packs below $15: If you sell $5.00 digital templates, decals, or cafe add-ons, offer 3-for-$13.50 bundles or stored-balance credits so fixed $0.30–$0.49 fees only hit once instead of three times.
  3. Eliminate unnecessary currency round-trips: If you pay software tools, cloud hosting, or subcontractors in US dollars, hold incoming USD client payments inside a USD merchant or multi-currency balance rather than converting USD to local currency at a 3.5% FX spread and then paying another 3.5% FX card fee when buying USD software subscriptions.
  4. Replace phone-keyed cards with SMS or email checkout links: Never type card numbers over the phone (3.40%–3.50% keyed rate + zero 3D Secure liability shift). Text or email a Stripe or Square payment link so the customer authenticates on their own device with Apple Pay, Google Pay, or 3D Secure at the standard 2.90% rate.
  5. Follow card-network rules if passing fees to buyers: Before adding a credit-card surcharge line item to customer invoices, verify card-network and statutory rules in your jurisdiction. Several US states, the UK, and the EU restrict or ban retail card surcharges on consumer debit/credit cards, while B2B early-payment/ACH discounts ("2% discount for ACH/bank wire") achieve the same net economics cleanly without triggering surcharge compliance violations.
  6. Request custom interchange-plus quotes above $80,000/month: Once monthly card volume consistently clears $80,000 to $100,000 with a dispute ratio below 0.25%, contact merchant sales with three months of processing statements to request interchange-plus pricing and lower fixed per-authorization fees ($0.08–$0.15 instead of $0.30).

Common accounting and checkout misunderstandings

Booking net payouts as gross revenue instead of expensing fees. When $96.02 lands in your checking account from a $100.00 PayPal sale, your accounting software must record $100.00 in Gross Sales Revenue and $3.98 in Merchant Processing Fees (Operating Expense). Booking only $96.02 as revenue understates gross turnover for VAT registration thresholds and creates an immediate audit discrepancy against annual gross settlement reports (such as US Form 1099-K) issued by PayPal, Stripe, and Square.

Forgetting that processors charge fees on sales tax, VAT, and shipping. Payment gateways apply their percentage fee to the total gross settlement authorized at checkout—not merely to your pre-tax product subtotal. If a $100.00 item carries $16.00 of 16% VAT and $10.00 of shipping ($126.00 checkout total), PayPal charges 3.49% on the full $126.00 ($4.40 + $0.49 = $4.89), meaning you pay $0.56 in processing fees simply to collect tax on behalf of the revenue authority.

Removing PayPal purely because its 3.49% + $0.49 rate exceeds Stripe's 2.90% + $0.30. Comparing unit fees without measuring checkout conversion rate can reduce total profit. On consumer e-commerce stores with gross margins above 45%, offering PayPal Express alongside Stripe card checkout often lifts overall checkout completion among mobile shoppers who prefer not to type 16-digit card numbers. If PayPal captures even 4% incremental orders at a 50% gross margin, the extra gross profit easily outweighs the extra 0.59% + $0.19 fee spread.

Assuming "instant payout" to a debit card is free. Standard automated clearing house payouts to a linked bank account (1 to 3 business days) are free on PayPal, Stripe, and Square, whereas Instant Transfers / Instant Payouts to an eligible debit card or bank rail typically deduct an additional 1.50% to 1.75% fee on the transferred balance—silently doubling your effective payment cost if used habitually for working capital.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage does PayPal take from a Goods and Services sale?

For standard US domestic PayPal Goods and Services checkout and commercial invoicing, PayPal charges 3.49% plus a $0.49 fixed fee per transaction. On a $100.00 sale, the fee is $3.98 ($3.49 percentage portion plus $0.49 fixed charge), leaving a net payout of $96.02 and an effective rate of 3.98%. International commercial payments add a 1.50% cross-border surcharge (bringing the percentage to 4.99% plus the fixed currency fee), and converting balances into another currency adds a separate 3.0% to 4.0% foreign exchange spread.

How do I calculate what to charge on an invoice so I receive an exact net amount after PayPal or Stripe fees?

Use the reverse gross-up formula: Required Invoice Charge = (Target Net Amount + Fixed Fee) / (1 - Percentage Rate as a decimal). Do not simply add 3.49% + $0.49 on top of your target net, because the processor applies its percentage fee to the entire gross invoice, including the fee adjustment itself. For example, to receive exactly $1,200.00 net via PayPal Goods and Services (3.49% + $0.49), compute ($1,200.00 + $0.49) / (1 - 0.0349) = $1,200.49 / 0.9651 = $1,243.90. Switch this calculator to 'I receive Y, I charge X' mode to compute the exact invoice amount automatically.

What is the difference between PayPal, Stripe, and Square processing fees?

Stripe and Square Online both charge 2.90% + $0.30 for standard US domestic online card payments ($3.20 on a $100.00 order), whereas PayPal Goods and Services charges 3.49% + $0.49 ($3.98 on a $100.00 order). Square In-Person offers the lowest rate of the three at 2.60% + $0.10 ($2.70 on a $100.00 tap, chip, or swipe sale) because physical EMV card presence sharply lowers fraud risk. For cross-border cards in the same settlement currency, Stripe charges 4.40% + $0.30 (2.90% base plus 1.50% international card fee).

Why is my effective fee rate much higher than the advertised percentage on small orders?

Every transaction includes a flat per-order charge ($0.49 on PayPal Goods and Services, $0.30 on Stripe and Square Online, or $0.10 on Square In-Person) that does not shrink when the sale price drops. On a $5.00 digital download paid via PayPal Goods and Services, the $0.49 fixed charge alone equals 9.80% of the sale, pushing the total fee to $0.66 and the effective rate to 13.29%—nearly four times the advertised 3.49% rate. Above $250.00, the fixed charge drops below 0.20% of the order and the effective rate converges toward the headline percentage.

Does PayPal or Stripe return processing fees when I refund a customer?

No. Under current merchant terms for PayPal, Stripe, and Square, if you issue a full or partial customer refund after a transaction has settled, the processor returns the gross purchase price to the buyer by debiting your merchant balance, while keeping the original processing fee (percentage plus fixed charge). On a $100.00 PayPal Goods and Services order, a full refund leaves your business out of pocket by $3.98 even when no chargeback occurred.

Can businesses use PayPal Friends and Family to avoid the 3.49% + $0.49 fee?

No. PayPal's User Agreement strictly restricts Friends and Family transfers to personal, non-commercial payments between individuals. Accepting commercial payments for goods, freelance work, or digital services through Friends and Family voids PayPal Seller Protection, violates platform rules, and routinely triggers automated account holds, retroactive commercial fee assessments, or permanent account freezes.

Do these calculator results include VAT, sales tax, or currency conversion spreads?

No. The calculator estimates direct payment gateway fees based on published list rates for the gross amount entered. Note that payment processors deduct their percentage fee from the entire checkout total—including any sales tax, VAT, or shipping collected from the buyer—and cross-border currency conversion adds an extra 1.0% (Stripe) to 3.0%–4.0% (PayPal) spread over wholesale mid-market exchange rates.

Should I record gross sales or net payouts after fees for business accounting and taxes?

Standard accrual and cash accounting rules (including IRS Publication 334 and IFRS/GAAP revenue recognition standards) require recording the full gross invoice or checkout amount as gross revenue and recording payment processing fees separately as a deductible business operating expense. Recording only net bank deposits understates both top-line turnover and deductible merchant expenses, causing mismatches against annual processor tax statements such as Form 1099-K or VAT returns.

Does this payment fee calculator store my invoice amounts or client transaction data?

No. All standard and reverse fee calculations execute locally inside your browser using JavaScript, and no transaction amounts are saved on any server. Only if you explicitly click the optional AI insight button is an anonymous numeric summary of calculation mode, processor tier, amount, fee, net payout, and effective rate sent once to generate commentary—customer names, emails, and account credentials are never requested or collected.
