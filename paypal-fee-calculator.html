What this calculator does

This tool takes a transaction amount and a processor, and returns three numbers that matter to any business that accepts digital payments: the fee the processor will charge, the effective rate of that fee as a share of the sale, and the net amount that actually lands in your account. Switch into reverse mode, and the tool answers the opposite question. Instead of what you will receive from a given charge, it tells you what you need to charge in order to receive a specific net figure after fees are deducted.

The point of the tool is not to reproduce arithmetic that a spreadsheet could handle. It is to make the difference between the advertised percentage and the true cost of accepting payment visible, because that difference quietly eats into the margin of every sale and is easy to overlook when the rate is quoted as a single round figure. A processor that advertises 2.9 per cent is really charging 3.3 per cent on a ten dollar sale, and 2.96 per cent on a five hundred dollar sale. The percentage is the same; the effective rate is not.

Every calculation runs inside your browser. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored, and nothing is tracked. If the AI insight button is pressed, only an anonymous summary of the mode, the processor category, the amount, the fee, the net, and the effective rate is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

How payment processor fees work

Every card or wallet transaction passes through several layers. The customer's bank issues the card, the merchant's acquiring bank holds the account, a card network such as Visa or Mastercard routes the transaction, and a payment processor such as PayPal, Stripe, or Square sits between the merchant and the network. Each layer takes a cut, and the merchant-visible fee is the sum of all of them, bundled into a single charge by the processor.

The merchant-visible fee is quoted as a percentage plus a fixed per-transaction charge. The percentage portion covers the interchange fee that the customer's bank receives, the network fee that the card scheme receives, and the processor's own margin. The fixed portion covers the processing overhead that applies to every transaction regardless of size, plus the risk the processor carries on chargebacks and fraud.

Fee = (Charge amount × Percentage rate) + Fixed charge
Net amount received = Charge amount − Fee

That formula is the same across PayPal, Stripe, and Square. What differs between them is the percentage, the fixed amount, and which transaction types they classify as domestic, international, or in-person. The calculator uses the currently published US rates for each processor as a starting point, and the numbers should be checked against your own merchant statement before being treated as final.

PayPal fees

PayPal charges around 3.49 per cent plus a fixed fee for Goods and Services transactions in the US domestic market. The Goods and Services product is the one that carries PayPal's buyer protection and dispute resolution, and it is the standard choice for any commercial transaction on the platform. Friends and Family transfers are free within the same country, but they offer no buyer protection and PayPal explicitly prohibits their use for business transactions.

Beyond the base rate, several situations increase the PayPal fee. Cross-border transactions carry an additional percentage on top of the domestic rate. Currency conversion from a buyer's currency into your account currency adds a further spread. Receiving payments through a card rather than a PayPal wallet sometimes triggers a different rate. The fee schedule is available on PayPal's merchant fee page and is updated periodically.

Stripe fees

Stripe charges around 2.9 per cent plus a fixed fee per standard domestic card transaction. That base rate covers most US-issued cards and is the number most Stripe merchants see on the majority of their sales. International cards are charged at a higher rate because the issuer is overseas and the transaction carries more risk, and the additional percentage is published on Stripe's pricing page.

Stripe does not operate a consumer wallet, which means every transaction runs through the card networks. That is a difference from PayPal, which does hold a wallet and can process some transactions without a card being involved. The trade-off is that Stripe's fees are consistent across all customers, while PayPal's fees can vary depending on whether the buyer pays with a wallet balance or a card.

Stripe also offers custom pricing for high-volume merchants, and many businesses above a certain monthly processing volume can negotiate a lower rate. The published list price is a starting point rather than a fixed ceiling.

Square fees

Square is unusual among the major processors because it offers a lower rate for in-person payments than for online ones. In-person tap, dip, or swipe transactions run at 2.6 per cent plus a small fixed fee, while online checkout runs at 2.9 per cent plus a slightly larger fixed fee, matching Stripe. The rate difference reflects the reduced fraud exposure of a card-present transaction and the lower infrastructure cost of a face-to-face payment.

Square is popular with physical retailers, market vendors, personal services, and food and beverage operators, precisely because the in-person rate is competitive and the hardware is simple to set up. For a business that handles both in-person and online sales, the effective rate on the combined revenue depends on the mix, and the calculator can be run separately against each channel to see the two fee layers clearly.

Effective rate versus advertised rate

The advertised percentage is only half of the fee. The other half is the fixed charge, which is the same regardless of how large the transaction is. On a five hundred dollar sale, a thirty cent fixed charge is six basis points, which barely moves the effective rate. On a ten dollar sale, the same thirty cents is three hundred basis points, or three full percentage points, and the effective rate on that sale is closer to six per cent than to three.

The effective rate is the number that matters for pricing. A business that prices its products using the advertised percentage rather than the effective rate will find that its margins are consistently a few points lower than it planned. Over a year of thousands of transactions, that gap is real money, and it accumulates silently.

The calculator shows the effective rate directly, and the monthly projection table makes the cumulative effect visible. A seller who processes five hundred small transactions per month at twenty-five dollars each pays far more in fixed charges than a seller who processes fifty large transactions at two hundred and fifty dollars for the same revenue, even though both use the same processor at the same percentage.

Reverse mode and pricing from net

Reverse mode answers a different question. Instead of what fee you pay on a given charge, it tells you what charge is required to receive a specific net amount. This is the mode freelancers and service businesses use most often, because the pricing conversation usually starts with the amount the seller wants to end up with, not with the amount the client pays on the invoice.

Required charge = (Net amount + Fixed fee) ÷ (1 − Percentage rate)

The formula inverts the standard calculation and accounts for the fact that both the percentage and the fixed charge apply to the required charge, not to the net amount. A freelancer who wants to receive five hundred dollars after PayPal fees needs to invoice a little over five hundred and twenty dollars, because the fee is charged on the larger figure. The calculator handles this in reverse mode without the user needing to work through the algebra.

The same logic applies to product pricing. A business that wants to achieve a specific net revenue per sale, after both payment processor fees and product costs, can use the reverse mode to back out the required list price. Doing this consistently prevents the slow margin erosion that occurs when fees are treated as an afterthought rather than as a cost of sale.

Common misunderstandings

Assuming the percentage is the whole fee. The percentage is only part of the cost. The fixed charge is the other part, and on small sales it is the larger of the two. A seller who prices using the percentage alone understates the true fee on every transaction below about fifty dollars.

Treating refunds as free. Most processors do not return the original processing fee when a sale is refunded, and some charge an additional fee to process the refund itself. A refunded transaction can therefore cost the seller the full original fee plus a refund charge, with no revenue to offset it.

Assuming Friends and Family is a business option. PayPal prohibits the use of Friends and Family transfers for commercial transactions and can reverse the payment or restrict the account if it detects a pattern. The rate advantage is not worth the account risk.

Comparing processors on the headline rate only. A processor with a lower percentage but a higher fixed charge can be more expensive on small sales than a processor with a higher percentage and a lower fixed charge. The correct comparison is the effective rate at the seller's typical transaction size, not the advertised percentage.

Ignoring currency and cross-border surcharges. Sellers who accept payments in currencies other than their own account currency are charged a conversion spread on top of the base fee. Sellers who receive payments from overseas cards are charged a cross-border rate that is a full percentage point or more above the domestic rate. Both are easy to miss in the checkout flow and show up only on the monthly statement.

Assuming low volume means low total fees. A business that processes a few large transactions a month pays less in total fees than one that processes thousands of small ones, but the fixed charge still applies to every transaction. A high-volume, low-ticket business can see payment fees take a materially larger share of revenue than a low-volume, high-ticket business, even when both use the same processor.

Where this calculator stops

This tool produces a fee estimate from the amount and processor you select, using the currently published list rates for each provider. It does not know your specific merchant agreement, any negotiated pricing you have arranged with the processor, any additional fees that apply to your account category, the exact interchange rate applicable to a given customer's card, or any tax treatment of fees in your jurisdiction. It also does not model refunds, chargebacks, dispute fees, or currency conversion spreads, all of which can raise the true cost well above what the calculator shows.

It also does not provide financial or tax advice. Whether a specific processor is the right choice for a given business, how fees should be treated for tax purposes, and whether to pass fees on to customers are all questions for a qualified accountant or business adviser. The tool is a planning aid, and the authoritative document is the merchant statement issued by the processor.

What the tool does give you is a fast, transparent, private estimate of the four numbers that matter most in payment processing: the fee, the effective rate, the net received, and the cumulative cost at volume. Those four numbers, taken together, are the difference between pricing that quietly loses margin every month and pricing that actually delivers the profit the seller intended.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage does PayPal take from a sale?

For a standard PayPal Goods and Services transaction on a US domestic sale, the rate is around 3.49 per cent plus a small fixed fee per transaction. International sales, currency conversions, and some account types carry additional charges on top of that base rate. The effective fee is always slightly higher than the advertised percentage because the fixed charge takes a larger bite out of small sales than of large ones.

What is the difference between Stripe and PayPal fees?

Stripe charges around 2.9 per cent plus a fixed fee per standard domestic card transaction. PayPal charges around 3.49 per cent plus a fixed fee for the equivalent Goods and Services product. PayPal also operates its own wallet ecosystem, so a portion of buyers can pay without a card being involved at all, which can lower the overall processing cost in some cases. Stripe has no wallet and always processes through the card networks.

Why is the effective rate higher than the advertised rate?

The advertised rate is the percentage portion of the fee only. The real cost also includes a fixed per-transaction charge, which is a flat amount regardless of the sale size. On a small sale, the fixed charge represents a much larger share of the total, which is why a two dollar transaction on a thirty cent fixed fee shows an effective rate several percentage points higher than the headline percentage.

How do I use the reverse fee mode?

Reverse mode answers a different question. Instead of asking what fee you will pay on a given sale, it asks what you need to charge in order to receive a specific net amount after the fee is deducted. Enter the net figure you want to receive, choose the processor, and the calculator works backward through the percentage and fixed fee to give you the required invoice total.

Do these fees include tax or currency conversion?

No. The calculator only estimates the payment processor fee on the sale. Sales tax, VAT, withholding tax, currency conversion charges, refunds, chargebacks, and dispute fees sit outside the base calculation and should be checked separately in the processor's statement. A sale that is refunded can carry the original processing fee without returning it, which raises the true cost above what this calculator shows.

How much do payment fees add up to over a year?

That depends on the sale size and the number of transactions. A business processing five hundred sales per month at a hundred dollars each pays a fixed percentage on every sale, and the fixed fee compounds across the volume. The monthly and annual projection in this calculator shows how the total fee grows as the number of transactions rises, which is often higher than the seller expects once the volume is visible in one column.

Are in-person card fees cheaper than online fees?

Yes, usually. Square's in-person rate is lower than its online rate, and the same pattern holds across most processors. The reason is risk and cost: a face-to-face transaction has a physical card present, which reduces fraud exposure, and the processor does not have to cover the same chargeback risk or infrastructure cost as an online checkout. A business that handles both channels should expect two different rates on its statement.

Does this calculator store my transaction details?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored on any server. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the mode, the processor category, the transaction amount, the fee, the net amount, and the effective rate is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Your customer names, invoice numbers, and account details are never collected because they are never asked for.
