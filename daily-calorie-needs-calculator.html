What daily calorie needs mean

Daily calorie needs are an estimate of the energy your body uses across a normal twenty-four-hour period. Calories are a unit of energy supplied by food and drinks. Your body spends that energy on keeping organs working, digesting food, moving through the day, training, and maintaining body temperature. The number this tool produces is not a target you have to hit every day, and it is not a measurement of your metabolism. It is a planning number that helps you think about food in relation to how your week actually looks.

Two people who weigh the same and are the same height can still have very different calorie needs, because muscle mass, daily movement, sleep, stress, and medical conditions all shift the total. That is why a calculator can only give a starting point, and why the more useful exercise is to compare the estimate against your own trend over two or three weeks. If the estimate says your maintenance is around two thousand four hundred calories but your weight is stable on two thousand two hundred, then two thousand two hundred is closer to your real maintenance, and the estimate is the thing that needs adjusting.

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation

The calculator estimates basal metabolic rate with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, published in 1990 by Mifflin, St Jeor and colleagues in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. It uses weight in kilograms, height in centimetres, age in years, and a small sex-specific constant. It replaced the older Harris-Benedict equation as the preferred tool in most clinical and nutrition settings, because it performs slightly better on average and does not overestimate as much in people with overweight or obesity.

Men: BMR = (10 × weight kg) + (6.25 × height cm) − (5 × age) + 5
Women: BMR = (10 × weight kg) + (6.25 × height cm) − (5 × age) − 161

Two features of the equation are worth noticing. The weight term carries a coefficient of ten, which means each extra kilogram adds about ten calories to BMR. The age term subtracts five calories per year, which reflects the slow decline in resting energy as muscle mass tends to fall and body composition shifts. The sex constant differs by about one hundred and sixty-six calories, which reflects the average difference in body composition between men and women rather than any inherent difference in metabolic rate per kilogram of tissue.

How activity multipliers work

The activity multiplier is the second half of the calculation and it captures everything that is not at rest. Walking to the bus, cooking, carrying children, standing at work, formal training, and the small ongoing cost of staying warm all sit inside that number. The standard multiplier set has been used in nutrition guidance for decades, and each level is a broad shortcut rather than a personal measurement.

LevelMultiplierTypical pattern
Sedentary1.20Desk work, little structured exercise, no walking commute
Lightly active1.375Light exercise one to three days a week, modest daily walking
Moderately active1.55Moderate exercise three to five days a week, active daily routine
Very active1.725Hard exercise six or seven days a week, or a physically demanding job
Extra active1.90Two training sessions a day, or heavy manual work plus training

The difference between the lowest and highest multipliers is roughly a factor of one and a half, which for a person with a BMR of one thousand six hundred calories means a range from about one thousand nine hundred calories at the sedentary end to over three thousand calories at the extra active end. That is a large spread, and it is the reason the multiplier is the most important field on the form. It is also the field people most often overestimate, because they remember their best training week rather than the average.

A useful check. If you feel like you would be exhausted at the level you selected, you have probably chosen one step too high. If you feel like you could do more, you have probably chosen one step too low. The honest middle is usually the right answer.

Choosing an honest activity level

The most common error is choosing an activity level that reflects the intention rather than the practice. Many people plan to train four times a week and actually train two. Many people plan to walk every day and drive on three of the seven. The estimate that produces the more useful result is the one that matches what happened last week, not what is meant to happen next week.

A second error is treating a single long training session as if it changed the whole week. Two hours on Saturday does not move the average as much as one hour on four separate days, because the body benefits from the frequency as well as the volume. The multiplier captures the frequency, not the peak.

A third error is forgetting that non-exercise movement is part of the total. A teacher who stands and walks for six hours a day is far more active than a software engineer who sits for six hours and then trains once in the evening, even if both report the same number of formal workouts. If you are unsure, err on the lower side and add movement to the week rather than adjusting the number to match a hoped-for routine.

Why a range beats a single number

The calculator produces a single maintenance figure for simplicity, but the range shown alongside it is closer to how the estimate should be used. A number is exact; a range acknowledges the uncertainty in the equation and in your own input. Two hundred calories either side of the maintenance estimate is a reasonable working window for most adults, and staying inside that window over the course of a week is a better goal than hitting the exact number on every single day.

This matters for a practical reason. Daily energy intake is naturally variable. Some days you eat more, some days less, and the body does not settle accounts on a twenty-four-hour clock. A range that you hit on average across the week is more useful and easier to sustain than a single target that you have to hit every single day.

If you want to use the number for planning, treat the middle of the range as the working estimate, and adjust up or down based on how your weight and energy feel over two or three weeks. A slow change in one direction is the signal that the estimate needs a small adjustment, not a large one.

Consider a thirty-four-year-old woman who weighs sixty-eight kilograms, is one hundred and sixty-five centimetres tall, and describes her usual week as lightly active with three short runs and a walk on the weekends. Her BMR works out as follows.

If this person tracked her weight for three weeks on around two thousand calories a day and it stayed stable, then the estimate is close to her real maintenance. If her weight crept up by half a kilogram across the three weeks, then her true maintenance is probably closer to nineteen hundred calories, and the estimate needs a small downward adjustment. If it dropped, the opposite is true. The estimate is a hypothesis; the trend is the evidence.

Common mistakes

Eating your BMR number. BMR is what you would burn if you lay still all day. Eating that amount every day is well below what most people need, and sustained restriction that low can reduce muscle mass and slow the metabolism further. Treat the maintenance estimate as the baseline, not BMR.

Choosing the wrong activity level. Most people overestimate their activity. If you are honest about the average, and not the best week, the estimate is likely closer to the truth.

Treating the number as exact. It is an estimate with a ten to fifteen per cent margin on either side. Using a range is more useful than chasing an exact figure.

Weighing yourself every day and reacting to every change. Daily weight swings by a kilogram or more from water, food, and hormones. Compare weekly averages, not daily readings.

Ignoring protein and fibre. Calories are one part of the picture. Protein supports muscle, fibre supports digestion and satiety, and both influence how a calorie target feels in practice.

Using the tool for pregnancy, breastfeeding, or children. The equation was validated in adults. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, and growth in children and adolescents need a different approach with professional guidance.

Ignoring how you feel. Persistent hunger, poor sleep, low energy, and low mood are signals that the plan is not working for your body, regardless of what the number says.

Where this tool stops

This calculator estimates resting energy with a population equation and applies a standard activity multiplier. It does not measure your metabolism directly, and it does not account for medications, medical conditions, thyroid disorders, body composition, or individual metabolic variation. It does not plan meals, recommend macronutrient splits, or replace a consultation with a doctor or a registered dietitian.

The tool is designed for general adult education and planning. It is not intended for pregnancy, breastfeeding, children, adolescents under the age of eighteen, athletes at the elite level, people recovering from eating disorders, or anyone with a medical condition that affects energy balance. If any of those apply, work with a qualified professional instead of relying on the numbers on this page.

What the tool does give you is a fast, transparent, private way to see an estimate, understand the equation behind it, and compare the estimate against your own trend. Used together with a two-to-three week weight trend and the guidance of a doctor or a dietitian if you need it, it is a practical starting point for anyone who wants to understand their daily energy needs.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Mifflin-St Jeor equation and why is it used?

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation is a predictive formula published in 1990 by Mifflin, St Jeor and colleagues in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. It estimates resting energy expenditure from weight in kilograms, height in centimetres, age in years, and a small sex-specific constant. It is one of the most widely used equations in clinical and general practice because it was validated across a broad range of adults and performs slightly better on average than older formulas such as the Harris-Benedict equation, especially in people with overweight or obesity.

What is the difference between BMR and TDEE?

BMR stands for basal metabolic rate and is the energy your body uses at complete rest, in a controlled environment, before food is digested. TDEE stands for total daily energy expenditure and includes BMR plus every other source of energy use: eating and digesting food, walking, household tasks, formal exercise, and the small ongoing cost of staying warm. This calculator shows BMR first, then multiplies it by an activity factor to give the maintenance estimate that most people find more useful for planning food.

How accurate is a calorie needs estimate?

A predictive equation gives a population average, not a personal measurement. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation is accurate to within roughly ten per cent for most adults, but individual variation can be larger. Muscle mass, genetic variation, thyroid function, some medications, and the exact amount of daily movement all shift the real number. Treat the maintenance estimate as a starting point. If you track intake and body weight for two or three weeks, you can compare the estimate against the actual trend and adjust as needed.

Should I eat my BMR number to lose weight?

No. BMR is the energy you would use at complete rest, and eating that figure every day is well below what most people need. It is safer to treat the maintenance estimate as the baseline and reduce from there in a modest, sustainable way, ideally with professional guidance. Very low calorie intakes can reduce muscle mass, lower energy levels, and disrupt hormones. If you are planning a significant change to your eating, speak to a doctor or a registered dietitian rather than relying on a calculator.

Why does the activity level change the result so much?

The activity multiplier is the second half of the calculation and it captures everything that is not at rest. The difference between a sedentary week and a very active week can easily be five hundred to a thousand calories a day for the same body, because exercise and daily movement add up. The multiplier is a broad shortcut rather than a measurement, so it cannot tell whether your week looks like your busiest day or your quietest one. Choosing the honest middle of the range usually produces the better estimate.

Does this calculator work for teenagers?

The calculator accepts ages from fifteen upwards, and the equation will produce a number for a teenager. However, energy needs during adolescence are strongly influenced by growth, pubertal stage, and individual variation, and the equation was originally validated in adults. A teenager planning meals should work with a parent, a school health service, or a doctor rather than relying on an adult equation alone. The tool is intended as an educational aid, not as a prescription for a young person.

Can I use this if I am pregnant or breastfeeding?

No, not for planning intake. Pregnancy and breastfeeding increase energy and nutrient needs in ways that depend on trimester, milk volume, and individual factors, and a general adult equation does not capture those changes. This tool is not intended for pregnancy or breastfeeding. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, or planning to become pregnant, follow the guidance of your doctor or a registered dietitian, who can factor in prenatal supplements, blood work, and any conditions you may have.

How do I convert pounds to kilograms and inches to centimetres?

One pound is about 0.4536 kilograms, so a person weighing 150 pounds weighs about 68 kilograms. One inch is exactly 2.54 centimetres, so a person who is 5 feet 8 inches tall is 68 inches, which is about 173 centimetres. If you weigh yourself regularly in pounds, divide by 2.205 to get kilograms, and if your height is in feet and inches, multiply the number of inches by 2.54. The calculator accepts decimal values, so you can round to the nearest half kilogram or whole centimetre without losing much accuracy.

Why does my weight fluctuate even when I eat the same amount?

Daily weight moves for reasons that have nothing to do with fat. Hydration, sodium intake, carbohydrate stores in the muscles and liver, digestion, the timing of the last meal, hormonal cycle, and even the temperature of the room all shift the number on the scale. That is why a single weigh-in tells you very little and a weekly or multi-week trend tells you a lot. Weigh yourself under similar conditions each time, average the readings across the week, and compare weeks, not days.

Is my data stored or shared?

No. Everything you enter stays in your browser tab, and the arithmetic runs locally using JavaScript. No number is transmitted to a server, and nothing is saved. The page is a static file, so there is no database behind it. If you press the AI insight button, only anonymous metadata is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. That payload contains the age band, the sex category, the activity multiplier, and the computed values, never your exact age, weight, height, or any identifier.
