What this checker actually does

This tool compares a single score against a single reference number. That is the whole idea. You enter the result you received, choose the scoring system it belongs to, pick a university and a course, and the checker tells you whether your score sits comfortably above the reference, right on it, or below it.

The reference number is what we call a baseline. A baseline is a planning estimate drawn from published admissions pages, official prospectuses, and past-year placement lists. It is not the same thing as an official cut-off for the current cycle, and it is not a promise about what will happen when you apply. What it does give you is a fast, honest starting point: a way to see roughly where you stand before you spend hours digging through university websites.

The checker covers six scoring systems used across the continent. JAMB UTME and Post-UTME composites in Nigeria. WASSCE aggregates in Ghana. KCSE cluster points in Kenya. The South African Admission Point Score. The Uganda Advanced Certificate of Education point system. And the ACSEE aggregate used in Tanzania. Each system has its own direction, its own scale, and its own quirks, and the checker handles all three.

Everything runs inside your browser. The comparison, the status band, the gap figure — all of it is calculated on your device. Nothing you type is sent anywhere unless you deliberately press the optional AI insight button, and even then only an anonymous summary of the calculation travels.

Why cut-off points are never fixed

Every year, thousands of students search for a course cut-off, find a number, and treat it as if it were a rule. It is not. A cut-off is a settlement point that emerges after admissions have closed. It is the score of the last candidate who got in. It only becomes known in hindsight, and it changes every single cycle.

Three forces move a cut-off up or down. The first is demand. If twice as many candidates apply to a programme in a given year, the cut-off rises, sometimes sharply. The second is capacity. If a department adds ten places, the cut-off eases. If it loses ten, the cut-off tightens. The third is policy. A ministry directive, a change in the placement system, a new quota rule, or a shift in how post-secondary results are weighted can all move the number without any change in the applicant pool.

The practical consequence is that a score that comfortably cleared a programme last year might sit just below the line this year, and the reverse is also true. A cut-off published in 2023 is a historical fact, not a forecast. This is why the checker uses the phrase planning baseline rather than cut-off, and why the two-point band exists. It is a deliberate cushion against the natural year-to-year movement of the numbers.

The six scoring systems explained

Each of the six systems has its own structure. Understanding the direction and the scale of the one you sat is the first step to reading your result properly.

JAMB UTME (Nigeria). The Unified Tertiary Matriculation Examination is scored out of 400. Higher is better. Most Nigerian universities add a post-UTME screening score to the JAMB result, which produces a composite that typically sits on a 0 to 100 scale. The baselines in this checker reflect the JAMB-only portion, so if your institution uses a composite, you will need to compare against that separately.

WASSCE aggregate (Ghana). The West African Senior School Certificate Examination produces letter grades from A1 to F9. A1 is worth one point, F9 is worth nine. The aggregate that universities use is normally three core subjects plus the best three electives. Because smaller numbers mean stronger grades, this is the one system where lower is better.

KCSE cluster points (Kenya). The Kenya Certificate of Secondary Education produces a mean grade from A to E, and universities use a cluster-point system that weights specific subjects for specific programmes. Cluster points are stronger when higher, and the baselines here are shown as cluster points on the standard scale used by the Kenya Universities and Colleges Central Placement Service.

South African APS. The Admission Point Score converts National Senior Certificate results into a single number, usually on a scale of 0 to 42 or 0 to 50 depending on the institution. Higher is better. Some universities apply subject-specific minimums on top of the APS, which the checker cannot see.

UACE / A-Level points (Uganda). The Uganda Advanced Certificate of Education produces A-Level style grades that convert to points. The Ugandan point scale runs higher-is-better and is usually expressed as a total across three or four principal subjects. Makerere and other public universities apply subject-specific weightings that vary by programme.

ACSEE aggregate (Tanzania). The Advanced Certificate of Secondary Education Examination produces a points-based aggregate. For many Tanzanian programmes, especially under the central admission system, lower aggregates are stronger, in the same way that WASSCE aggregates work. The baselines here reflect that direction.

How to read the three statuses

When you run a check, you get one of three statuses. Each one has a specific meaning, and none of them is a promise.

High admission chance. Your score clears the baseline by at least two units in the direction that matters for your system. For JAMB, that means your score is at least two points above the baseline. For WASSCE, it means your aggregate is at least two points below the baseline. The two-point band exists because cut-offs move between cycles. A result that clears the line by one point could easily slip below it next year.

Borderline / highly competitive. Your score is right on the baseline, or one unit stronger. This is the most useful status to pay attention to, because it tells you that the programme is genuinely within reach but that the outcome will depend on factors the checker cannot see. Subject grades, quotas, and the strength of the applicant pool all matter more at this boundary than they do further out.

Below estimated baseline. Your score is weaker than the reference number. This is a signal to widen your search, not a rejection. Many strong candidates end up in excellent programmes through a less direct route, and a below-baseline result is usually the point where that wider search begins.

A note on the two-point band. The band is applied in the correct direction for each system. Higher-is-better scores need to exceed the baseline by two. Lower-is-better aggregates need to sit at least two below. This keeps the comparison meaningful across all six systems and makes the result easy to audit against the baseline shown on screen.

A worked example in each system

The best way to understand the checker is to walk through a few examples. Take a candidate in Ghana with a WASSCE aggregate of 9, applying to Medicine at the University of Ghana. The baseline used here is 8. Because lower is better for WASSCE, the candidate's score of 9 sits one unit above the baseline. That places them in the borderline band. The message is that Medicine is within reach but that the applicant pool will decide the outcome.

Now consider a Nigerian candidate with a JAMB score of 340, applying to Law at the University of Ibadan. The baseline shown is 290. Because higher is better for JAMB, the candidate clears the baseline by 50 points. That is comfortably beyond the two-point band, so the status is high admission chance. The note that comes with it reminds the candidate that Law also has subject requirements on English and Literature that the checker cannot verify.

In Kenya, a candidate with 41 cluster points applying to Engineering at the University of Nairobi sees a baseline of 40. The candidate clears by one point, which is inside the two-point band. Status: borderline. The message here is worth reading carefully, because Kenyan engineering placement is heavily affected by the KUCCPS cluster weighting, which weighs Mathematics and the physical sciences more than most students expect.

In South Africa, a candidate with an APS of 39 applying to Commerce at the University of Cape Town sees a baseline of 36. That is a clear three-point margin, so the status is high admission chance. The reminder that accompanies the result is that UCT's commerce faculty also requires specific subject results in Mathematics and English, and that the APS is only the first filter.

Each of these examples shows the same underlying structure. The score, the baseline, the direction, the gap. Once you understand that structure for the system you sat, the checker becomes a very fast way to sort through dozens of programme options in a few minutes.

Subject requirements that override the score

A high score does not fix a missing subject. This is the single most important thing to understand about eligibility, and it is where most candidates get caught out.

Every programme publishes entry requirements that sit on top of the score. For Medicine, that usually means credit in Biology, Chemistry, and either Physics or Mathematics. For Law, it usually means a strong pass in English and often in Literature or a related subject. For Engineering, it means Mathematics and Physics. For Accounting, it means Mathematics and often Economics. For Education, it means the subject you intend to teach.

If you fail one of those required subjects, no score rescues the application. A candidate with a WASSCE aggregate of 7 who has an E8 in Chemistry cannot be admitted to a programme that requires credit in Chemistry, no matter how strong the rest of the aggregate is. The programme simply rejects the application at the eligibility filter, before any ranking takes place.

This is why the checker shows a reminder under every result. It compares a score against a baseline. It does not know whether you hold the required credits. Before you rely on any number here, read the entry requirements for the exact programme you are applying to and confirm that you satisfy every subject rule. The requirements are usually published on the faculty's own page, in the prospectus, or in the official admission notice for the year.

Building a shortlist, not a single prediction

The most useful thing you can do with this checker is build a shortlist, not pin all your hopes on a single number. A well-constructed shortlist usually contains three groups of programmes.

The first group is ambitious. These are programmes where your score sits in the borderline band, or perhaps just below the baseline. You apply to one or two of these because the outcome is worth reaching for and because a strong subject combination or a favourable applicant pool can produce a pleasant surprise.

The second group is competitive but realistic. These are programmes where your score clears the baseline by a comfortable margin, usually between two and six points in the correct direction. You apply to two or three of these. They are the core of your application and the most likely source of a good offer.

The third group is safer. These are programmes where your score clears the baseline by a wide margin, or where the programme is at a less competitive institution but still teaches the subject you care about. You apply to at least one of these so that a good outcome is close to guaranteed.

A shortlist of this kind is much more robust than a single bet. It also forces you to actually look at programmes you might otherwise have dismissed, which is often how students discover a course that turns out to be a better fit than the one they originally had in mind.

The most common mistakes

A handful of mistakes come up again and again, across every country and every system. Knowing them in advance saves a lot of wasted time.

Using an old cut-off as if it were current. The most frequent error. A cut-off list from three years ago is a historical document. It can be useful as a rough guide, but it cannot be trusted as a planning figure for the current cycle.

Comparing the wrong scales. A JAMB score of 250 is not comparable to a WASSCE aggregate of 25. A KCSE mean grade of B is not the same as an APS of 30. Each system has its own scale, and mixing them produces nonsense. Always compare like with like.

Forgetting subject requirements. As covered above, a strong score does not compensate for a missing required subject. Read the requirements before you calculate.

Ignoring the direction of the scale. In WASSCE and ACSEE, lower is better. Many students instinctively read a lower aggregate as weaker, which is exactly the wrong way around. The checker handles the direction automatically, but it is worth knowing so you can sanity-check the result.

Relying on a single year's data. Cut-offs move. A programme that sat at 15 last year might sit at 13 or 17 this year. The two-point band exists precisely because of this movement, and it is worth taking seriously.

Assuming private universities work the same way. Many private institutions use their own admission criteria, sometimes with far more flexibility on scores and far more weight on interviews, portfolios, or subject grades. The baselines here focus on public institutions. If you are considering private universities, check their individual admission pages.

Where this tool stops

This checker does what a score-versus-baseline comparison can do, and it does it well. It gives you a fast, clear read on where your score sits against a planning reference across a range of institutions and programmes. It shows you the gap, the direction, and a status band.

It does not know about subject requirements, portfolio reviews, interviews, post-UTME scores, NBT results, quotas, or any of the many other factors that go into a real admissions decision. It does not know which cycle you are applying in, and it cannot know the cut-off for a programme that has not yet closed admissions for the year.

What it can do is start a conversation. Use it to narrow a list from forty programmes down to ten. Use it to check whether a score that felt weak is actually stronger than you thought, or whether a score you were confident about is closer to the line than you realised. Then take that shortened list and dig into the official pages for the specific programmes and the specific year you are applying in.

For anything formal, the university's own admissions office is the authority. The prospectus, the official notice, and the current placement rules are the documents that decide. The result from this checker is a useful first step, and it is designed to be exactly that.

Frequently asked questions

Does a high admission chance result guarantee admission?

No. A high chance result only means the score you entered clears the planning baseline by two points or more. Subject requirements, quotas, competition, and current-year policy still decide the outcome. Treat the result as a sign that a programme is worth investigating, not as a promise.

Why are some scores compared the other way round?

JAMB, KCSE cluster points, APS, and UACE points are stronger when the number is higher. WASSCE aggregates and ACSEE admission aggregates are stronger when the number is lower. The checker preserves the correct direction for each system so that a stronger result always reads as stronger.

Where do the baselines come from?

Each baseline is a planning reference drawn from official admissions pages, published prospectuses, and past-year placement lists. They are updated periodically but they are not live official cut-offs. Verify the current cycle with the specific institution before making any application decision.

What is the two-point band and why does it matter?

A score has to beat the baseline by at least two points (or two units, in the case of aggregates) to be flagged as a high chance. A score right on the baseline or one point above is treated as borderline. The band exists because real cut-offs move up and down between cycles, and a result that sat comfortably above the line last year can slip just below it the next.

Can I use this checker before my results are released?

Yes. Enter a range of plausible scores and treat each result as a scenario rather than a prediction. When your official results arrive, run the check again with the actual number and revisit your shortlist against the current published requirements.

Does the checker know about subject requirements?

No. It compares a score against a course baseline. It does not know whether you hold the required credit in English, Mathematics, or a science subject. A programme can reject a strong score if a required subject is missing, so read the programme's entry requirements before you rely on any number here.

How accurate is the African university data?

The dataset is a planning aid built from published sources and past cycles. Baselines shift from year to year, and some programmes do not publish fixed cut-offs at all. For anything formal, use the official admission notice for the exact programme and year you are applying to.

Is my score sent anywhere?

No. Everything you type stays inside your browser, and the eligibility check is performed locally. If you choose to press the optional AI button, only an anonymous summary of your inputs and the resulting status is sent to generate a short written response. Nothing is stored.

What should I do if the result is below the baseline?

A below-baseline result is a signal to explore options, not a dead end. Look at related programmes at the same university, less competitive institutions, different entry routes such as diploma-to-degree, and the possibility of improving specific grades in a subsequent sitting. Speak with an admissions officer before ruling anything out.
