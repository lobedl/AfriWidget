What this calculator works out

A mortgage is a long, slow, monthly transaction that is easy to get wrong. Before you sign anything, it is worth knowing three numbers: what you will actually pay each month, how much interest will build up over the full term, and how much of your monthly income will be going into it.

This calculator answers all three. It takes the property price, your down payment, the interest rate, and the term, and returns the monthly principal and interest, the total you will pay across the life of the loan, and a debt-to-income ratio if you provide your income. It then shows the balance year by year so you can see how slowly the loan shrinks at first and how quickly it shrinks at the end.

What it does not do is decide whether a mortgage is a good idea. That depends on your situation, your job, your savings buffer, and what the housing market in your city is doing. This is a calculator. It gives you numbers to think with, not a recommendation to buy.

How a mortgage payment is actually built

Every mortgage payment you make splits into two parts. Part of it covers the interest that has built up on the outstanding balance since your last payment. The rest goes towards paying down the principal, which is the money you actually borrowed.

What surprises most first-time buyers is how uneven that split is early on. In the first years of a 30-year loan, most of each payment goes to interest and only a small slice goes to principal. By the second half of the term, the picture flips: most of the payment cuts down the principal, and interest is a much smaller portion. That is not a trick or a scam. It is the natural consequence of compounding running against you at the start and for you at the end.

Take a concrete example. Suppose you borrow 200,000 at 6 percent over 30 years. The monthly payment works out to about 1,199. In the first month, roughly 1,000 of that is interest and only about 199 goes to principal. By year 15, the interest portion is closer to 700, and by the final year it is under 50. That shape shows up in every fixed-rate mortgage, regardless of the country.

The formula in plain words

The mathematics behind a mortgage is one formula, and it looks more intimidating than it is:

M = P × [ r(1 + r)^n ] ÷ [ (1 + r)^n − 1 ]

Each letter means something simple. M is the monthly payment you are trying to find. P is the loan amount, meaning the property price minus your down payment. r is the monthly interest rate, which is the annual rate divided by twelve. And n is the total number of monthly payments, which is the term in years multiplied by twelve.

The bit in brackets, the (1 + r) to the power of n, is what does the heavy lifting. It captures the fact that a small monthly rate, applied again and again over many months, produces a number much bigger than the rate itself. Over 30 years at 6 percent, that expression is roughly 5.9. It is why the loan grows so much in total cost even though the monthly payment feels manageable.

You do not need to run the formula by hand. The calculator does it in the background the moment you press the button. But knowing what it is doing helps you understand why changing the term from 30 years to 15 years has such a large effect on the total interest, and why a small change in the rate matters so much more over a long term than over a short one.

Why the down payment matters more than the rate

When people talk about buying a home, the conversation usually drifts straight to the interest rate. That is understandable, but it misses something important. The down payment is often the single biggest lever you have.

Every unit you put down reduces the loan amount, which reduces the interest that gets charged on that amount, which reduces the total you pay over the full term. The effect compounds. A buyer who puts down 30 percent instead of 10 percent on the same property saves not just the difference in principal, but all the interest that the extra principal would have generated over 20 or 30 years. On a 250,000 property at 6 percent over 30 years, moving from a 10 percent deposit to a 20 percent deposit saves well over 30,000 in total interest.

There is a second reason the deposit matters. In several markets, notably the United States, a deposit under 20 percent triggers an extra monthly fee commonly called private mortgage insurance. It protects the lender, not you, and it disappears only once your equity reaches the threshold. In the UK, similar products exist under names like "higher lending charge" or simply a higher interest rate for a higher loan-to-value. In South Africa, the deposit requirement varies by bank, and a larger deposit often translates into a lower rate as well as a smaller loan.

The practical takeaway is that if you are deciding between a slightly lower rate and a slightly larger deposit, the deposit usually wins. Rates can be renegotiated later if market conditions change. The money you did not borrow is permanent.

Choosing between a 15-year and a 30-year term

The term is the length of the loan, and it changes everything about the numbers. A shorter term means higher monthly payments but far less total interest. A longer term means lower payments but more interest accumulated across the life of the loan.

Here is a concrete comparison. Take a 300,000 loan at 6 percent, with no other changes. Over 15 years, the monthly payment comes to about 2,532 and the total interest is roughly 155,700. Over 30 years, the monthly payment drops to about 1,799, but the total interest rises to roughly 347,500. The monthly difference is about 733. The total difference in interest is nearly 192,000.

That gap is why the 15-year term has such a strong reputation as a wealth-building choice. Every mortgage payment you make on a 15-year term is doing roughly twice the principal work of the same payment on a 30-year term. You build equity much faster, and you free up your cash flow much sooner.

But the 30-year is not automatically worse. A lower monthly payment preserves cash for investments, emergency funds, business ventures, or simply for getting through a lean period without stress. The right answer depends on which you value more at this stage of your life, and how much cushion you have if something goes wrong.

A practical middle path. Many borrowers take the 30-year term for the safety of the lower payment, then pay extra on top of it whenever their cash flow allows. This keeps the flexibility of the long term while capturing most of the interest savings of a shorter one. The calculator below does not model extra payments, but the concept is simple: every extra amount paid goes straight to principal and cuts the loan down faster.

What a good debt-to-income ratio looks like

Before a lender agrees to a mortgage, they look at how much of your income the payment would consume. This is called the debt-to-income ratio, or DTI, and it comes in two versions.

The front-end DTI is just the mortgage payment divided by your gross monthly income. Most lenders in the US prefer this to sit below 28 percent. In the UK, the loan-to-income multiple matters more than a percentage, and lenders commonly cap borrowing at around 4.5 times annual income. In South Africa, banks typically allow monthly repayments of up to 30 percent of gross income.

The back-end DTI adds every other monthly debt you have, from car loans to credit cards to student loans, and divides the total by your income. This is the number most lenders actually care about. The traditional benchmark is 36 percent or lower. Many lenders will stretch to 43 percent. A few will go higher if you have compensating strengths such as a large deposit or a long, stable employment record.

The calculator above shows both figures whenever you provide your income. If your back-end DTI comes out above 43 percent, most lenders will either decline the application or offer a smaller loan. That is not a moral judgement on your finances; it is simply the lender protecting itself against the possibility that your income drops while your debts stay.

Extra payments and the interest you save

One of the quietest ways to save money on a mortgage is to pay slightly more than required, as often as you can. This is not a trick, and it does not require any special product. Every extra amount you pay goes directly to reducing the principal, which reduces the interest charged the following month, which reduces the balance further, and so on.

The effect is bigger than most people expect. On a 300,000 loan at 6 percent over 30 years, paying an extra 100 a month would cut the term by roughly 5 years and save around 65,000 in interest. Paying an extra 200 a month cuts the term by more than 8 years and saves closer to 105,000. Small consistent overpayments do the work that a large lump sum would do, just more slowly.

Two things are worth checking before you commit to overpaying. First, some lenders charge an early repayment penalty, especially in the first few years. In many countries the penalty is small or nonexistent, but it exists in others, and it is worth reading the loan offer carefully. Second, if your mortgage rate is very low and you can earn a higher after-tax return on the same money somewhere else, the maths might favour investing over overpaying. That is a decision about risk and liquidity as much as it is about returns.

How mortgage markets differ around the world

This calculator uses the standard amortisation formula, which is the same worldwide. What changes between countries is the surrounding environment: typical rates, usual terms, deposit expectations, and the fees that come with the loan.

In the United States, 30-year fixed-rate mortgages are the norm, and the government-sponsored entities Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have shaped a market where fixed rates are common and long. In the United Kingdom, fixed rates usually last 2 to 5 years and then move to a variable rate, so the total cost over 25 years depends heavily on what happens at each renewal. In Canada, fixed terms are typically 5 years even when the amortisation period is 25 years. In Australia, variable rates are common and many borrowers split between fixed and variable portions.

In South Africa, mortgage rates are typically linked to the prime rate set by the Reserve Bank, so they move with monetary policy. In Nigeria, mortgage rates have historically been high and terms shorter than in Western markets, though products have improved in recent years. In Kenya, mortgages are usually priced off the Central Bank Rate plus a margin, and terms of 15 to 25 years are common. In India, floating-rate home loans dominate, tied to an external benchmark, with terms of 20 to 30 years.

None of this changes the arithmetic. But it changes the interpretation. A 6 percent rate is considered high in Japan and low in Nigeria. A 25-year term is standard in the UK and unusually short in the US. When you compare offers across markets, use the same inputs each time and remember that the loan itself is only one part of the true cost.

What this monthly figure does not include

The figure at the top of the results panel is principal and interest. That is the pure loan repayment, and it is the same number your bank will use to calculate the loan itself. It is not the same as your total monthly housing cost, and it is worth being clear about what sits outside it.

  • Property tax. In most countries, owners pay an annual tax based on the assessed value of the property. In the US it is often collected monthly into an escrow account and paid on your behalf; in the UK it is called council tax and paid directly. Either way, it does not appear in this figure.
  • Buildings and contents insurance. Required by most lenders as a condition of the loan, and usually paid monthly or annually. Cost depends on the property and where it is.
  • Private mortgage insurance or equivalent. As mentioned above, this is an extra monthly fee in several markets when the deposit is below a threshold.
  • Service charge or homeowners association fee. Applies to apartments, gated communities, and some planned estates. Can be substantial in high-rise buildings.
  • Maintenance and repairs. Rarely counted in mortgage estimates, but real. Boilers break, roofs leak, and paint does not last forever. A reasonable planning figure is 1 percent of the property value per year, set aside monthly.
  • One-off costs. Arrangement fees, valuation fees, legal fees, stamp duty or transfer tax, and moving costs all sit outside the monthly figure but are part of the true cost of buying.

When you add all of those on top of the principal and interest figure, the real monthly outflow is usually 25 to 40 percent higher than the loan payment alone, depending on the market and the property type. It is worth running those numbers separately before you commit.

Frequently asked questions

Does this mortgage calculator include property taxes and insurance?

No. The figure you see is principal and interest only, which is the pure loan repayment. Your real monthly housing cost will also include property tax, buildings and contents insurance, and in some cases private mortgage insurance, a service charge, or a homeowners association fee. Those vary a lot by country, city, and property type, so they are best added separately once you know the local figures.

How accurate is the monthly payment figure?

The arithmetic is accurate to the standard amortisation formula that banks use. What can make the real figure differ are arrangement fees added to the loan, a different compounding convention, an introductory rate that expires, or a lender that quotes an effective annual rate rather than a nominal one. Use the number as a close estimate, then confirm the exact figure with the lender before signing anything.

Why does a thirty-year loan end up costing so much more than a fifteen-year loan?

Because interest is charged on the outstanding balance every month, and a longer term keeps a large balance alive for far more months. Even though the monthly payment is lower on the thirty-year, the total interest paid can be more than double the fifteen-year figure on the same loan amount at the same rate. The trade-off is cash flow versus total cost.

What is private mortgage insurance and does this calculator include it?

Private mortgage insurance, or PMI, is a fee some lenders charge when the down payment is below a certain threshold, most commonly twenty percent in the US. It protects the lender, not you, and it is normally added to the monthly payment until your equity reaches the threshold. This calculator does not include it. If your deposit is small, ask the lender whether a similar fee applies to your loan.

Can I use this for a home loan in South Africa, Nigeria, or Kenya?

Yes. The standard amortisation formula the calculator uses is the same one most banks apply worldwide. What varies between countries is the typical interest rate, the maximum term, the minimum deposit, and the additional fees that get bundled in. Enter the figures your bank has quoted, and the arithmetic will hold.

Should I try to pay off my mortgage early?

It depends on your interest rate relative to what your money could earn elsewhere, and on whether your loan has a prepayment penalty. Paying an extra amount each month is mathematically powerful because every extra unit goes straight to principal. But if the same money can earn a higher after-tax return in a savings account or an investment, that route may leave you better off. Run the numbers for your own situation before deciding.

What is the difference between interest rate and APR?

The interest rate is what the lender charges on the balance. The APR, or annual percentage rate, is a wider number that also folds in arrangement fees, valuation fees, and some other costs. Two loans with the same headline rate can have different APRs. When comparing offers, look at the APR alongside the rate, because it is a fairer reflection of the true cost.

What debt-to-income ratio do I need to qualify for a mortgage?

It varies by country and lender, but the common benchmark is a back-end DTI, meaning all monthly debts divided by gross monthly income, of thirty-six percent or lower. Many lenders will consider up to forty-three percent, and a smaller number will go higher with compensating factors such as a large deposit or a long employment history. Anything above forty-five percent becomes difficult without special programs.
