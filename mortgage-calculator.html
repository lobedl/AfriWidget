What this mortgage calculator works out (and how to read every output)

A residential mortgage is typically the largest debt contract a household signs, stretching across 180 to 360 monthly billing cycles. Because small changes in your deposit, nominal rate, or repayment horizon compound across decades, relying on a rough mental estimate can misstate your lifetime borrowing cost by tens of thousands of currency units. Before making an offer on a property or locking an interest rate with a bank, you need clarity on three core dimensions: your contractual monthly repayment, the cumulative interest charged across the full loan term, and the share of gross household income that the debt will absorb.

This calculator computes all three locally inside your browser across twelve major currencies. Once you enter the agreed or asking property price, your down payment (either as a percentage or a cash deposit, which stay synchronised automatically), the loan term in years, and the lender's nominal annual interest rate, the results panel generates four headline metrics alongside your monthly principal-and-interest installment:

  • Loan amount (principal borrowed): The net capital financed after subtracting your cash deposit from the purchase price. Every interest calculation and lender loan-to-value (LTV) check starts from this figure rather than the headline property price.
  • Total interest over the full term: The cumulative cost of renting the bank's capital across every scheduled month assuming no rate changes and no overpayments.
  • Total cost (loan plus interest): The combined principal and interest outflow from month one to final loan clearance, excluding upfront purchase taxes and ongoing property bills.
  • Interest per unit borrowed (interest-to-loan ratio): Total interest divided by the initial loan amount, displayed as a multiple such as 1.28×. An interest multiple above 1.00× means you will pay more in lifetime interest than the original principal you borrowed—a standard reality on 25-year and 30-year loans whenever nominal rates sit above roughly 5.5 percent.

When you also enter your optional gross monthly income and recurring non-mortgage debt obligations (vehicle finance, student loans, personal loans, and minimum credit card payments), the calculator unlocks a Debt-to-Income (DTI) underwriting panel showing both your front-end housing ratio and your back-end total debt ratio, followed by a complete year-by-year amortisation schedule tracking your ending loan balance, annual principal reduction, and annual interest expense from Year 1 through final payoff.

How a mortgage payment is built and why early years feel so slow

Every standard repayment mortgage (called a capital-and-interest mortgage in the United Kingdom or a reducing-balance home loan in South Africa, Kenya, and India) keeps your total monthly payment constant while continuously shifting the internal split between interest and principal. Each month, the bank multiplies your unpaid loan balance from the end of the previous month by one-twelfth of your nominal annual interest rate. That interest charge gets paid first out of your monthly installment. Only the leftover remainder reduces your principal debt.

What catches many first-time buyers off guard is how heavily front-loaded the interest portion is during the first third of a long mortgage. In the opening years of a 30-year loan, your unpaid principal is near its peak, so monthly interest consumes 75 to 88 percent of every payment you make. That front-loading is not a hidden bank penalty or "rule of 78" surcharge; it is the direct mathematical result of charging periodic interest on the outstanding balance while holding the total monthly payment flat so your household cash flow remains predictable.

Consider a borrower financing $300,000 at 6.00 percent over 30 years. The fixed monthly payment is $1,798.65 (rounded to $1,799 on summary charts). In Month 1, the interest due on $300,000 at 0.50 percent per month is exactly $1,500.00, leaving just $298.65 to reduce the loan balance. In Month 2, the balance has dipped to $299,701.35, so Month 2 interest drops slightly to $1,498.51 and principal reduction nudges up to $300.14. Month after month, the principal slice compounds upward at the exact rate the interest slice shrinks. Yet it takes until Year 18 (Month 222)—more than halfway through the 30-year schedule—before the monthly principal reduction finally overtakes the monthly interest charge.

This slow early amortisation carries an important practical lesson for buyers who expect to move within three to five years. If you purchase with a small 5 percent deposit on a 30-year schedule, five years of regular payments at 6.50 percent reduce your loan principal by only about 6.6 percent of the borrowed amount. Once you factor in estate agent selling commissions (typically 1.5 to 6 percent depending on the country) and legal transfer costs, a flat housing market can leave you with almost zero net cash equity when you sell—and a modest 8 percent market downturn can place you in negative equity, where the remaining mortgage exceeds the net sale proceeds of the home.

The standard amortisation formula in plain words (plus worked example)

Underneath the form above, the calculator evaluates the universal fixed-payment reducing-balance amortisation equations used by retail lenders worldwide. Expressing every variable in plain arithmetic shows exactly how your inputs interact:

Loan Principal (P) = Property Price − Down Payment
Monthly Interest Rate (r) = (Annual Nominal Rate % ÷ 100) ÷ 12
Total Monthly Payments (n) = Loan Term in Years × 12
Compounding Factor (F) = (1 + r) raised to the power of n
Monthly Payment (M, when r > 0) = P × (r × F) ÷ (F − 1)
Monthly Payment (M, when r = 0) = P ÷ n
Total Cost = M × n  |  Total Interest = Total Cost − P  |  Interest per Unit = Total Interest ÷ P
Front-End DTI (%) = (M ÷ Gross Monthly Income) × 100
Back-End DTI (%) = ((M + Other Monthly Debts) ÷ Gross Monthly Income) × 100

Notice the explicit zero-interest branch (M = P ÷ n). If you enter 0% as the annual interest rate—for example when testing an off-plan property developer's interest-free installment schedule or a zero-markup family loan—the standard fraction would divide by zero, so the calculator cleanly divides the net principal equally across all scheduled months.

Worked Example 1: Verifying a $350,000 home purchase step by step

Suppose a household is buying a $350,000 home with a 20 percent down payment ($70,000), financing the remaining balance over 30 years at a quoted nominal interest rate of 6.50 percent. The household earns $7,500 in gross monthly income and pays $450 per month toward a car loan and student loan. Entering those exact values into the calculator produces the following step-by-step arithmetic:

  1. Net loan principal (P): $350,000 − $70,000 = $280,000.
  2. Monthly periodic rate (r) and payment count (n): r = 0.065 ÷ 12 = 0.00541667 per month; n = 30 × 12 = 360 months.
  3. Compounding factor (F): (1 + 0.00541667) raised to the 360th power = 6.991798.
  4. Monthly principal and interest (M): $280,000 × (0.00541667 × 6.991798) ÷ (6.991798 − 1) = $280,000 × 0.0378722 ÷ 5.991798 = $1,769.79 per month.
  5. Lifetime totals: $1,769.7919 × 360 = $637,125 total paid (exact unrounded product $637,125.08; the calculator multiplies exact floating-point monthly payment by 360, yielding $637,125 total cost and $357,125 total interest, or an interest-to-loan ratio of 1.28×).
  6. DTI underwriting check: Front-end DTI = ($1,769.79 ÷ $7,500) × 100 = 23.6%. Back-end DTI = (($1,769.79 + $450) ÷ $7,500) × 100 = 29.6%, earning the Comfortable verdict (well inside the 36 percent benchmark).
  7. Year 1 amortisation row: Across the first 12 payments ($21,237.50 paid in total), $18,108 goes to interest, $3,130 reduces principal, and the ending Year 1 balance stands at $276,870.

Down payment size, loan-to-value (LTV) tiers, and rate pricing thresholds

Homebuyers often obsess over central bank rate headlines while overlooking the single variable they control directly: the down payment (deposit) and its mirror image, the Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratio, calculated as (Loan Amount ÷ Property Valuation) × 100. Increasing your deposit creates a double compounding benefit that few borrowers model side by side.

First, every additional currency unit paid upfront reduces your starting principal P, permanently eliminating 20 to 30 years of compound interest on that borrowed slice. Second, mortgage lenders price credit risk in discrete LTV pricing bands—typically at 95%, 90%, 85%, 80%, 75%, and 60% LTV. Crossing one threshold—for example stretching your deposit from 9 percent (91% LTV) to 10 percent (90% LTV), or from 18 percent to 20 percent (80% LTV)—frequently unlocks a lower nominal interest rate on the entire mortgage balance while simultaneously eliminating high-LTV insurance surcharges.

5% Down ($15,000 · 95% LTV) $285,000 6.85% $1,867.49 $387,296 Highest rate tier; mandatory PMI in US/Canada or strict credit score floor.
10% Down ($30,000 · 90% LTV) $270,000 6.65% $1,733.31 $353,990 Saves $33,306 in interest vs 5% down; lower PMI factor or wider bank choice.
15% Down ($45,000 · 85% LTV) $255,000 6.55% $1,620.17 $328,260 Strong equity cushion against early market dips; reduced PMI surcharge.
20% Down ($60,000 · 80% LTV) $240,000 6.40% $1,501.21 $300,437 Eliminates US PMI completely; saves $86,859 in interest vs 5% down.
25% Down ($75,000 · 75% LTV) $225,000 6.25% $1,385.36 $273,731 Unlocks premier UK/SA pricing bands (75% LTV) and resilient equity from Day 1.

Table 1 illustrates a $300,000 property purchase across realistic LTV rate tiers. Moving from a 5 percent deposit ($15,000) to a 20 percent deposit ($60,000) requires $45,000 more cash upfront, yet it cuts the monthly loan repayment by $366.28 (before even counting the removal of monthly PMI) and slashes lifetime interest by $86,859.

However, there is a critical liquidity boundary: never empty your entire cash reserve to reach a higher deposit tier. Once cash is locked inside bricks and mortar, you cannot spend it at the supermarket or use it to repair a burst geyser or roof leak without applying to borrow it back. Always keep three to six months of essential household expenses—plus all one-time legal, tax, and moving costs—in liquid savings before committing the remainder to your down payment.

Choosing your loan term: 15-year vs 20-year vs 30-year trade-offs

The loan term selector in the calculator covers six standard horizons: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 years. Shortening the repayment term is the fastest mathematical way to crush total interest cost, even if the nominal interest rate stayed completely unchanged (and in many markets, 15-year fixed loans also carry a 0.40 to 0.75 percentage point lower interest rate than 30-year fixed loans).

To help you compare every option available in the dropdown above on an identical footing, Table 2 holds the borrowed principal constant at 300,000 units and the nominal interest rate constant at 6.50 percent across all six loan terms:

Look closely at the 20-year middle ground in Table 2—the standard home loan horizon in South Africa and Kenya, and an increasingly popular choice for UK and US buyers. Stepping down from 30 years to 20 years adds just $340.52 per month (+18.0%) to the required installment, yet it eliminates $145,820 in lifetime interest (-38.1%) and clears the mortgage ten full years sooner.

The contractual flexibility strategy: If your mortgage product has no early prepayment penalty, taking a 25-year or 30-year contractual term and setting up an automatic monthly overpayment equal to the 15-year or 20-year payment gives you the best of both worlds. During normal earning years, you pay off the loan on the accelerated schedule and capture the massive interest savings. If a job loss, maternity leave, medical bill, or business downturn hits, you can immediately drop back to the lower contractual payment without begging the bank for a loan modification.

Debt-to-income (DTI) ratios, loan-to-income caps, and rate stress testing

Passing your own spreadsheet budget is only half of buying a home; you also have to pass formal bank credit underwriting. Whenever you enter a monthly income figure in the calculator, it computes the two core affordability ratios used by retail credit committees worldwide:

  • Front-end DTI (housing ratio): Your monthly mortgage repayment divided by gross pre-tax monthly income. Traditional North American underwriting known as the 28/36 rule prefers front-end housing costs (including property tax, insurance, and HOA dues) to stay at or below 28 percent of gross income. In South Africa, major retail banks (Standard Bank, FNB, Absa, Nedbank) apply a comparable 30 percent payment-to-income (PTI) ceiling on gross monthly pay alongside a net-surplus check under the National Credit Act.
  • Back-end DTI (total debt service ratio): Your monthly mortgage payment plus all other recurring contractual debts—car loans, student loans, personal loans, store cards, maintenance orders, and minimum credit card payments (typically assessed at 3 to 5 percent of revolving balances)—divided by gross monthly income.

In the calculator's DTI panel, a back-end ratio of 36.0 percent or lower receives a Comfortable rating; 36.1 to 43.0 percent receives Workable (matching the standard 43 percent US Qualified Mortgage general underwriting benchmark, where approval often depends on strong credit scores, stable employment, or post-closing cash reserves); and anything above 43.0 percent triggers Likely too high.

Two additional underwriting checks sit alongside DTI depending on where you live:

  1. Loan-to-Income (LTI) multiples in the United Kingdom and Ireland: Rather than relying solely on monthly DTI, UK lenders cap total mortgage borrowing at roughly 4.0 to 4.5 times gross annual household income. Under Bank of England Financial Policy Committee rules, no more than 15 percent of a lender's new residential mortgages may exceed 4.5 times income, reserving 5.0× or 5.5× multiples for high-income or regulated professional borrowers with low LTVs.
  2. Interest-rate stress testing: If you apply for a variable-rate loan or a short 2-to-5-year fixed deal, banks do not just test whether you can afford today's quoted rate. Underwriting engines routinely add a +2.0 to +3.0 percentage point stress buffer (for example testing a 6.0 percent quote at 8.0 or 9.0 percent) to verify that a central bank tightening cycle will not force your household into default. You can run that exact stress test yourself right now: calculate your baseline mortgage, then add 2.0 percentage points into the Annual interest rate box and check whether your back-end DTI still stays below 43 percent.

If you are self-employed, a company director, or paid partly through bonuses and commissions, remember that underwriters rarely accept your highest recent month. Most banks average your last two to three years of verified tax returns or audited financial statements (such as IRS Form 1040 Schedule C in the US, HMRC SA302 tax calculations in the UK, or SARS ITA34 assessments in South Africa) and haircut irregular bonus income by 20 to 50 percent.

Fixed-rate, variable-rate, tracker, and offset mortgages compared

Although two mortgages may share the exact same Year 1 monthly payment in this calculator, the legal structure of the interest rate determines who bears the risk when inflation and central bank policy rates move:

  • Full-term fixed-rate mortgages: Common in the United States (15-year and 30-year fixes) and offered on select affordable-housing schemes such as Kenya's KMRC-refinanced loans. Your nominal rate and monthly principal-and-interest payment never change for the entire life of the loan. If market rates fall, you can refinance downward (subject to closing costs); if market rates surge, your housing payment stays locked.
  • Short-term fixed deals (2, 3, or 5 years): Standard in the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand. Your rate is fixed for an initial window—say 5 years on a 25-year amortisation—after which the loan either reverts to the lender's higher Standard Variable Rate (SVR) or must be remortgaged/renewed onto a new deal at whatever market rates prevail in Year 6.
  • Variable, floating, and tracker mortgages: Dominant in South Africa (priced at the SARB Prime Lending Rate plus or minus a personalised credit concession, such as Prime minus 0.50%), Kenya (Central Bank Rate plus bank margin), and India (External Benchmark Lending Rate tied to the RBI Repo Rate). Whenever the central bank raises or cuts its benchmark rate, your monthly installment or remaining loan term adjusts within weeks.
  • Discount points (buying down the rate): In the US and some European markets, lenders let you pay upfront "points" at closing—where 1 point equals 1 percent of the loan amount—in exchange for a permanently lower interest rate (often 0.25 percentage points lower per point paid). To test whether points are worth buying, run the calculator twice (with and without the rate discount), find the monthly payment savings, and divide the upfront cost of the points by that monthly saving to find your break-even month. If it takes 54 months to break even and you might move or refinance within 4 years, skip the points.
  • Offset mortgages and access bonds: Widely available in the UK, Australia, and South Africa (where they are called access bonds). Your everyday savings or transaction balance sits in a linked account that offsets your mortgage principal before daily interest is calculated. Holding 30,000 in an offset account against a 250,000 mortgage means you are charged interest on only 220,000 while retaining instant penalty-free access to your cash buffer.

Extra payments, bi-weekly schedules, recasting vs refinancing

Because early mortgage installments send so little money toward principal, voluntary overpayments punch far above their weight. Every extra unit you pay above your scheduled monthly installment bypasses the interest column completely and deducts 100 percent from your unpaid principal balance. That shrinks next month's interest charge, which automatically makes next month's regular payment chip away slightly more principal as well—creating a compounding snowball in your favour.

Repayment Strategy ($300,000 at 6.50%, 30 Yr) Monthly Outflow Effective Payoff Time Total Interest Paid Interest Saved
Standard scheduled payment (baseline) $1,896.20 30 yrs 0 mos $382,633 $0
Add $100 extra per month $1,996.20 26 yrs 0 mos $321,639 $60,994
True bi-weekly (26 half-payments = 13 full/yr) ~$2,054.22 eq. 24 yrs 2 mos $295,377 $87,256
Add $250 extra per month $2,146.20 21 yrs 10 mos $262,297 $120,336
Add $500 extra per month $2,396.20 17 yrs 6 mos $202,874 $179,759

Table 3 demonstrates how modest, consistent principal reductions transform a $300,000 30-year loan at 6.50 percent. Notice the true bi-weekly strategy: paying half of your monthly payment every two weeks produces 26 half-payments per calendar year—equivalent to 13 full monthly payments instead of 12. That single extra payment per year shaves 5 years and 10 months off the mortgage and saves $87,256 in interest. You do not need to pay a third-party processor to set up bi-weekly debits; simply divide your monthly P&I by 12 (here $1,896.20 ÷ 12 = $158.02) and add that amount as a recurring monthly principal-only overpayment.

Before sending extra cash to your lender, verify three rules on your loan statement:

  1. Instruct "principal reduction," not "advance payment": In some banking portals, an unlabelled extra transfer merely marks next month's bill as paid early without recalculating interest immediately. Select the principal curtailment or capital reduction option explicitly.
  2. Check Early Repayment Charges (ERCs): Many UK fixed-rate mortgages cap penalty-free overpayments at 10 percent of the outstanding balance per year during the fixed period, charging a 1 to 5 percent fee above that allowance. In South Africa, Section 125 of the National Credit Act allows banks on large agreements to charge up to 90 days' interest if you settle a bond completely without giving 90 days' written notice.
  3. Know when to recast versus refinance: If you receive an inheritance, bonus, or sale of an asset and make a large lump-sum principal payment, your regular monthly payment stays the same by default while your payoff date moves closer. If your goal is to lower your required monthly payment without losing a low locked-in interest rate, ask your lender for a mortgage recast (re-amortisation). By contrast, a refinance originates a brand-new loan at current market rates—worthwhile only when market rates have dropped enough (often 0.75 to 1.00 percentage points or more) that monthly savings recoup the legal, valuation, and origination fees before you sell.

Worked Example 3: Kenyan commercial rate vs KMRC-backed mortgage (KES) and UK 5-year renewal check (GBP)

In high-rate markets, comparing concessional housing schemes against commercial bank pricing in the calculator reveals dramatic differences. Suppose a Nairobi buyer purchases an apartment for KSh 12,000,000 with a 20 percent deposit (KSh 2,400,000), borrowing KSh 9,600,000 over 20 years:

  • At a commercial bank floating rate of 14.50 percent, the monthly repayment is KSh 122,879.79 and total interest reaches KSh 19,891,149 (an interest-to-loan multiple of 2.07×).
  • At a Kenya Mortgage Refinance Company (KMRC) participating lender rate of 9.50 percent (for eligible loans within KMRC limits), the monthly payment drops to KSh 89,484.59 and total interest falls to KSh 11,876,303 (1.24×)—saving KSh 33,395.20 every month and KSh 8,014,846 over 20 years.

Similarly, UK buyers on 2-year or 5-year fixes can use the year-by-year amortisation table to model remortgage renewal shock. Borrowing £272,000 (£320,000 property with a 15% deposit) over 25 years at 4.75 percent produces an initial payment of £1,550.72 per month. Looking down to Year 5 in the amortisation table shows the remaining balance will be £239,966. Re-entering £239,966 with a 0% deposit over the remaining 20 years at 6.75 percent immediately shows that a two-point rate rise at renewal would lift the monthly payment to £1,824.62 (+£273.90 per month).

What this monthly figure excludes: PITI, escrow, levies, and cash-to-close

In housing finance, banking professionals distinguish between P&I (Principal and Interest—the number shown at the top of this calculator) and PITI + M (Principal, Interest, Taxes, Insurance, HOA/Levies, and Maintenance—the true monthly cash outflow required to keep the roof over your head). Mistaking a bank's P&I quote for your total housing cost is one of the most common causes of first-year homeowner cash stress.

Before committing to a property price, add two separate checklists onto the P&I output above:

1. Ongoing monthly ownership costs outside P&I

  • Property taxes, Council Tax, or municipal rates: Assessed annually by local government (often 0.5% to 2.5% of property value in the US, Council Tax bands A–H in the UK, or municipal property rates in South Africa and Kenya). US lenders typically collect one-twelfth of your annual tax and insurance bill each month into an escrow (impound) account alongside P&I.
  • Homeowners buildings insurance and credit life cover: Lenders universally mandate structural buildings insurance from the date of exchange or registration. In South Africa, Kenya, and several African markets, lenders also require a ceded mortgage protection / credit life insurance policy that settles the loan if the borrower dies or becomes permanently disabled.
  • Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI) or high-LTV fees: Charged monthly or upfront when your deposit sits below 20 percent in North America.
  • HOA dues, sectional title levies, or leasehold service charges: Mandatory monthly contributions covering security, lifts, exterior insurance, common gardens, and reserve funds in apartments and gated estates. Always inspect the body corporate or HOA financial statements for special levies before buying.
  • Maintenance and capital replacement reserve: Unlike tenants, homeowners pay for every failed water heater, roof repair, damp treatment, and repainting cycle. Setting aside 1 percent of the property's value per year (divided by 12 each month) prevents routine wear and tear from pushing you onto high-interest credit cards.

2. One-off upfront cash-to-close costs

  • Purchase transfer taxes: Stamp Duty Land Tax (UK), SARS Transfer Duty (South Africa), State/Provincial Land Transfer Tax (Canada/Australia), or Stamp Duty (Kenya/India/Nigeria).
  • Legal conveyancing and registration charges: Transfer attorney fees, deed office registration fees, title insurance (US), and separate mortgage/bond registration legal fees.
  • Lender arrangement, origination, and valuation fees: Product booking fees (often £999 to £1,499 in the UK or 1% origination in the US/Africa) plus independent RICS survey, home inspection, and bank valuation charges. Avoid adding arrangement fees onto the mortgage balance unless necessary, as doing so charges you 25 to 30 years of compound interest on an upfront administrative fee.

Real-world lender figures may differ due to daily interest accrual (365-day conventions), semi-annual compounding rules (such as Canadian fixed-rate mortgages), introductory fixed rates that revert to variable benchmarks, upfront arrangement fees added to the loan balance, compulsory credit life or mortgage insurance, property tax and insurance escrow adjustments, and lender-specific net-disposable-income underwriting formulas.

Verify Before Committing: Housing markets, tax laws, transfer duties, and lending regulations change periodically and vary by jurisdiction. Always verify exact figures against an official Loan Estimate (US), Mortgage Illustration / ESIS (UK), or Pre-Agreement Statement and Quotation (South Africa) issued by an authorised lender, and consult a licensed mortgage broker, conveyancing attorney, or independent financial adviser before signing a binding property purchase agreement.

Frequently asked questions

Does this mortgage calculator include property taxes and insurance?

No. The headline monthly figure displays principal and interest only, which is the contractual bank repayment on the loan balance. Your true monthly housing outflow will also include local property tax or Council Tax, buildings insurance, and—where applicable—private mortgage insurance, sectional title or HOA levies, and ongoing maintenance reserves. Because tax rates and insurance premiums vary sharply by municipality and property type, add those local costs separately when setting your household budget.

How accurate is the monthly payment figure?

The calculation is exact to the standard fixed-rate monthly-compounding amortisation equation used by retail banks worldwide, including zero-interest payment plans. Minor differences between this estimate and a formal bank offer usually come from arrangement fees capitalised into the loan principal, daily interest accrual across 28-to-31-day calendar months, semi-annual compounding conventions in countries such as Canada, or a reverting variable rate after an introductory fix. Always verify final numbers against your lender's Loan Estimate, Mortgage Illustration, or Pre-Agreement Statement.

Why does a thirty-year loan end up costing so much more than a fifteen-year loan?

Interest is charged every month on the remaining unpaid principal balance. Stretching a mortgage from 15 years to 30 years cuts the monthly principal reduction in early years by more than half, leaving a large balance exposed to monthly interest charges across 360 billing cycles instead of 180. On a 300,000 loan at 6.50 percent, a 30-year term lowers the monthly payment from 2,613.32 to 1,896.20, but total interest climbs from 170,398 to 382,633—more than double the interest cost.

What is private mortgage insurance and does this calculator include it?

Private mortgage insurance (PMI) is a risk premium charged by lenders—most notably in the United States and Canada—when a buyer's down payment is below 20 percent of the purchase price (an LTV above 80 percent). It insures the lender against default loss, not the borrower, and typically adds 0.4 percent to 1.5 percent of the loan amount per year onto your monthly bill until your equity reaches 20 percent. This calculator excludes PMI; in the UK and South Africa, lenders more commonly price low-deposit risk directly into a higher nominal interest rate.

Can I use this for a home loan in South Africa, Nigeria, or Kenya?

Yes. Commercial banks and housing finance institutions in South Africa, Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, and India use the exact same reducing-balance monthly amortisation formula implemented here. Simply select ZAR, NGN, KES, GHS, or INR in the currency selector and enter the nominal annual rate and term quoted by your lender—such as a prime-linked 20-year bond in South Africa, a KMRC-backed or commercial bank mortgage in Kenya, or an NHF or commercial facility in Nigeria.

Should I try to pay off my mortgage early?

Overpaying a mortgage generates a guaranteed, tax-free return equal to your loan's nominal interest rate because every extra unit paid reduces principal immediately and eliminates future compounding on that slice of debt. Early payoff usually makes strong financial sense when your mortgage rate is high (for example above 6 to 7 percent) and you already hold an emergency cash buffer. However, check your contract for early repayment charges during fixed periods, and compare your mortgage rate against the after-tax return available on employer-matched retirement contributions or high-yield treasury instruments.

What is the difference between interest rate and APR?

The nominal interest rate is the annual percentage charged strictly on your unpaid loan balance and is the rate you should enter into this calculator to find your monthly installment. The Annual Percentage Rate (APR)—or APRC in the United Kingdom—folds in mandatory upfront arrangement fees, origination points, valuation charges, and scheduled reverting rates across the loan term. Use the nominal rate to calculate your monthly cash outflow, and use the APR to compare the true all-in cost of competing loan offers.

What debt-to-income ratio do I need to qualify for a mortgage?

Underwriting thresholds vary by jurisdiction, but a back-end debt-to-income (DTI) ratio—all monthly debt payments including the new mortgage divided by gross monthly income—of 36 percent or lower is widely considered comfortable. Many lenders approve borrowers between 36 percent and 43 percent when supported by strong credit history, cash reserves, or a 20 percent deposit, while ratios above 43 percent frequently trigger declines or smaller loan offers. Remember that lenders include property tax, insurance, and HOA dues inside their official housing ratio even though this tool calculates DTI on principal and interest plus your entered other debts.

What is the difference between mortgage recasting and refinancing?

Mortgage recasting (also called re-amortisation) lets you make a lump-sum principal payment and ask your existing lender to recalculate a lower monthly payment across your remaining term while keeping your current interest rate intact, usually for a small administrative fee. Refinancing replaces your old mortgage with an entirely new loan—potentially at a new interest rate and new term—requiring full credit underwriting, property valuation, and legal closing fees. Recasting is ideal when you receive a cash windfall and already hold a lower interest rate than current market offers.

How does Canadian semi-annual compounding or daily interest calculation affect my payment?

In the United States, United Kingdom, South Africa, and most African and Asian markets, fixed-rate mortgages divide the nominal annual rate by 12. Under Canada's Interest Act, fixed-rate mortgages compound semi-annually rather than monthly, which makes the effective monthly rate slightly lower—for example, a 6.00 percent Canadian fixed quote produces an effective monthly rate of 0.4939 percent instead of 0.5000 percent, lowering the payment on a CAD 300,000 25-year loan by roughly CAD 11 per month. Daily-interest loans can also show tiny penny variations depending on the exact day of the month your debit order clears.

How much cash do I actually need at closing beyond the down payment?

Buyers typically need an additional 2 percent to 6 percent of the property purchase price in liquid cash on top of the down payment to cover one-time transaction costs, plus an untouched 3-to-6-month emergency fund. Depending on your country, closing costs include stamp duty or transfer duty, legal conveyancing and deed registration fees, bond registration charges, lender origination or arrangement fees, property valuation and structural survey costs, and initial property tax or insurance escrow reserves.

Does this mortgage calculator store my income, loan figures, or personal data?

No. All mortgage payment, debt-to-income, and year-by-year amortisation calculations run 100 percent locally in your browser using client-side JavaScript. None of the numbers you type into the form are saved to a server or external database when you calculate. Only if you explicitly click the optional AI insight button are the anonymous numerical loan figures sent once to generate commentary—never your name, property address, bank, or contact details.
