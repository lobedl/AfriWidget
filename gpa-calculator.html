What this GPA and CGPA calculator does

This tool takes the courses you are taking this semester, converts each grade to its grade point on your university's scale, weights it by the credit hours of the course, and returns a semester GPA. If you also provide your previous CGPA and the number of credits you had already completed, it returns your new cumulative CGPA after this semester. It then gives you a target planner that shows the GPA you would need across your remaining credits to reach a CGPA you set for yourself.

Everything is calculated on your device. No course names, no grades, and no CGPA are uploaded anywhere. The only time anything leaves your browser is when you press the AI insight button, and even then only an anonymous summary of the computed figures is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and stops.

The calculator supports nine pre-configured grading systems plus a custom scale. Where the official degree classification bands for a system are verified from the regulator or quality body, the tool shows them. Where the bands vary by institution, the tool says so and does not display a classification. That distinction is deliberate: guessing at classification bands is worse than being honest that they vary.

GPA vs CGPA — what the difference is

GPA stands for grade point average. It is the weighted average of your grade points across the courses in a single semester or term. CGPA stands for cumulative grade point average. It is the weighted average of your grade points across every semester you have completed so far.

A useful way to think about it: your GPA is a snapshot of one term, and your CGPA is the running total. Your university transcript shows both, but it is the CGPA that appears on the final degree certificate and that graduate schools and employers usually ask for. A single very strong semester can lift your CGPA by a meaningful amount, but it cannot rewrite the earlier semesters. That is why the target planner matters: it tells you what the arithmetic actually requires.

The formulas behind the numbers

There are three formulas in this calculator. All three are simple arithmetic, and the tool applies them in the order shown here.

Semester GPA

GPA = Σ (grade point × credit hours) ÷ Σ credit hours

For each course, multiply the grade point by the credit hours. Sum those products. Divide by the total credit hours. A 3-credit course at grade point 4 contributes 12 points. A 4-credit course at grade point 3 contributes 12 points as well. Two 3-credit courses at 4 and 2 contribute 18 points across 6 credits, giving a GPA of 3.0.

Cumulative CGPA

CGPA = (Previous CGPA × Previous Credits + Semester GPA × Semester Credits) ÷ (Previous Credits + Semester Credits)

This is the formula for combining an existing average with a new one. The key thing is that the credits have to be the correct totals: if you had 60 credits before this semester and you add 15 now, the denominator is 75, not 15. The most common mistake is forgetting to include the previous credits in the denominator, which makes the new CGPA look far worse than it is.

Required GPA for a target CGPA

Required GPA = (Target CGPA × Total Credits − Current CGPA × Current Credits) ÷ Future Credits

The target planner turns the CGPA formula around. It asks: if I want my final CGPA to be T after N more credits, what GPA do I need to average from here? If the answer is above the maximum of your scale, the target is mathematically impossible with that many credits, and the tool will say so. If it is zero or below, you have already reached the target. Anything in between is a real requirement you can measure your remaining semesters against.

One example of the target in action. Suppose your current CGPA is 3.10 across 60 credits, you want a 3.50, and you plan to take 60 more credits before graduating. The required GPA is (3.50 × 120 − 3.10 × 60) ÷ 60 = (420 − 186) ÷ 60 = 3.90. On a 4.0 scale that is possible but demanding. If you only plan 30 more credits, the required GPA is (3.50 × 90 − 3.10 × 60) ÷ 30 = (315 − 186) ÷ 30 = 4.30. That is above 4.0 and therefore impossible. The number of future credits changes whether the target is reachable.

How to choose your grading system

The calculator offers three groups of grading systems.

Verified profiles. These are systems whose grading scale and degree classification bands are published and verifiable from the national regulator or quality body. Nigeria's NUC scale, the standard Ghanaian 4.0 scale, and the UK honours classification are in this group. For these profiles the tool shows classification bands, and each band comes from a source on this page.

Common scales. These are grading scales that are widely used but where the degree classification bands vary from institution to institution. The US 4.0 scale, Kenya's 4.0 scale, South Africa's percentage bands, Canada's 4.0 scale, India's 10.0 CGPA, and Australia's 7.0 scale are in this group. The tool uses these to compute the GPA and CGPA accurately, but does not claim a classification band, because the bands depend on the individual university's handbook.

Custom. If your university uses a variant of one of these scales, or a scale that is not listed at all, choose Custom. You enter the maximum of your scale and then enter the numeric grade point per course directly. The GPA and CGPA are computed exactly, but no classification bands are shown, because the tool cannot know them.

If you are unsure which group your university falls into, look at your transcript or your student handbook. The scale maximum is usually stated at the top of the transcript. The letter-to-point mapping is usually in the academic regulations. If neither is available, ask your department office — the answer takes about a minute and it is the difference between a correct result and a misleading one.

Worked example: semester GPA and cumulative CGPA

Take a student at a Nigerian university on the 5.0 scale. She had 60 credits completed before this semester with a CGPA of 3.45. This semester she is taking five courses.

CourseCreditsGradePointsWeighted
Data Structures3A515
Operating Systems3B412
Discrete Maths4A520
Technical Writing2B48
Lab Practical3C39

Total credits this semester: 3 + 3 + 4 + 2 + 3 = 15. Total weighted points: 15 + 12 + 20 + 8 + 9 = 64. Semester GPA = 64 ÷ 15 = 4.27.

New CGPA = (3.45 × 60 + 4.27 × 15) ÷ (60 + 15) = (207 + 64) ÷ 75 = 271 ÷ 75 = 3.61.

That crosses the 3.50 boundary, so her classification on the Nigeria 5.0 scale moves from Second Class Upper into the upper half of the same band. If she had taken 30 credits this semester instead of 15 and scored the same GPA, the new CGPA would be (3.45 × 60 + 4.27 × 30) ÷ 90 = (207 + 128) ÷ 90 = 3.72. More credits at a high GPA move the cumulative average further, which is why late-stage semesters with many credits matter so much.

Planning with the target CGPA calculator

The target planner uses the same numbers but runs the CGPA formula in reverse. It needs four inputs: your current CGPA, your current completed credits, the CGPA you want to reach, and the number of credits you plan to take from here.

Three outcomes are possible.

  • Reachable. The required GPA is between 0 and the maximum of your scale. You know exactly what average you need to hit across your remaining credits.
  • Already achieved. The required GPA is zero or below. Your current CGPA is already at or above the target, and any future pass keeps it there. This is a common and pleasant surprise for students who set a target lower than their actual CGPA.
  • Impossible with the credits you planned. The required GPA is above the maximum of your scale. No amount of future effort will reach the target with that many remaining credits. The tool shows the maximum CGPA that is achievable with the credits you plan, and if you increase the future credits in the input, you can often see a reachable version of the same target.

The third case is the one that saves students the most wasted effort. A student who wants to reach a 4.5 CGPA on a 5.0 scale but has only one semester left, for example, will typically find the target is mathematically out of reach. Better to know that in the first week of the semester than in the last.

Common mistakes when calculating GPA

Forgetting the previous credits in the denominator. When you combine a semester GPA with a previous CGPA, the denominator must be the total credits across both. A student with 60 previous credits and 15 new credits has 75 total credits, not 15. Using 15 as the denominator produces a number that is roughly a tenth of the real CGPA.

Treating 0-credit courses as if they add grade points. Some universities include zero-credit seminars, induction modules, or pass/fail courses. A pass/fail course does not carry a grade point, and including it in the credit-weighted average inflates or deflates the GPA incorrectly. The calculator only counts courses that have both credit hours and a numeric grade, which matches the standard convention.

Assuming a high GPA is the same number on every scale. A 3.5 GPA is strong on a 4.0 scale and middling on a 5.0 scale. When comparing across systems, always convert to the same scale first, or ask the receiving institution how it evaluates your scale. This is why the calculator labels each profile explicitly.

Using the wrong grade points for retakes. Some universities replace the original grade entirely with the retake, and some average both attempts. The calculator uses whatever grade you type in the course row. If your university averages retakes, enter each attempt as its own course row so the credits reflect both attempts.

Ignoring the difference between semester GPA and CGPA when planning. A single strong semester can raise your semester GPA by a full point but barely move your CGPA if you already have 120 credits completed. This is not a fault of the calculator; it is the arithmetic of weighted averages. The target planner makes it visible so you can plan with the correct expectation.

Where this tool stops

This calculator computes your semester GPA and cumulative CGPA from the courses you enter. It does not know the specific classification bands your university uses unless they match one of the verified profiles, and it does not know about institutional policies that affect classification, such as dropping the lowest module, weighting final-year courses differently, or excluding certain modules from the degree classification.

It does not replace your official transcript. The transcript is the document that governs your degree. What the calculator gives you is a transparent, fast way to see where you stand, what a target requires, and whether the plan you have in mind is reachable. If your institution's handbook has a rule that changes the result, follow the handbook, and treat the calculator as a planning aid rather than a decision-maker.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between GPA and CGPA?

GPA is the grade point average for a single semester or term. CGPA is the cumulative grade point average across every semester you have completed. GPA tells you how you did this term. CGPA tells you how you have done across your whole degree so far. Universities award the degree based on CGPA, not on any single semester's GPA.

How do I calculate my semester GPA?

For each course, multiply the grade point by the credit hours. Add those products together, then divide by the total credit hours for the semester. The formula is GPA equals the sum of grade point times credit hours divided by the sum of credit hours. A course worth 3 credits at grade point 4 contributes 12 grade-credit points. A course worth 3 credits at grade point 3 contributes 9. If the semester had both, the GPA is twenty-one divided by six, which is 3.5.

How do I calculate my cumulative CGPA?

Multiply your previous CGPA by the number of credits you had already completed. Multiply this semester's GPA by this semester's credits. Add the two products. Divide by the new total of credits. The formula is new CGPA equals previous CGPA times previous credits plus semester GPA times semester credits, all divided by previous credits plus semester credits.

What is a good GPA?

A good GPA depends on the scale your institution uses and what you are trying to do. On a 4.0 scale, 3.5 or above is generally strong and keeps most competitive graduate programmes and scholarships open. 3.0 to 3.49 is solid and clears many postgraduate thresholds. 2.5 to 2.99 is a working GPA that may require a strong final year to lift. Below 2.5 often limits options for graduate study and certain employers. On Nigeria's 5.0 scale, 3.5 or above is the first threshold that most employers and graduate programmes expect.

How does the target CGPA calculator work?

The target calculator tells you the GPA you would need to earn in your future credits to reach a target CGPA. It uses your current CGPA, your current completed credits, the target CGPA you want, and the number of future credits you plan to take. The formula is target CGPA times total credits minus current CGPA times current credits, all divided by future credits. If the required GPA is above the maximum of your grading scale, the target is mathematically impossible with that many future credits and the tool will say so. If the required GPA is at or below zero, you have already reached the target.

Can I use this calculator for any university?

Yes. The calculator has nine pre-configured grading systems plus a custom option. The pre-configured systems include Nigeria's 5.0 scale, Ghana's 4.0 standard scale, UK honours classification, the US 4.0 scale, Kenya's 4.0 scale, South Africa's percentage bands, Canada's 4.0 scale, India's 10.0 CGPA, and Australia's 7.0 scale. If your university uses a scale that is not listed, or uses a variant of one that is, choose Custom and enter your own scale maximum. Each course row then accepts any numeric grade point between zero and your maximum.

What if my university's grading system isn't listed?

Choose the Custom option. Enter the maximum of your scale (for example 4.0, 5.0, or 7.0), then for each course enter the numeric grade point your university awarded. The calculator does not need to know the letter-to-point mapping for a custom scale, because you enter the points directly. Degree classification bands are not shown for custom scales, because the tool cannot know your institution's official bands.

Does this tool store my grades?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab. The courses you enter, your grades, and your CGPA are never uploaded to any server. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the computed values is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Course names, student names, index numbers, and any personal identifiers are never part of that payload.

Is this calculator official?

No. It is an independent tool built to match the published grading scales and classification bands that the major systems publish. The official transcript your university issues is the only document that governs your degree classification. Use this calculator to plan, to cross-check, and to see the effect of a target on your future semesters. Treat your university's handbook and your official transcript as the definitive source.
