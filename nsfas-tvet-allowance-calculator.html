What this calculator does

This tool takes the three facts that most affect a TVET student's NSFAS package — the programme category, the living arrangement, and the academic cycle length — and returns an estimated annual total, a monthly cash figure, and a category-by-category breakdown. It draws on the published NSFAS bursary rules and the DHET funding framework for public TVET colleges.

The point of the tool is to make the split visible. Tuition is paid straight to the college and never reaches the student. Accommodation is paid to an accredited landlord or a residence. Only the personal care allowance and the travel allowance arrive in the student's own account. Many students are surprised to learn how much of the headline number is never theirs to spend, and the breakdown on this page makes that clear before the semester starts.

Every calculation runs in the browser. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored, and nothing is tracked. If the AI insight button is pressed, only an anonymous summary of the institution category, living arrangement, cycle length, and the computed allowance totals is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

How NSFAS funding works at a TVET college

The National Student Financial Aid Scheme is the government bursary that funds qualifying South African students at public universities, universities of technology, and TVET colleges. For TVET students, the bursary is a full-cost award: it covers tuition, registration, and the allowances described in the next section, and it does not need to be repaid as long as the student meets the academic progression rules and stays registered on an approved programme.

NSFAS funds two main TVET programme families. The National Certificate (Vocational), usually written NC(V), runs over three years at levels 2, 3, and 4, and combines a vocational specialisation with general subjects including a language and mathematics or mathematical literacy. The NATED programmes, also called Report 191, run as N4, N5, and N6, and are delivered either on a semester cycle for Business Studies or on a trimester cycle for Engineering Studies. The programme family affects the cycle length and therefore how the annual amount is split into monthly payments, but the funding categories and the eligibility rules are the same for both.

Funding is administered at college level. The college financial aid office confirms registration to NSFAS, and NSFAS disburses according to the cycle the student is registered for. That means the payment dates and the number of instalments are managed locally, and the college financial aid office is the first point of contact for any payment query.

The allowance categories and how they are paid

The NSFAS TVET bursary is built from five categories. Each one is paid to a different recipient, and knowing who receives what is the key to reading the estimate correctly.

Tuition and registration. Paid by NSFAS directly to the college according to the agreed programme fee. The student never sees this money and does not need to budget for it. If a college asks a funded student to pay tuition upfront, that is a query to raise with the financial aid office.

Personal care allowance. A modest annual amount, disbursed in monthly instalments across the cycle, paid into the student's own account or approved mobile wallet. It is intended for basic personal needs. For TVET students the amount is smaller than the university living allowance because colleges often provide subsidised meals, learning materials, and toolkits on campus.

Accommodation cap. Paid to the accredited landlord or the residence, not to the student. The cap differs by area, with urban and metropolitan placements usually attracting a higher cap than rural or non-metropolitan placements. Accommodation in unaccredited private housing is not covered, which is why accreditation matters before a student signs a lease.

Travel allowance. Paid to the student, and only to students who live at home with family and commute from within the recognised distance band. It is a fixed monthly amount, and it replaces the accommodation cap rather than adding to it.

Learning materials. Often delivered as physical textbooks, toolkits, or equipment through the college rather than as cash. Where a cash allowance is paid, it is credited against the materials budget and is not part of the monthly cash that reaches the student.

Accommodation or travel, never both

The single most common misunderstanding about the TVET bursary is that a student can receive accommodation funding and travel funding in the same cycle. They cannot. The rules treat the two as alternative ways of funding the same living cost, and the student is placed in one category or the other based on where they actually live.

A student who moves into accredited off-campus housing or a college residence has the housing cap paid directly to the landlord or the residence. That student is expected to walk or use campus transport, and does not receive a separate travel allowance. A student who lives at home and commutes receives the travel allowance in cash and does not have a housing cap paid on their behalf.

If your living arrangement changes mid-year, the college financial aid office needs to know. Changing from home to accredited housing, or the reverse, changes the allowance category, and the change has to be registered with NSFAS before the correct amount can be paid. Students who move without updating their record often find themselves on the wrong allowance for several months.

How the monthly cash figure is worked out

The monthly cash figure is the amount that actually reaches the student's account each month. It is calculated by dividing the annual personal care allowance and, where applicable, the annual travel allowance by the number of months in the academic cycle.

Monthly cash to student = (Annual personal care ÷ cycle months) + (Annual travel ÷ cycle months, if living at home)

Accommodation and tuition are not part of this figure, because neither is paid to the student. A student in accredited housing with a full annual package will see a large annual total, but the monthly cash in their own account will be modest, because most of the funded amount flows to the landlord and the college. A student living at home will see a much smaller annual total, but a higher share of it arrives as cash.

The cycle length matters here. A student on a trimester cycle receives the annual personal care amount across three and a half months instead of ten, which means the monthly instalment is proportionally larger but the number of payments is fewer. The annual total does not change; only the shape of the payments does.

A worked example

Take a student registered for an NC(V) Level 3 programme at a public TVET college in Gauteng, living in accredited off-campus housing in an urban area, on the standard ten-month annual cycle.

The personal care allowance for the year is a small amount, paid in ten monthly instalments. The accommodation cap for urban accredited housing is the largest single line item and is paid to the landlord across the cycle. Tuition is paid in full to the college and does not appear as cash. Learning materials are supplied through the college and do not appear as cash either.

The annual package on paper is therefore substantial. The cash that reaches the student, however, is only the personal care amount, spread over ten months. If the same student had been living at home instead, the accommodation cap would have been replaced by the travel allowance, the annual total would have dropped by a large margin, and the monthly cash would have been higher because the travel allowance is paid to the student rather than the landlord.

Running both scenarios side by side is the fastest way to understand the trade-off. Accreditation is what unlocks the accommodation cap, but living at home is what puts the largest share of the funded amount into the student's own hands. Neither is universally better; the right choice depends on where the student actually lives and what the commute costs in time and money.

Common misunderstandings

Assuming the headline figure is cash. The annual NSFAS package includes tuition and accommodation, neither of which is paid to the student. Only personal care and travel are cash, and only one of those two applies.

Signing a lease before accreditation is confirmed. Accommodation is only funded if the landlord or residence is on the accredited list. A student who signs an unaccredited lease and expects NSFAS to cover it will be left with the full rent on their own.

Changing living arrangements without telling the college. Moving between home and accredited housing changes the allowance category. If the financial aid office is not informed, the wrong allowance continues to be paid and the student may end up owing money back.

Assuming SASSA status alone completes the application. SASSA beneficiary status satisfies the financial test, but the student still has to be registered at a public college on an approved programme, submit a valid ID, and complete the NSFAS agreement. Missing any of those steps leaves the funding on hold.

Failing too many subjects in one cycle. NSFAS requires students to pass a minimum proportion of their registered subjects to keep funding in the following cycle. Students who fall below the threshold lose funding until the academic record is repaired, which usually means repeating the failed subjects at their own cost.

Expecting payment on the first day of the cycle. Payments follow registration confirmation, agreement signing, and banking details verification. The first instalment can lag the start of the academic cycle by several weeks, and students who budget assuming the money will be there immediately often run short in the first month.

Eligibility and how to keep the funding

Qualification comes down to four checks. The student must be a South African citizen or permanent resident with a valid ID. The student must be registered at a public TVET college on an approved NC(V) or NATED programme. The combined gross household income must fall below the annual threshold set out in the NSFAS rules, with a higher threshold for students with a recognised disability. And the student must complete the NSFAS application, sign the bursary agreement, and submit banking details through the college financial aid office.

Keeping the funding is a matter of academic progress. NSFAS uses a minimum pass rate across registered subjects to confirm continued funding, and colleges apply the rule at the end of each cycle. Students who pass the required proportion continue automatically. Students who fall below the threshold lose funding for the following cycle until they have cleared the failed subjects. Keeping the funding is therefore mostly about doing the coursework, not about any further paperwork once the initial application is complete.

Where this calculator stops

This tool estimates the allowance package from the published rules and the three inputs you provide. It does not know your college's specific fee schedule, the accredited housing list in your area, the exact personal care figure for the current funding cycle, or the exact travel allowance band that applies to your address. The published rates are reviewed each year, and the amounts on the NSFAS website at the time of your application are the ones that govern.

The tool also does not decide eligibility. Qualifying is a matter for NSFAS and the college financial aid office, and the answer depends on documents the calculator never sees. Use the estimate here to plan, and confirm the exact figures with the financial aid office before you sign a lease or commit to a monthly budget.

What the tool does give you is a fast, clear view of how the different living and enrolment choices change the money that actually reaches you. That view is worth having before the semester starts, because the decision about where to live is often the largest single financial decision a TVET student makes in the year.

Frequently asked questions

What does the NSFAS TVET bursary actually cover?

The NSFAS TVET bursary covers the full cost of tuition and registration at a public TVET college, plus a personal care allowance, an accommodation cap if you live in accredited housing, a travel allowance if you live at home, and learning materials supplied through the college. Tuition is paid directly to the college, accommodation is paid to the accredited landlord or residence, and personal care and travel allowances are paid into your own account or mobile wallet. It is a bursary rather than a loan, so the funded amounts do not have to be repaid as long as you meet the academic progression rules.

How much is the NSFAS personal care allowance for TVET students?

The personal care allowance for TVET college students is a modest annual amount, paid in monthly instalments across the academic cycle. It is smaller than the university living allowance because TVET colleges often provide subsidised meals, learning materials, and toolkits on campus. The exact figure is reviewed each funding cycle and published in the annual NSFAS bursary rules. Use this calculator to see the annual total and the monthly instalment based on the current published rate.

Can a TVET student receive both accommodation and travel allowance?

No. Accommodation and travel are mutually exclusive. A student placed in an accredited off-campus residence or a college-managed residence has the housing cap paid to the landlord or the residence, and does not receive a separate travel allowance. A student who lives at home with family and commutes from within the recognised distance band receives the travel allowance instead. The rule exists so that the same living cost is not funded twice. If your living arrangement changes mid-year you must inform the college financial aid office so that the correct allowance is paid.

When does NSFAS pay TVET allowances?

Allowances are paid monthly across the registered academic cycle. The first payment usually follows confirmation of registration by the college financial aid office and completion of the NSFAS onboarding steps, which include signing the bursary agreement and submitting banking details. Personal care and travel amounts reach the student directly. Accommodation caps are paid to the landlord or the residence on the agreed schedule, and tuition is paid to the college in the funding cycle. Always confirm the exact dates with your college's financial aid office, because payment runs are managed at college level.

Who qualifies for NSFAS funding at a TVET college?

To qualify, you must be a South African citizen or permanent resident with a valid ID, be registered for an approved NC(V) or NATED programme at a public TVET college, and meet the household income threshold. The combined gross household income must not exceed the annual threshold set out in the NSFAS rules, which is reviewed each funding cycle. Students with a recognised disability have a higher household income threshold. SASSA grant beneficiaries are automatically eligible on the financial test, provided they are registered at a public college.

Does SASSA beneficiary status qualify me automatically for NSFAS?

SASSA beneficiary status satisfies the financial eligibility test, but it is not the only condition. You still need to be accepted and registered at a public TVET college on an approved programme, submit a valid South African ID, and complete the NSFAS application and agreement process. Once those steps are met, students from SASSA-supported households are funded on the same terms as any other qualifying student. The exemption does not change the allowance amounts or the payment schedule.

What is the difference between NC(V) and NATED funding at a TVET college?

NC(V) is the National Certificate (Vocational) programme, which runs over three years of full-time study at levels 2, 3, and 4, and combines a vocational specialisation with general subjects. NATED, also called Report 191, runs as N4, N5, and N6 and is delivered on a semester or trimester cycle depending on whether the programme sits in Business Studies or Engineering Studies. Both are funded by NSFAS on the same bursary terms for tuition and personal care, but the academic cycle length affects the number of monthly instalments and the way the annual amount is spread across the year.

Does this calculator store my information?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored on any server. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the institution category, living arrangement, academic cycle length, and the computed allowance totals is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Your ID number, name, college, and banking details are never collected because they are never asked for.
