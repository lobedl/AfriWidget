What this debt payoff calculator models (and how payment rollover works)

Carrying balances across two, three, or five separate credit facilities makes household repayment planning deceptively hard to judge by intuition. One statement quotes a 26.4% revolving credit card Annual Percentage Rate (APR) with a small minimum payment; another shows a fixed-installment personal loan at 15.0% APR; a third lists an 8.0% appliance or vehicle loan with only a modest remaining principal. Paying scattered round numbers whenever spare cash appears feels productive, yet unfocused overpayments dilute principal reduction across every account instead of extinguishing any single obligation cleanly.

This debt payoff calculator takes your active debts—each defined by its current balance, nominal APR, and required monthly minimum payment—adds any optional extra monthly contribution you can sustain, and runs a month-by-month amortization simulation across two structured repayment sequences: Debt Avalanche (highest interest rate first) and Debt Snowball (smallest balance first). For both strategies, it calculates your total months to zero, estimated debt-free calendar date, cumulative interest paid across all accounts, total principal-plus-interest cash outlay, and the exact month each individual debt clears.

Crucially, the engine models full fixed-budget payment rollover—the mechanism that gives structured debt elimination its speed. Suppose your minimum payments initially total $455 per month across three debts and you add $145 per month in extra cash, committing to a fixed monthly debt budget of $600. The moment Debt 1 hits $0, your monthly budget does not drop by Debt 1's old $50 minimum. Instead, that freed-up $50 combines with your $145 extra contribution so $195 of accelerated firepower rolls straight onto Debt 2 on top of Debt 2's own scheduled minimum—and if Debt 1 needs only part of its payment in its final payoff month, the unused remainder rolls forward in that very same billing cycle.

Every calculation executes locally inside your browser tab. No creditor names, balances, interest rates, or repayment schedules are saved to a server or persisted after you close the page. Only if you click the optional AI insight button is an anonymous numeric summary of your aggregate figures transmitted once to generate short commentary.

Debt avalanche vs. debt snowball: core mechanics and behavioral trade-offs

Both debt avalanche and debt snowball obey the same two foundational rules every billing cycle: first, pay at least the contractual minimum on every open account on time so no account incurs late fees, penalty APR hikes, or delinquency marks; second, concentrate 100% of remaining monthly debt-repayment cash onto a single priority target until that account reaches zero. The strategies differ solely in how they rank the priority queue.

The Debt Avalanche Method (Rate-Priority Ordering)

Under the avalanche method, accounts are sorted strictly from highest APR to lowest APR, regardless of balance size. If two accounts share the exact same APR, the smaller balance breaks the tie. Because every currency unit of extra principal applied to a 26.4% APR credit card prevents 2.20 cents of monthly interest—whereas the same unit applied to an 8.0% loan prevents only 0.67 cents—targeting the highest APR first is mathematically optimal. Across any multi-debt portfolio with unequal interest rates and a fixed monthly budget, no unassisted repayment sequence can produce a lower total interest bill or an earlier final payoff date than debt avalanche.

The practical challenge of avalanche is psychological patience. When your highest-APR obligation also happens to be a $9,000 or $14,000 balance, you may make disciplined extra transfers for eighteen or twenty-four consecutive months while still juggling the exact same number of monthly bills. The interest savings accumulate quietly inside your amortization math, invisible unless you track total net debt each month.

The Debt Snowball Method (Balance-Priority Ordering)

Under the snowball method, accounts are sorted from smallest current principal balance to largest balance, ignoring APR unless two balances are tied (in which case the higher APR goes first). Concentrating all extra cash onto a $600 medical bill or a $1,500 retail installment loan clears an entire trade line within a few months. Closing that first account accomplishes two practical things immediately: it removes one bill and due date from your monthly administrative load, and it delivers tangible proof that the budget works.

Empirical consumer-finance studies examining credit card repayment records—including research published in the Journal of Consumer Research and the Journal of Marketing Research—found that borrowers who concentrated repayments on one account at a time and closed small sub-accounts early were measurably more likely to complete multi-year debt elimination than borrowers who spread extra cash thinly across accounts. Snowball trades a quantifiable amount of extra interest for faster early account closures.

Hybrid Sequencing When One Small Nuisance Balance Blocks Focus

In real household budgets, you do not have to treat the choice as ideological. If you hold four debts—say a $380 store card, a $7,800 credit card at 25.9% APR, a $4,200 personal loan at 13.5% APR, and an $11,000 vehicle loan at 7.9% APR—knocking out the $380 nuisance bill in Month 1 to simplify automatic billing and immediately switching to pure APR-ordered avalanche from Month 2 onward captures almost 100% of avalanche's interest savings while securing an immediate administrative win.

Exact monthly amortization, rollover, and payoff equations

Reviewing the exact equations used inside this calculator makes it easy to audit any result by hand or in a spreadsheet. Each simulated month t = 1, 2, 3, ... updates every active account k in four deterministic steps using standard monthly periodic compounding:

Step 1 — Monthly Periodic Rate: rm,k = (APRk ÷ 100) ÷ 12
Step 2 — Monthly Interest Accrual: Interestk,t = Balancek,t−1 × rm,k  |  AccruedBalancek,t = Balancek,t−1 + Interestk,t
Step 3 — Fixed Monthly Rollover Budget: Budget = (Σ InitialMink) + ExtraMonthlyPayment
Step 4a — Scheduled Minimum Allocation (All Active Debts): ReqPayk,t = min(AccruedBalancek,t, Mink, RemainingPool)
Step 4b — Priority Target Curtailment (Avalanche or Snowball Order): ExtraPaytarget,t = min(PostMinBalancetarget,t, RemainingPool)
Step 5 — Ending Balance & Payoff Threshold: Balancek,t = AccruedBalancek,t − ReqPayk,t − ExtraPayk,t  (snapped to 0 when ≤ 0.005)
Single-Debt Closed-Form Payoff Months (when P > B × rm and rm > 0): N = −ln(1 − (B × rm ÷ P)) ÷ ln(1 + rm)
Positive Amortization Solvency Condition: Budget > Σ (Balancek,0 × rm,k)

Two mathematical consequences follow directly from those equations and explain almost every surprise borrowers encounter when testing numbers:

  • Why minimum payments barely move high-APR balances at the start: On a $9,000 credit card at 26.4% APR, the monthly periodic rate is 26.4% ÷ 12 = 2.20%. Month 1 interest alone equals $9,000 × 0.022 = $198.00. If your required minimum payment is $270, only $270 − $198 = $72.00 (26.7% of your payment) actually reduces principal; the other 73.3% is pure interest expense.
  • Why the solvency floor matters: If the combined first-month interest across your portfolio exceeds your total monthly debt budget (for instance, because an informal minimum was typed below monthly interest accrual), unpaid interest capitalizes onto principal and balances grow indefinitely. Whenever a portfolio cannot be extinguished within 600 months (50 years), the calculator flags the shortfall explicitly and reports the exact first-month interest floor your budget must clear.

When avalanche saves hundreds or thousands—and when both tie

How much money you leave on the table by choosing snowball over avalanche depends on the correlation between balance size and APR across your specific accounts, combined with the spread between your highest and lowest interest rates.

  • Aligned portfolios (zero difference): Whenever your smallest debt also carries your highest APR—for example, a $1,200 payday or store card at 29.9% APR, a $4,500 personal loan at 14.5% APR, and a $13,000 car loan at 7.5% APR—both avalanche and snowball rank the three accounts in the exact same order (1 → 2 → 3). Payoff months, monthly cash flows, and total interest are identical down to the last cent.
  • Clustered-APR portfolios (minimal difference): If you hold three credit cards at 21.9%, 23.4%, and 24.9% APR, the 3-percentage-point rate spread generates only a modest cost difference over a two-year payoff plan—often $40 to $120 in total—making early account closure under snowball relatively inexpensive.
  • Inverse / divergent portfolios (major avalanche savings): When your smallest balance carries a low single-digit APR (such as an 8% appliance loan or promotional installment plan) and your largest balance sits on a 24% to 29% APR credit card, snowball spends months or years retiring cheap debt while your largest balance compounds at more than 2% per month.

Worked Example 1: Aligned $19,500 Three-Debt Portfolio at $700/Month

Suppose a household holds three typical consumer obligations and sets aside $700 per month in total debt service:

  • Credit Card A: $2,500 balance at 22.5% APR, $75 required minimum (Month 1 interest = $2,500 × 0.225 ÷ 12 = $46.88).
  • Personal Loan B: $5,000 balance at 14.0% APR, $120 required minimum (Month 1 interest = $5,000 × 0.14 ÷ 12 = $58.33).
  • Auto Loan C: $12,000 balance at 8.5% APR, $250 required minimum (Month 1 interest = $12,000 × 0.085 ÷ 12 = $85.00).

The three scheduled minimums sum to $75 + $120 + $250 = $445/month, so committing $700/month provides an Extra Monthly Payment of $255. Because Credit Card A has both the highest APR (22.5%) and the smallest balance ($2,500), followed by Personal Loan B (14.0%, $5,000) and Auto Loan C (8.5%, $12,000), Debt Avalanche and Debt Snowball execute the exact same three-stage schedule:

  1. Months 1 to 9 (Clearing Credit Card A): Loan B receives $120/month and Loan C receives $250/month ($370 combined), leaving $700 − $370 = $330/month ($75 minimum + $255 extra) for Credit Card A. Credit Card A reaches $0 in Month 9 after accruing $222.16 in interest, and the unused portion of Month 9's $330 allocation immediately rolls onto Personal Loan B in that same month.
  2. Months 10 to 19 (Clearing Personal Loan B): Auto Loan C continues receiving its $250 minimum while Personal Loan B now absorbs $120 + $75 + $255 = $450/month. Personal Loan B hits $0 in Month 19 after accruing $767.55 in cumulative interest.
  3. Months 20 to 32 (Clearing Auto Loan C): Having already been paid down steadily by its $250 minimum during the first 19 months, Auto Loan C now receives the entire $700/month budget and finishes in Month 32 (2 years 8 months) with $1,836.05 in cumulative interest.

Across the complete 32-month plan, total interest paid equals $222.16 + $767.55 + $1,836.05 = $2,825.77 (rounding differences of one cent across sub-accounts come from unrounded internal floating-point summation), and total cash paid equals $22,325.77.

Why extra monthly principal payments bend the interest curve non-linearly

Of all the numbers you enter into a debt calculator, your monthly surplus above the minimums exerts far greater leverage than strategy ordering—especially during the first $50 to $150 per month of extra cash. That non-linearity happens because minimum payments sit dangerously close to a high-rate debt's monthly interest floor.

Consider a single $5,000 credit card balance at 24.0% APR (a periodic rate of exactly 2.00% per month, generating $100.00 of interest in Month 1). If you send $110 per month, the first $100 pays interest and only $10 reduces principal. Raising your payment by just $15—from $110 to $125 per month—increases your total cash outlay by only 13.6%, yet it multiplies your Month 1 principal reduction by 2.5 times (from $10 to $25). Raising the payment to $200 per month (+$90 above $110) multiplies Month 1 principal reduction tenfold (from $10 to $100).

Even if your household budget feels tight, finding $25 to $50 per month—canceling unused subscriptions, selling idle equipment, or dedicating one overtime shift—captures the steepest, highest-return drop on the amortization curve.

Decision framework: avalanche, snowball, 0% balance transfers, and consolidation

Self-directed avalanche and snowball plans operate on your existing credit agreements without new underwriting. Depending on your credit profile, APR spread, and discipline, two structural refinancing tools—a 0% promotional balance transfer card or a fixed-rate debt consolidation loan—can lower the APR input itself before you begin your payoff cascade.

Evaluating a 0% APR Balance Transfer Offer (Break-Even Math)

Balance transfer cards typically offer 0% promotional APR for 12 to 21 months in exchange for an upfront transfer fee of 3% to 5% of the amount moved. Moving a balance is profitable only when the upfront fee is substantially lower than the cumulative interest you would pay under self-directed avalanche, and your monthly budget clears the balance before the standard post-promotional APR (often 22% to 29%) resumes:

Upfront Transfer Cost: Fee = TransferredBalance × TransferFeeRate
Required Monthly Payoff During Promo Window: RequiredMonthly = (TransferredBalance + Fee) ÷ PromoMonths
Net Savings vs Self-Directed Payoff: NetBenefit = ProjectedAvalancheInterest − Fee

For example, transferring a $5,000 card at 24.0% APR onto an 18-month 0% card with a 3% transfer fee adds a one-time $150 fee (new balance $5,150) and requires $5,150 ÷ 18 = $286.11/month to clear inside the promo window. Compared with paying $300/month at 24.0% APR ($1,143.34 interest), the transfer saves roughly $993 net of the fee. By contrast, if you were already going to pay off a $2,000 balance in 3 months at 20% APR (total interest about $67), paying a 5% transfer fee ($100) actually loses money.

True 0% Promo APR vs. Retail "Deferred Interest" Offers: Distinguish bank credit card 0% APR promotions from store financing labeled "No interest if paid in full within 12 months." Under a true 0% APR promotion, if $300 remains unpaid when Month 12 ends, standard interest begins accruing forward only on that $300. Under a retail deferred-interest contract, leaving even $1 unpaid on the expiration date triggers retroactive interest billed all the way back to Day 1 on the original purchase amount. If you hold a deferred-interest balance nearing its deadline, elevate it to your #1 payoff target ahead of standard avalanche ordering.

How to set up a payoff plan that survives real-world billing rules

Turning a calculator output into an actual zero-balance timeline requires aligning your bank transfers with lender billing mechanics. Follow this five-step operational workflow before sending your first extra payment:

  1. Pull exact statement figures—not estimates—for every liability: Open your most recent monthly statement for every credit card, store account, overdraft, personal loan, student loan, and auto facility. Record the current statement balance, exact purchase APR (noting any separate cash-advance or penalty APR segments), required minimum payment, and payment due date.
  2. Ring-fence a starter cash buffer first: Before committing every spare dollar to extra principal curtailment, hold a starter emergency reserve—typically $1,000 or one month of bare-bones essential expenses (rent, utilities, groceries, transport)—in an instant-access account. Without that buffer, a routine tyre replacement or clinic bill forces you straight back onto your highest-rate credit card, resetting your grace period and undoing months of progress.
  3. Automate every scheduled minimum payment: Set automatic bank-draft or standing-order transfers for the required minimum on every debt 3 to 5 business days before its monthly due date. Automation guarantees you never lose 0% promotions or trigger $30–$40 late fees and 29.99% penalty APRs on non-target accounts while focusing on your primary target.
  4. Designate extra installment payments as "Principal Curtailment Only": Revolving credit cards automatically apply payments above your minimum toward your highest-APR balance under consumer credit allocation rules (such as the US CARD Act). Fixed installment loans (auto, personal, student, and mortgage loans) often do not: unless you check the "Apply to Principal Only / Principal Curtailment" box in the servicer's portal, many lenders place your extra cash into "Paid Ahead" status—holding it to pay future scheduled bills rather than cutting today's interest-bearing principal immediately.
  5. Keep your monthly debt budget fixed on payday: As credit card balances shrink, issuers quietly reduce your required minimum payment (often down to 2% of the new balance or a $25–$35 floor). Ignore that shrinking statement minimum and keep transferring your full fixed monthly debt budget until the final account hits zero.

Credit utilization, credit scores, and sequence-of-payoff effects

While neither avalanche nor snowball is designed specifically as a credit-scoring hack, the sequence in which you retire balances affects your credit profile differently depending on whether your accounts are revolving lines (credit cards, retail lines of credit) or installment loans (auto, personal, student loans).

  • Individual and aggregate credit utilization thresholds: Major credit-scoring models (including FICO and VantageScore in North America and bureau risk models used across the UK, South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria, and Ghana) weigh revolving credit utilization heavily—both across all cards combined and on each individual card. Crossing below 30% utilization and then below 10% utilization on a maxed-out credit card produces rapid upward score movement, whereas paying an extra $2,000 onto an auto installment loan barely changes revolving utilization. Because credit cards usually carry higher APRs than installment loans, debt avalanche naturally improves credit utilization faster in most portfolios.
  • Think twice before closing paid-off zero-annual-fee cards: Once a credit card reaches $0, closing the account removes its credit limit from your total available revolving credit denominator, which can spike your aggregate utilization percentage on remaining cards. If the card charges no annual fee and keeping it open in a drawer (or locked in its mobile app) poses zero temptation to overspend, leaving the trade line open preserves both available credit capacity and account age. If an open card carries an annual fee with no offsetting benefit—or tempts impulse spending—close it without hesitation; behavioral solvency matters far more than a minor utilization shift.
  • Installment loan closure dips are normal and temporary: Paying off your sole active auto or personal installment loan can occasionally trim a credit score by 10 to 25 points for one or two billing cycles because scoring algorithms reward active "credit mix." Never keep paying interest on an unnecessary loan merely to preserve a credit-mix score factor.

Seven mistakes that stall or reverse a debt payoff plan

Most repayment plans that break down between Month 3 and Month 9 fail because of operational friction rather than flawed math:

1. Continuing new purchases on a credit card carrying a balance. The moment you carry an unpaid revolving balance past a statement due date, most credit cards revoke your interest-free purchase grace period. Every coffee, grocery run, or fuel purchase charged to that card begins accruing interest from the exact transaction date—not at the end of the month. Route everyday spending through a debit card or a separate paid-in-full card while eliminating revolving debt.

2. Draining checking liquidity to zero for an oversized extra payment. Committing $450/month of extra cash when your realistic sustainable surplus is $250/month guarantees overdrafts or fresh credit card swipes whenever quarterly utility, school-fee, or insurance bills arrive. Budget irregular annual expenses into a monthly sinking fund first so your extra debt payment stays consistent every month.

3. Letting freed-up minimums leak back into lifestyle spending. When Debt 1 clears and frees up $75/month, absorbing that $75 into dining or subscriptions breaks the rollover cascade modeled by the calculator. Redirect the standing transfer to Debt 2 on the very day Debt 1 closes.

4. Overlooking "trailing / residual interest" on the month after card payoff. Because credit cards accrue interest daily between the statement issue date and the day your final payment posts, a card paid down to $0.00 on its due date will often generate one final statement the next month for $4 to $25 of residual daily interest. Ignoring that "closed" card leads to a forgotten late fee and a 30-day delinquency mark—always check every paid-off card one billing cycle later.

5. Extending loan maturity dates during consolidation ("clock resetting"). Consolidating $12,000 of debt with 28 months remaining into a lower-APR 72-month personal loan lowers the mandatory monthly payment, which feels like relief—but paying only the new minimum for six years frequently results in higher total lifetime interest than keeping your shorter payoff horizon.

6. Paying upfront fees to unregulated commercial "debt settlement" companies. Commercial for-profit debt-settlement marketers often instruct borrowers to stop paying creditors altogether while accumulating fees in an escrow account—a tactic that triggers severe credit bureau defaults, penalty interest, collection lawsuits, and potential tax liabilities on forgiven debt. If you need structured relief, work directly with your lender's hardship team or a regulated non-profit credit counseling agency.

7. Failing to re-run numbers after variable-rate or income changes. Variable-rate credit cards, overdrafts, and benchmark-linked loans adjust whenever central bank policy rates move. Re-test your balances and APRs every quarter—or immediately after negotiating a lower rate—to confirm your target order and debt-free date remain accurate.

Where this calculator stops, model assumptions, and financial disclaimer

This tool is engineered to give a transparent, mathematically rigorous monthly planning baseline, yet real consumer credit contracts include billing nuances that no single browser simulation can observe automatically:

  • Monthly periodic compounding vs. Average Daily Balance (ADB): The calculator accrues monthly interest as Balance × (APR ÷ 12). Most credit card issuers calculate daily periodic interest (APR ÷ 365 or APR ÷ 360) against your Average Daily Balance across 28-to-31-day billing cycles. Paying earlier inside your billing cycle reduces your Average Daily Balance slightly below the monthly model, whereas daily compounding on unpaid revolving interest raises effective annual cost slightly above nominal APR. Across typical 12-to-48-month plans, this variance is usually within 0.5% to 2.0% of total interest.
  • Fixed minimum inputs vs. declining card minimum formulas: If you test a scenario with Extra Monthly Payment = $0, remember that credit card issuers recalculate minimum payments downward as your balance falls (typically max(1% of principal + monthly interest, $25 to $35)), which stretches minimum-only card repayment across 10 to 25+ years if you reduce your payment each month. Because this calculator models a fixed total monthly budget equal to your initial minimums plus extra cash, its $0-extra simulation assumes you keep paying at least today's initial dollar total rather than riding declining minimums downward.
  • Single APR per row vs. multi-bucket card statements: A single credit card may simultaneously hold a 0% promotional balance transfer, a 21.9% purchase balance, and a 28.9% cash-advance balance. Enter separate rows for distinct APR buckets on the same card if you want to visualize their exact rate impact, while noting that issuers apply your minimum payment according to statutory allocation rules.
  • Taxes, fees, and prepayment terms: The simulation does not deduct mortgage or student-loan interest tax credits, nor does it add annual card fees, origination fees, foreign-exchange variations, or early-settlement charges on fixed-rate contracts that carry prepayment penalties.

If your required minimum payments consume an unsustainable share of household income or you are facing missed payments or collection action, consult a licensed financial adviser or an accredited non-profit credit counselor in your jurisdiction (such as NFCC-member agencies in the US, StepChange or Citizens Advice in the UK, NCR-registered debt counsellors in South Africa, or licensed financial advisers in your country).

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the debt avalanche and debt snowball methods?

Both strategies require paying the scheduled minimum on every debt each month while directing all extra cash to a single target account. Debt avalanche ranks targets from highest Annual Percentage Rate (APR) to lowest, minimizing total interest paid. Debt snowball ranks targets from smallest balance to largest, eliminating individual accounts earlier to build behavioral momentum.

Which debt payoff method saves the most money?

Mathematically, the debt avalanche method always pays equal or lower total interest than the debt snowball method at the same monthly budget because each extra currency unit retires the highest-cost debt first. The savings gap is largest when your highest-APR balance is also your largest debt, and shrinks to zero when your highest-APR debt also happens to have the smallest balance.

How does payment rollover work when one of my debts reaches zero?

In a true avalanche or snowball plan, your total monthly debt budget stays constant until every debt is gone. When your first target debt is paid off, you do not reduce your monthly spending; instead, that cleared account's former minimum payment plus your extra payment rolls into the next target debt, creating a compounding payment cascade as each account closes.

Should I pay off high-interest debt or build an emergency fund first?

Most financial planners recommend building a starter cash buffer first—typically $1,000 to one month of essential living costs—while paying scheduled minimums on all debts. Once that buffer is in place to prevent an unexpected car repair or medical bill from forcing new credit card borrowing, direct spare cash aggressively toward high-APR debt before expanding your emergency reserve to three to six months of expenses.

How much does adding an extra $50 to $200 per month actually save?

Extra monthly payments reduce interest non-linearly because 100% of every dollar paid above the scheduled minimum cuts principal immediately, lowering interest charges in every future billing cycle. For example, on a $5,000 credit card at 24% APR, raising a $110 monthly payment by $90 to $200 per month cuts the payoff timeline from 122 months to 36 months and reduces total interest from $8,319.96 to $2,000.56.

Why did my credit card still charge a small amount of interest after I paid the statement balance to zero?

Card issuers calculate revolving interest using Average Daily Balance, so interest continues accruing daily between the date your billing statement is generated and the day your payoff transfer settles. This leftover charge—called trailing or residual interest—appears on the next statement. Check the account one cycle after payoff and clear that final residual balance promptly.

How should I enter credit card minimum payments versus fixed installment loans?

For fixed-term auto, personal, or student loans, enter the exact contractual monthly installment shown on your loan agreement. For credit cards—whose issuers lower the required minimum as your balance drops—enter your current required minimum payment from your latest statement. Because this calculator keeps your total monthly debt budget fixed, committing to at least today's minimum avoids stretching out your payoff schedule.

When is a 0% APR balance transfer better than using avalanche or snowball alone?

A 0% balance transfer saves money when the upfront transfer fee (typically 3% to 5% of the transferred balance) is smaller than the interest you would pay under your current APR, and you can pay off the transferred balance before the promotional window expires. Avoid retail deferred-interest offers unless you are certain you will clear 100% of the balance before the expiration date, as unpaid balances can trigger retroactive interest from day one.

What happens if my debt interest rates are variable?

Enter the current APR listed on your most recent billing statement and re-run the calculator whenever benchmark central-bank or prime rates shift. Variable rates move your monthly interest charge immediately, and if a rate jump pushes an account above another debt's APR, your optimal avalanche target order may also change.

Does paying off debt early hurt or improve my credit score?

Paying down revolving credit card balances almost always improves your credit score quickly by lowering both individual-card and aggregate credit utilization below the 30% and 10% scoring benchmarks. Paying off an installment loan early can occasionally cause a small, temporary dip if it was your only active installment trade line, so keep paid-off zero-annual-fee credit cards open when doing so will not tempt overspending.

Why must I specify Principal Curtailment Only when sending extra money to an installment loan?

On auto, personal, and student loans, servicers sometimes treat undesignated extra payments as an early advance toward next month's regular bill (Paid Ahead status) rather than immediate principal reduction. Selecting the Principal Only or Principal Curtailment option in your lender's payment portal ensures the entire extra payment reduces your interest-bearing balance immediately.

Does this debt payoff calculator store or transmit my financial data?

No. All multi-debt simulations run locally inside your browser tab, and nothing you enter is saved to a database. Only if you click the optional AI insight button is an anonymous numeric summary—debt count, rounded total balance, weighted APR, monthly budget, strategy name, and payoff months—sent once to generate the short commentary, with zero creditor names or account numbers.

Are the results from this calculator professional financial advice?

No. Results are mathematical estimates for educational and personal budgeting purposes only. Actual payoff dates and total interest vary with daily versus monthly compounding conventions, statement cutoff dates, fees, promotional rate expirations, and variable-rate changes. Verify figures against official lender disclosures and consult a qualified financial adviser or accredited non-profit credit counselor before making binding credit decisions.
