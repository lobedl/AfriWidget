What this calculator actually models

This tool takes a list of debts, each with a balance, an annual interest rate, and a required minimum payment, and simulates month by month how those balances fall to zero under two repayment strategies. It also lets you add an extra monthly payment above the minimums and see how much that extra amount changes the timeline. The output is a payoff month count, a total interest figure, a total cash figure, and a side-by-side comparison of the two strategies on your own numbers.

The simulation is straightforward and honest about its assumptions. It assumes monthly compounding, which is close enough to how most credit cards and personal loans behave in practice. It assumes the minimum payments and the interest rates stay the same throughout, which is the standard simplification for a planning tool. It assumes you make a payment every month without missing one, and it assumes you do not add new debt while the plan is running. Each of those assumptions is reasonable and each of them can be broken by real life. The article below explains which ones matter most and where the model tends to drift.

Everything runs inside the browser tab. No account balances, creditor names, or interest rates are sent anywhere. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the figures on the page is used, and even that is discarded the moment the response is returned.

Avalanche and snowball, explained plainly

Both methods work the same way at the base level: you pay the minimum on every debt every month, and you direct all spare cash toward one target balance until it hits zero. The difference is entirely in the order you choose the target, and that order has real consequences for both the cost of the plan and the way the plan feels to live with.

The avalanche method ranks your debts by interest rate, highest first, and directs all extra money to the highest-rate balance. When that one is cleared, the next highest rate becomes the target, and the payment that was going to the first debt is redirected to the second. Because you are attacking the most expensive money first, the total interest you pay over the life of the plan is minimised. This is the strategy that most financial advisers recommend when the goal is purely to save money, and on most portfolios it does.

The snowball method ranks your debts by balance, smallest first, and directs all extra money to the smallest balance. When that one is cleared, the next smallest becomes the target. Because the smallest debt usually takes the least time to clear, you see your first fully paid-off account sooner, which is a real psychological reward. The snowball method often costs a bit more in total interest than the avalanche method, but on some portfolios the difference is small enough that the motivation benefit is worth more than the money.

Neither method is wrong. They optimise for different things, and the right choice for you depends on which one you will actually finish.

The arithmetic behind both methods

Every monthly payment is split between two things: the interest that has accrued on the balance since the last payment, and the reduction of the principal. Interest is calculated on the outstanding balance, so the larger the balance, the larger the interest charge, and the smaller the portion of your payment that actually reduces the debt. This is why a minimum payment that looks generous on a statement can take years to clear a balance, and why the effect of an extra payment is disproportionate.

The formula for one month of a single debt is short. Interest for the month equals the balance multiplied by the annual rate divided by twelve. The minimum payment is subtracted from that interest plus any principal above the interest. If the minimum payment is smaller than the monthly interest, the balance actually grows, which is the state most people are in when they are only paying the minimum on a high-rate credit card.

Because interest is charged on the balance, whatever method reduces the highest-rate balances fastest pays the least total interest. This is the mathematical case for avalanche. But because motivation is not a line in a spreadsheet, the method that gets you to a fully paid-off account soonest is often the one that keeps the plan alive. That is the case for snowball. The calculator shows both so you can weigh them against each other with your own balances in front of you.

Which strategy usually saves more

On most portfolios, avalanche saves more money. The size of the gap depends on three things: how far apart your interest rates sit, how the balances are distributed, and how large your extra monthly payment is. When your highest-rate debt is also your smallest balance, the two strategies behave almost identically and the gap is close to zero. When the highest-rate debt is also your largest balance, the gap widens substantially, because avalanche pays down the most expensive money first while snowball spends its early energy on a cheaper balance that happens to be smaller.

There is a separate question worth considering, which is how long the plan will actually run if you commit to it. A plan that saves an extra two hundred in interest but gets abandoned after three months is worth less than a plan that costs a bit more and gets finished. Studies of consumer behaviour have found that early wins matter measurably for follow-through, and the snowball method deliberately manufactures those early wins. The correct answer for most people is not which method is mathematically optimal, but which method they will keep doing in month seven when the novelty has worn off.

A practical rule. If your highest-rate debt is also your largest balance, and the rate gap between your debts is more than five percentage points, avalanche is worth the extra discipline. If your balances are clustered and your rates sit close together, snowball usually costs the same and feels better to live with.

A worked example

Take a portfolio with three debts and a total monthly budget of one thousand two hundred in whatever currency you use.

DebtBalanceAPRMinimum
Credit card A2 50022.5%75
Personal loan B5 00014.0%120
Auto loan C12 0008.5%250

The minimum payments total four hundred and forty five. An extra payment of two hundred and fifty five brings the monthly budget to seven hundred.

Under the avalanche method, Credit Card A is the target because it carries the highest rate. It clears in roughly four months. Then the whole seven hundred goes to Personal Loan B, which clears in about eight more months. Then the whole seven hundred goes to Auto Loan C, which clears in a further fifteen months or so. The total payoff time lands somewhere near twenty seven months and the total interest paid is on the order of two thousand four hundred.

Under the snowball method, Credit Card A is still the first target, because it also happens to have the smallest balance. That is a coincidence of this particular portfolio, and it is why the two methods produce similar totals here. If the smallest balance had been the auto loan instead, the numbers would diverge: snowball would spend the first two years paying down a low-rate balance while the high-rate card kept accruing interest at twenty two and a half per cent, and the total interest paid would rise by several hundred.

The lesson from the example is that the two methods can produce wildly different answers on portfolios where the highest-rate debt is not the smallest balance, and nearly identical answers on portfolios where they happen to line up.

The extra payment is the real lever

Of all the inputs on this page, the extra monthly payment moves the result the most. Ordering strategy shifts the cost by a few per cent in either direction. Adding two hundred a month to a portfolio with fifteen thousand in total debt and an average rate around fifteen per cent can cut years off the timeline and save well over a thousand in interest, because every extra dollar goes straight to principal, and a lower principal produces smaller interest charges in every subsequent month.

The effect is easiest to see on a single high-rate balance. On a two thousand balance at twenty per cent, the minimum payment of fifty a month will take over six years to clear and cost more than a thousand in interest. Pay two hundred a month instead and the same balance clears in about twelve months, and the interest paid drops to somewhere around two hundred and twenty. The extra payment did not change the interest rate or the balance; it changed how much of each payment was principal, and that is what drives the whole thing.

The practical consequence is that the biggest single improvement you can make to any payoff plan is not choosing the perfect ordering strategy. It is finding one more source of cash that can go toward the debt each month. Even fifty extra a month, directed at the highest-rate balance, changes the arithmetic more than most people expect.

Six mistakes that slow a payoff plan down

Most plans that fail do not fail for mathematical reasons. They fail for one of a small handful of behavioural ones.

Running the plan while still adding to the debt. Using a credit card for daily spending while you are trying to pay it down is the single most common mistake. Every new charge resets the clock on the balance and delays the moment it clears. If a card is part of the payoff plan, it should be frozen during the plan or used only for purchases that will be paid off in full that same month.

Leaving no emergency buffer. A plan that sends every spare unit to the debt leaves nothing for the unexpected car repair or medical bill that arrives in month four. Without a small cash buffer, the plan gets interrupted by a new charge on the very card that was almost paid off. A starter fund equal to one month of essential expenses is usually enough to protect the plan.

Missing minimum payments on the non-target debts. The plan only works if the minimums are paid on schedule. Missing one triggers a late fee, damages your credit score, and in some cases raises the rate on the account. Automating the minimums through your bank is worth the five minutes it takes to set up.

Ignoring a rate change. Variable rates move with the benchmark, and a one per cent rise on a large balance can add months to the plan. Re-run the calculator whenever the rate changes. The number you entered last quarter may not be the number you are paying now.

Choosing a strategy you will not stick with. The avalanche method saves money on paper. If it means five months before you see a single account at zero, and you are the kind of person who needs that visual to stay motivated, then snowball is the better strategy for you. The mathematically optimal plan you abandon is worth less than the slightly more expensive plan you finish.

Not telling anyone about the plan. Money plans that live only in one person's head tend to slip. Sharing the payoff goal with a partner or a trusted friend, even informally, creates a small accountability that raises follow-through measurably. It is the cheapest intervention available.

Where this calculator stops

The simulation assumes monthly compounding, a fixed interest rate, and consistent payments for the whole duration of the plan. Real debts behave slightly differently. Some cards compound daily rather than monthly, which makes the true interest slightly higher. Some loans charge interest on the average daily balance rather than on the closing balance. Late fees, over-limit fees, and promotional rates that expire partway through the plan all move the actual numbers in ways this calculator cannot see.

The tool also does not know about any tax treatment of the interest, any negotiated rate changes you might achieve, or any new debt you might take on during the plan. It does not model the effect of a windfall payment in month six, or the effect of skipping a month because your hours were cut. It is a planning snapshot, not a forecast.

Finally, the tool is not financial advice. For anything with real stakes, speak with a licensed financial counsellor or adviser in your jurisdiction. The calculator is a useful way to see the shape of your plan, compare two reasonable strategies, and understand which lever matters most. It is not a substitute for someone who knows your full financial situation and can advise on the specific products you hold.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between the debt avalanche and debt snowball methods?

The debt avalanche method orders your debts from highest interest rate to lowest, and directs all extra money toward the highest-rate balance first. It minimises the total interest you pay. The debt snowball method orders debts from smallest balance to largest, and directs all extra money toward the smallest balance first. It produces the first fully paid-off debt the soonest, which many people find more motivating. The two methods can produce the same result on some debt mixes, and different results on others.

Which method saves the most money?

On most debt portfolios, the avalanche method saves more money, because paying down the highest-rate balance first reduces the total interest that accrues. The gap depends entirely on how far apart your interest rates sit and how the balances compare to each other. When your highest-rate debt is also your smallest balance, the two methods behave almost identically. When the highest-rate debt is also your largest, the gap between the two strategies widens.

Why does the snowball method exist if it costs more?

Because personal finance is not purely mathematical. The snowball method produces its first fully paid-off account sooner, and that visible closure is a psychological reward that keeps people going when a payoff plan would otherwise stall. Studies of consumer behaviour have found that people who eliminate a small balance early are measurably more likely to stick with the plan and clear the rest. A method that saves a few hundred dollars but gets abandoned after month four is worth less than a method that saves a bit less and gets finished.

Should I pay off debt or build savings first?

The usual answer is to build a small starter emergency fund first, then attack the debt, and only then expand long-term savings. The starter fund is typically one month of essential expenses, or roughly one thousand in whatever currency you use. Its purpose is not to be a full emergency fund, but to keep an unexpected car repair or medical bill from sending you back to the credit card you just paid off. Once the debt is cleared, the emergency fund can grow to three to six months of expenses.

How much does an extra monthly payment really help?

The effect is larger than most people expect. Every extra unit you pay above the minimum goes directly to principal, and a lower principal balance produces smaller interest charges in every subsequent month. That reduction compounds. On a portfolio with an average interest rate of eighteen per cent, a two hundred monthly extra payment on top of minimums can cut years off the payoff timeline and save hundreds or thousands in interest, depending on the starting balances.

What if my interest rates are variable?

Enter the current rate and re-run the calculator whenever the rate changes. Variable-rate debts such as some credit cards and most student loans move with a benchmark rate, and the payoff timeline moves with them. A one per cent rise across a large balance can add months to the plan. Treating the calculator as a snapshot rather than a fixed forecast is the right way to use it.

Does making only the minimum payment on some debts actually work?

Yes, as long as you keep paying at least the minimum on every account while you focus your extra money on one target. Missing a minimum payment triggers a late fee, damages your credit score, and in some cases raises the interest rate on the account. The strategy only works if the minimums are paid on schedule. Automating the minimum payments through your bank removes the risk of forgetting one during a busy month.

Can I negotiate a lower interest rate?

Often, yes. Credit card issuers will sometimes lower a rate for a customer with a long history of on-time payments, especially if the customer mentions a competing offer. A five per cent reduction on a large balance can shorten the payoff timeline by several months. It is worth a phone call before you commit to a payoff plan, because the rate you enter into the calculator is the biggest single input on the timeline.

Does the tool store my debt balances?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the number of debts, the rounded total balance, the rounded average interest rate, the monthly budget, the chosen strategy, and the computed payoff months is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. No creditor names, no account numbers, and no personal identifier.

Is this calculator financial advice?

No. It is an educational estimation tool. Real repayment schedules depend on your lender's billing conventions, on daily rather than monthly interest calculations in some cases, on late fees, on promotional rates that may expire mid-plan, and on whether you take on any new debt during the payoff window. If the stakes are significant, speak with a licensed financial counsellor or adviser in your jurisdiction before committing to a plan.
