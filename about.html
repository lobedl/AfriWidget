About AfriWidget

Built from Curiosity. Shaped by Real Problems. Made to Be Useful.

AfriWidget is an independent project from Ghana, created from a simple belief: technology should make difficult tasks easier to understand and everyday problems easier to solve. This is the story of how it started, what it offers, and the thinking behind the tools.

Last Updated: 21 October 2026

AfriWidget Is More Than a Collection of Tools

AfriWidget is an independent, browser-based tools platform built by me, Obed Avorlenu, a self-taught web developer based in Accra, Ghana. It brings practical utilities together in one place: tools that help people calculate, compare, understand, plan, and make progress on a task without having to install an app or work through an unnecessarily complicated process.

The platform has grown to include more than 100 tools and useful pages across education, finance, health, productivity, business, and everyday problem-solving. Its direction is becoming more focused over time, especially on education, because I see how much value a well-made academic tool can provide when a student needs a clear answer or a better way to plan.

AfriWidget is built for people, not for one continent alone. It started in Ghana, and many tools respond to needs in Ghana and other African education systems, but the wider idea is universal: make useful digital help easier to reach, wherever a person lives or whatever device they use.

The idea behind AfriWidget: Find a real problem, understand what makes it difficult, and build something that helps people move forward.

How the Idea Took Shape

I did not begin with a company, a team, or a complete roadmap. I began with curiosity. I was interested in how online products could bring people together, and seeing the early impact of Airtalk made that possibility feel almost magical. It pushed me to learn how websites and web applications were made.

I started teaching myself HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and Python through online learning resources, coding apps, experimentation, and plenty of mistakes. Learning to code was not a straight line. I would get an idea, try to build it, run into problems I did not yet know how to solve, and keep working until I understood a little more. Every attempt taught me something—not only about code, but about how much thought goes into making a website work for the people using it.

One of my early projects was Random Connect, a single-page application inspired by the idea of helping strangers connect. I then tried building voice-chat websites, including Voice Connect Talk and Global Voice Chat. Some ideas did not work out as I hoped. I had to learn about website setup, search visibility, the pages a website needs, and the difference between getting a project online and making it useful and sustainable.

I also explored publishing. Inspired by the way news creators could make current events easier to follow, I tried building News Today with Next.js. The complexity eventually became a lesson in choosing the right approach for my skills and goals. I later built VectricHub, then reconsidered that direction as concerns around AI-generated news aggregation became clearer. VectricEarn followed as I explored online earning and shared what I was learning with young people interested in making money online.

Those projects were not wasted detours. They helped me learn how to build, launch, improve, and rethink an idea. They also taught me that having a website is not the same as solving a problem. A project becomes meaningful when a real person can use it to do something they need to do.

Why I Created AfriWidget

At the centre of AfriWidget is something personal and straightforward: I love solving problems. I enjoy noticing when a task is confusing, repetitive, or harder than it needs to be, then thinking about how a small, well-designed tool could make it simpler.

My earlier projects helped me understand the work involved in building products, but I wanted to put that experience into something practical—something people could visit when they needed an answer, rather than another website they had no reason to return to. AfriWidget grew from that intention. Instead of making one product for one narrow purpose, I began building focused tools for specific tasks and bringing them together in one accessible place.

I also know that access matters. Not everyone has a powerful computer, a paid subscription, time to learn a complicated system, or the money to pay for every small digital service they need. A useful tool should not create more barriers than the problem it is supposed to solve. That is why AfriWidget is designed to work in a browser, remain straightforward to use, and keep its tools free to access.

This is not a claim that every problem can be fixed with a calculator or a webpage. It is a commitment to look for practical improvements where technology can genuinely help—and to keep refining the work as I learn from people who use it.

Why Education Is Becoming a Major Focus

As AfriWidget grew, education tools began to show me a particularly clear way to be useful. Students often need to understand grading systems, calculate an aggregate or GPA, estimate admission points, check subject combinations, or work out how continuous assessment contributes to a final mark. The rules can differ between countries, examination systems, programmes, and institutions. Even when the calculation itself is simple, finding the right method and applying it correctly can be stressful.

AfriWidget’s education tools aim to make these tasks easier to work through. Examples include WASSCE and BECE aggregate calculators, GPA and CGPA calculators, JAMB UTME aggregate tools, Kenya’s KCSE and KUCCPS-related tools, South African APS and ICASS calculators, and Ghana Nursing Training admission calculators. The purpose is not simply to display a number; it is to help students understand the calculation they are making and use the result as one piece of information when planning what to do next.

I want this part of AfriWidget to go beyond one-off calculations. Features such as performance history, progress comparisons, and weakness-checking experiences are intended to help students return to their learning, recognise patterns, and identify areas that may need more attention. The goal is to make a tool useful after the first result, not just for the few seconds it takes to calculate a score.

Education tools must also be honest about what they can and cannot do. A calculator can estimate a result using the rules provided, but it cannot guarantee admission, replace a school’s official records, or override an examination body’s requirements. I encourage users to confirm important decisions with the relevant school, institution, or official source. Accuracy, clarity, and appropriate limitations matter as much as a clean interface.

What You Can Find on AfriWidget

AfriWidget is organised around practical needs rather than technology for its own sake. The collection continues to change as tools are improved and new needs become clear.

Education and academic planning

Calculators and study-support tools help students work through grades, aggregates, GPA or CGPA, admission points, subject combinations, and assessment marks. Many are designed around specific education systems so that the method is more relevant than a generic calculator.

Finance and everyday calculations

Financial utilities help people explore figures such as savings goals, repayment plans, budgets, and freelance rates. They are intended to make numbers easier to examine and compare, not to replace professional financial advice or guarantee an outcome.

Health and wellness

Health-related tools provide accessible calculations and simple ways to review selected wellness information. They are practical aids for general understanding, not diagnoses or a substitute for advice from a qualified health professional.

Productivity, business, and work

Other tools support everyday digital tasks, planning, content and document preparation, business calculations, and freelance work. The common thread is usefulness: a tool should help someone complete a task with less friction, not add complexity for the sake of looking advanced.

Features Built Around the Way People Actually Use Tools

A result should not have to be the end of the experience. I have been shaping AfriWidget around the small things that make a practical tool worth returning to: being able to find it again, keep useful work, share an answer, and make sense of progress over time. These features are not identical on every page; availability depends on the tool and the stage of its development.

Save favourite tools and return to them quickly

When someone finds a calculator or utility they use often, they should not have to search the whole website every time. The favourites feature lets users keep useful tools close at hand, making AfriWidget easier to use as a regular resource rather than a collection of pages they have to rediscover.

Keep your work when you come back

For supported tools, AfriWidget can remember a user's recent work locally so they can continue where they left off instead of starting again from zero. This is especially useful when a student is reviewing results or when someone needs to revisit figures they have already entered. Local saving is not the same as a cloud account, and users should check the behaviour of the particular tool they are using.

Share a result, not just a link to a homepage

AfriWidget includes result-sharing and copy-link experiences in supported tools. The idea is simple: if a result could help a classmate, friend, parent, or colleague, it should be easier to pass it along. Instead of making someone repeat the entire process, sharing can help them understand what is being discussed and continue the conversation. I also want to understand which shared results genuinely help people discover and use the tools.

Compare results with a friend

For academic tools where comparison makes sense, I am building the experience around the idea “Compare Results with a Friend & Get Ahead”. The point is not to turn learning into a popularity contest or make students feel bad about a score. A useful comparison should help students see differences, discuss how they approached a task, and identify what they could learn from one another. It should support progress, not replace personal goals or a teacher's guidance.

Look beyond one score with academic progress history

A single grade tells only part of a student's story. Performance history and progress-tracking features are intended to make it easier to revisit earlier results and notice changes over time. Where a tool supports it, a visual trend can make improvement, consistency, or a possible drop in performance easier to recognise than a list of numbers alone. The aim is to help students ask a better question than “What did I get?”—“What is changing, and what should I work on next?”

Get context through AI-powered insights where available

Some AfriWidget tools offer AI-powered insights to help users interpret a result or consider practical next steps. AI is an added layer, not a replacement for the underlying calculation: the result should still come from the relevant rules and inputs, while any generated explanation should be treated thoughtfully and checked when the decision matters. AI-powered features are only available where they have been enabled.

Move from calculating marks to understanding weaknesses

My ambition for education tools is to help students move beyond entering marks and receiving an aggregate. A more useful learning experience can offer level-appropriate practice questions, explain why an answer is right or wrong, and help identify topics that deserve another look. I am developing this direction for subject-focused learning, including the idea of mixed daily questions at easy, medium, or difficult levels. Where such diagnostic features are not yet available in a particular tool, I do not want to pretend they are already finished; this is an area I want to build carefully and make genuinely helpful.

Purpose-built tools for different education systems

AfriWidget's education collection is not meant to be one generic grade calculator with different labels. It includes tools for different grading and admissions systems, including WASSCE and BECE aggregates, GPA and CGPA, JAMB UTME, KCSE and KUCCPS-related planning, South African APS and ICASS, and Ghana Nursing Training admission calculations. Each system has its own rules and terminology, so the goal is to make the process relevant to the student using it while encouraging them to confirm high-stakes decisions with the appropriate institution.

Simple access without another account to manage

AfriWidget is designed so people can open a tool in a browser and get started without a mandatory account or paid subscription for ordinary use. A tool should help solve the task in front of someone—not force them through an unnecessary registration process first. Supported pages are designed to work across phones and computers, with direct forms, clear results, and minimal friction.

What Makes AfriWidget Different

I would not claim that AfriWidget invented calculators, progress tracking, or sharing. Those ideas already exist elsewhere. What I want to make distinctive is how they come together around specific everyday problems, especially the needs of students navigating different education systems.

Instead of stopping at a number, a well-designed AfriWidget experience should help a person move through a useful sequence: enter the right information, understand the result, keep or share it if needed, revisit progress where supported, and decide on a sensible next step. For a student, that could mean calculating an ICASS mark today and eventually using subject practice and performance history to understand where to improve. For another visitor, it may simply mean getting a clear financial estimate without having to install an app.

AfriWidget is independently built and still evolving. Some tools are more advanced than others, and not every feature is available everywhere. I would rather explain that honestly than promise a perfect platform. The work is to make each tool clearer, more accurate, and more useful with every improvement—and to listen when users show me what is missing.

How I Want AfriWidget to Feel Different

I do not want AfriWidget to feel like a directory full of pages that all look and behave the same. A tool should earn its place by doing a clear job well. That means paying attention to the details people notice when they actually use it: whether the inputs make sense, whether the result is explained, whether the page works on a phone, and whether the next step is obvious.

Useful results, not numbers without context

Where appropriate, I want a tool to help users understand what a result means and what they might consider next. In education, that can mean explaining a grading calculation or helping a student identify a subject to review. In planning tools, it can mean making assumptions and estimates clear instead of presenting them as certainty.

Built for phones as well as computers

People should not need to install an application just to use a simple web tool. AfriWidget is designed for modern browsers and responsive screens, including the phones many people rely on every day. I try to keep interactions direct and interfaces understandable rather than making users work to find the main function.

Free to access

The tools are available without a paid subscription or a required account for ordinary use. Keeping a website running and improving it takes time and resources, so advertising may help support that work. The aim is still for the tool itself to remain accessible.

Built and maintained by a real person

AfriWidget is not managed by a large product department. I build and maintain it independently. That means I work on the tools, check problems, update pages, learn new things, and make decisions about what deserves attention next. It also means the project is still evolving; I would rather be honest about that than pretend every feature is perfect.

Privacy, Access, and Trust

Ease of use should not mean ignoring privacy. Many AfriWidget calculators can perform their calculations directly in your browser, so the figures entered into those calculators do not need to be sent to a server for the calculation itself. Some features—particularly those that rely on external services—may handle information differently. Please review the relevant privacy information for the tool you are using, and do not enter sensitive details unless the page clearly explains how they are handled.

I also want users to understand the limits of a tool before relying on it. Rules change, inputs can be entered incorrectly, and an estimate is not an official decision. Where the stakes are important, verify the result with the appropriate authority. Trust is built by being clear about these things, not by promising that a tool can do everything.

What Comes Next

AfriWidget is a work in progress in the best sense of the phrase: there are more problems to understand, more tools to improve, and better ways to explain results. My current priority is to keep strengthening the education experience and make those tools genuinely helpful beyond the first calculation—while continuing to maintain the wider collection where it serves people well.

I do not expect every idea to be right the first time. I have learned from projects that changed direction, features that needed rethinking, and problems that took longer to solve than I expected. I plan to keep listening, testing, correcting mistakes, and building carefully. The aim is not to publish the largest number of tools possible. It is to make the tools that are there worth using.
