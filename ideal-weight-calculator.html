What "ideal weight" actually means

The phrase "ideal weight" is a historical accident. It comes from actuarial tables and clinical formulas that were built to answer a much narrower question than the one most people are asking when they search for it. The Devine formula, for instance, was created in 1974 to help pharmacists work out drug doses for adults of different heights. The Hamwi formula predates it by a decade and was used in similar clinical settings. Neither was designed to tell anyone how much they should weigh in a general sense, and neither was built to be a personal health target.

What these formulas do is produce a single number from your height. They do not look at your muscle mass, your body frame, your age, your ancestry, your training load, or the state of your health. They also do not distinguish between weight that comes from muscle and weight that comes from fat, which is a distinction that matters a great deal for the question of whether any given weight is a healthy one. The formulas are reference points from a particular era of medicine, and they are still useful in that context, but they are not a plan.

This page shows four of them together on purpose. Devine, Robinson, Miller, and Hamwi will each give you a slightly different number, usually within three to seven kilograms of one another. Showing them side by side is the honest way to present them, because a single formula presented alone implies a precision that none of them actually has.

The four formulas explained

All four formulas use the same basic shape. They take your height above five feet, expressed in inches, and multiply that excess height by a coefficient, then add a base weight. The differences between them are in the base weight and in the per-inch coefficient.

Devine (1974). Base weight of 50 kg for men and 45.5 kg for women, plus 2.3 kg for every inch over five feet. The Devine formula is the one most commonly used in clinical pharmacology and is the formula many hospital dosing guidelines still reference.

Robinson (1983). A revision that used data from a broader population sample. Base weight of 52 kg for men and 49 kg for women, plus 1.9 kg for men and 1.7 kg for women for every inch over five feet. The Robinson formula usually produces a slightly lower figure than Devine for taller people.

Miller (1983). Published alongside Robinson as a different fit to a similar dataset. Base weight of 56.2 kg for men and 53.1 kg for women, plus 1.41 kg and 1.36 kg respectively per inch over five feet. The Miller formula tends to give a higher floor than the others but rises more slowly with height.

Hamwi (1964). The oldest of the four and originally a quick bedside estimate for clinical use. Base weight of 48 kg for men and 45.5 kg for women, plus 2.7 kg and 2.2 kg per inch over five feet. Hamwi usually sits between the Miller floor and the Devine top end.

All four formulas use inches because they were developed in the United States, and all four were fitted using predominantly European and North American sample populations. That is a limitation worth remembering.

Every input and every output

The tool has two inputs, and both matter.

Height. Entered in centimetres. The formula internally converts centimetres to inches at the standard ratio of one inch to 2.54 centimetres, then subtracts sixty inches to find how far above five feet you are. If your height is below 152.4 centimetres, the formulas were not designed to produce a meaningful result and the tool will warn you. If your height is above 210 centimetres, the results are still produced but should be read with care, because the original study populations did not include many people at that height.

Formula category. The dropdown selects male or female. This is not a question about identity or gender expression. It is a technical choice about which set of coefficients to apply, because the original studies published separate equations for male and female subjects. If your body does not obviously fit either category, the honest approach is to try both and use the outputs as a rough comparison rather than a personal target.

Four outputs come back. Each one is a reference weight in kilograms, and each is also converted to pounds using the standard 2.20462 factor. The comparison range shown at the top is the lowest and highest of the four, and the table at the bottom also shows the equivalent BMI for each formula result, so you can see how far above or below the healthy BMI band each number sits.

A worked example at 175 cm

Take someone who is 175 centimetres tall and selecting the male category. The height in inches is 175 divided by 2.54, which is approximately 68.9. Subtracting 60 gives 8.9 inches above five feet.

Applying the four formulas: Devine gives 50 plus 2.3 times 8.9, which is 70.5 kg. Robinson gives 52 plus 1.9 times 8.9, which is 68.9 kg. Miller gives 56.2 plus 1.41 times 8.9, which is 68.7 kg. Hamwi gives 48 plus 2.7 times 8.9, which is 72.0 kg. The comparison range is 68.7 to 72.0 kg, which is a spread of just over three kilograms.

Now convert each to pounds: Devine is about 155.4 lb, Robinson is about 151.9 lb, Miller is about 151.5 lb, and Hamwi is about 158.7 lb. The BMI equivalent of each sits between roughly 22.4 and 23.5, all comfortably inside the WHO healthy band of 18.5 to 24.9. If the same person had a heavier training load, their actual healthy weight might sit above all four of these reference figures, and that would not indicate a problem.

Now suppose the same height is entered with the female category. The devine result becomes 45.5 plus 2.3 times 8.9, which is 66.0 kg. Robinson gives 49 plus 1.7 times 8.9, which is 64.1 kg. Miller gives 53.1 plus 1.36 times 8.9, which is 65.2 kg. Hamwi gives 45.5 plus 2.2 times 8.9, which is 65.1 kg. All four sit inside the healthy BMI range, and the spread between them is under two kilograms.

The spread is the point. Four formulas built from four different study populations give four different answers to the same question. If one of them were correct and the others wrong, we would know which to use. We do not, which is why the calculator shows all four and does not recommend a specific one.

Where the formulas came from

The Hamwi formula was published in 1964 as a quick bedside method for estimating appropriate drug doses in hospital patients. It was not the product of a large epidemiological study; it was a clinical rule of thumb that spread because it was easy to remember and gave a usable answer fast. The Devine formula followed in 1974 with a similar purpose and slightly different coefficients, and it became the standard reference in clinical pharmacology textbooks for the next few decades.

Robinson and Miller were published together in 1983 as an update to the earlier formulas. The authors noted that Hamwi and Devine had been built from relatively narrow populations and produced estimates that drifted from the actual weights observed in a broader cross-section of adults. Robinson and Miller used a larger dataset and fitted new coefficients, though they kept the same basic linear shape as the earlier formulas. Neither formula is more correct than the others in an absolute sense. They are simply fitted to different data.

A more recent effort published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition in 2016 tried to produce a single equation applicable across body mass index values. That equation has not replaced the older formulas in clinical practice, largely because practitioners are trained on the older ones and because the differences between them are small enough that they do not usually change a clinical decision. For the purpose of a general weight reference, any of the four is as useful as any other.

Why the formulas disagree

Three things explain most of the disagreement between the four formulas.

The first is the study population. Hamwi and Devine were built from relatively small samples in the mid-twentieth century, and those samples were not broadly representative of the full range of adult heights and body types. Robinson and Miller used larger and slightly more diverse samples, but they too were limited to the populations available at the time. None of the four formulas was built with a globally representative sample, and none was validated against Asian, African, or Latin American populations specifically.

The second is the shape of the equation itself. All four formulas are linear: they add a fixed amount per inch of height. But real human bodies are not linear. Average weight rises more steeply with height at the lower end of the height range and flattens out at the upper end, roughly following a curve rather than a straight line. A linear fit will always be slightly wrong at the extremes of the height range, and the four formulas fit those extremes differently, which is why they diverge more at very tall and very short heights.

The third is the purpose. A formula designed for drug dosing needs to err on the side of a lower estimate, because the harm from overdosing a patient is greater than the harm from a slightly low dose. A formula designed to describe a population average weight would err in the opposite direction. Each of the four formulas carries its original purpose somewhere in its coefficients, and that purpose shapes the numbers they produce.

Reading the result sensibly

Three habits make the output of this calculator more useful and less likely to cause harm.

Treat it as context, not a target. The numbers are reference points from historical formulas, not personal goals. If your current weight sits well above or below all four figures, that is a fact about where you are relative to a historical reference, not a statement about your health. A long, stable weight history outside the range is a different situation from a sharp recent change, and both deserve a doctor's attention rather than a calculator's verdict.

Look at the range, not one formula. Pick one formula and you can convince yourself that any weight is "correct" by choosing the one that matches. Look at all four together and the honest picture emerges: there is a band within which a healthy weight for your height is likely to sit, and the precise edges of that band are not something any formula can determine for you individually. The healthy BMI range shown in the table is a wider and more public-health-grounded band for the same reason.

Bring it to a conversation, not a decision. If the result prompts a question, write that question down and take it to a doctor or dietitian. They can look at the whole picture, including the parts a height-based formula cannot see, and help you decide whether anything needs to change. The calculator prepares the question. It cannot answer it.

Common mistakes with ideal weight formulas

Seven mistakes come up repeatedly when people work with these formulas, and each one is worth knowing about before you take any of the outputs seriously.

Treating one formula as authoritative. Choosing Hamwi because it gives a lower number than Devine is not a scientific choice. It is a preference dressed up as one. The four formulas exist because none of them is decisively better than the others.

Ignoring muscle mass. A person who trains seriously will usually weigh more than any of the four formulas suggest, and that extra weight may be entirely muscle. If you are physically active and your weight sits above the range, that is not a signal to lose weight. It is a signal that a height-based formula is not the right tool for you.

Applying the formulas to children. None of the four was designed for anyone under eighteen. Children grow on a different schedule and are assessed against age and sex specific growth charts. Applying an adult formula to a growing child will almost always produce a misleading result.

Reading the kilogram figure as an exact target. The formulas give a value with one decimal place, which looks precise but is not. The precision of the arithmetic is much higher than the precision of the underlying research. Round to the nearest kilogram and treat the number as a direction rather than a destination.

Ignoring the healthy BMI range in the table. The BMI range shown alongside each formula is a public health reference that is easier to interpret than an isolated weight figure. If the formula result sits near the edge of the healthy BMI band, the two references are telling you slightly different things, and that discrepancy is worth noticing.

Assuming the male and female categories describe personal identity. They are formula coefficients, not categories of personhood. If you are not sure which to use, try both and compare. The results are close enough that the choice rarely changes the overall picture.

Using the output to justify extreme action. A reference weight is not a reason to begin a very low calorie diet, to purge, to over-exercise, or to skip meals. If a number from a historical formula is pushing you towards any of those behaviours, that is a signal to stop using the tool and to speak to a qualified professional, ideally someone with experience in eating behaviour and body image.

Where this tool stops

This calculator compares four historical formulas and shows the healthy BMI range for your height. That is the full extent of what it does. It does not know your muscle mass, your bone density, your age, your training load, your ancestry, your medical history, or your medications, and it does not try to guess any of them.

It also does not diagnose underweight, overweight, obesity, or any other condition. It does not prescribe a calorie target, a diet, an exercise plan, a medication dose, or a treatment. The formulas it uses are population-level references that were built for specific clinical purposes and are useful in those contexts. They are not a plan for any individual person, and no amount of additional calculation on this page would make them one.

What the page does do is make the disagreement between the four formulas visible, so that the number that lands in your hands is honest about what it is and what it is not. If you have a medical condition that affects weight, if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, if you are under eighteen, if you are recovering from an eating disorder, or if you are managing the health of someone else, the right source for guidance is a clinician who knows the whole situation. The calculator prepares a useful comparison. It does not replace that conversation.

Frequently asked questions

Which ideal weight formula is the most accurate?

None of them is universally most accurate. Devine, Robinson, Miller, and Hamwi were each developed in a different era, for a different clinical purpose, and using data from different populations. Devine, for instance, was created for drug dosing in the 1970s and never meant to be a health target. This is why the calculator shows all four side by side rather than picking one. The disagreement between them is part of the answer.

Why does the calculator show a range instead of one number?

Because the four formulas rarely agree, and the spread between the lowest and highest result is usually three to seven kilograms for an adult. Showing that spread makes the disagreement visible. If a single formula gave one precise figure, it would give the impression of a personal target when it is really a historical reference point. A range is the honest way to present four population formulas.

What is the difference between ideal weight and healthy weight range?

The ideal weight formulas are historical reference equations built from height alone. The healthy weight range, on the other hand, is derived from the WHO adult BMI thresholds of 18.5 to 24.9 applied to your height. The two often overlap but they are calculated differently, and the healthy range is usually wider. If you want context from a public health reference, the BMI range is the one to look at. If you want a drug dosing reference or a historical clinical equation, the ideal weight formulas are the ones to look at.

Can these formulas be used for children or teenagers?

No. The Devine, Robinson, Miller, and Hamwi formulas were all developed for adults, and they do not apply to anyone under eighteen. Children and teenagers are assessed against age and sex specific growth charts, which plot height and weight percentiles across the full range of growth. Applying an adult formula to a child will usually give a misleading result. If you are concerned about a child's growth, see a paediatrician.

Why do the male and female versions use different numbers?

Because the original studies that produced these formulas collected data separately for men and women and fitted different coefficients to each group. The baseline weights are lower for women and the per-inch increments are smaller. This reflects statistical differences in the sample populations the studies used, not a claim about what any individual should weigh. The formulas cannot distinguish between a muscular woman and a slim man of the same height, and they do not try to.

Does the calculator account for muscle mass or body frame?

No. None of the four formulas looks at muscle, bone density, body fat distribution, or frame size. A person with a heavy training load will usually weigh more than any of the formulas suggest, and that extra weight may be entirely healthy. Frame size in particular is not something the formulas can detect. The outputs should be read as height-based reference points, not as individual targets.

How is the healthy weight range calculated on this page?

The healthy range is derived from the WHO adult BMI thresholds. Your height in metres is squared, then multiplied by 18.5 to get the lower bound, and by 24.9 to get the upper bound. Those two numbers define the weight band within which an adult of your height would have a BMI in the standard healthy category. The range is a screening reference, not a personal target, and it does not account for muscle, ancestry, or clinical history.

Should I use this to set a weight loss goal?

It is better not to. Weight targets based on a historical formula can push people towards unrealistic expectations, and the number on the scale is a poor measure of health on its own. If you are working towards a change in body composition, energy, strength, blood pressure, sleep, or how easily you move are usually better markers than a specific weight. If you do want a numerical goal, work with a doctor or dietitian who can look at your whole picture.

Is the calculator safe to use during pregnancy?

No. Pregnancy changes body composition, blood volume, and weight in ways that make ordinary adult ideal weight formulas unsuitable. Antenatal care uses a different set of references and personalised weight gain ranges based on pre-pregnancy BMI. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, follow the guidance of your midwife or doctor rather than a general purpose calculator.

Does this tool store my height or weight?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the height, the sex category, the four computed formula outputs, and the healthy weight range is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. No name, no email, no device identifier, and no history.
