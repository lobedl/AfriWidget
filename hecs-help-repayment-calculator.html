What HECS-HELP actually is

The Higher Education Loan Program, usually shortened to HELP, is the Australian government's income-contingent loan system for tertiary study. The idea behind it is simple. The government pays your tuition up front, you start repaying only once your income crosses a statutory threshold, and the size of each repayment is set by a sliding scale that rises with what you earn. There is no interest, no security, no guarantor, and no requirement to repay anything while your income stays below the minimum.

This is why Australian students often describe their student loan as the least stressful debt they will ever carry. There is no bank manager deciding whether to approve it, no monthly minimum that has to be met regardless of income, and no penalty for taking a low-paid first job while you find your feet. The trade-off is that you do not choose the repayment schedule. The ATO does, based on your annual Repayment Income, and the money comes out through your employer's PAYG withholding before you ever see it.

What has made the system more complex over the years is indexation. Because HELP loans carry no interest, the balance is instead adjusted once a year to keep pace with the cost of living. For a long time that adjustment tracked the Consumer Price Index. When inflation spiked in 2022 and 2023, indexation briefly climbed above 7 percent, and the resulting public debate led to the Universities Accord reforms that now cap the annual adjustment at the lower of CPI and the Wage Price Index. That cap matters because it links the growth of the debt to the growth of wages rather than the growth of prices, which is the more relevant benchmark for a borrower.

The loan categories and how they differ

HELP is an umbrella program, and the different categories exist to match different study pathways. The repayment rules, however, are the same across every category. Your loan type affects how much you borrowed, not how you repay it.

HECS-HELP is the loan attached to a Commonwealth Supported Place. In a CSP, the government subsidises part of the course cost and the student pays the remainder, known as the Student Contribution Amount. The subsidy makes a CSP the cheapest way to study at an Australian university, and it is the category most domestic undergraduates will encounter.

FEE-HELP is for domestic students paying full fees, typically at a private provider or in a postgraduate course that does not offer a Commonwealth Supported Place. The loan covers the full tuition amount, which can be substantially higher than the student contribution on a CSP. The repayment mechanics are identical.

VET Student Loans sit under the same umbrella but fund vocational training rather than university degrees. Diploma, advanced diploma, and a small set of graduate certificates are the usual targets. The loan caps per course are set by the government and reviewed periodically.

SA-HELP lets you defer the Student Services and Amenities Fee, the charge universities levy for non-academic services such as counselling, sport, and student societies. It is a small loan relative to the others, but it still sits in the same balance and is repaid on the same scale.

OS-HELP funds a period of overseas study for Commonwealth supported students. It is a separate small loan with its own limit, repaid through the same system. If you took out OS-HELP alongside a HECS-HELP loan, the two balances are combined in your ATO record and repaid together.

Repayment Income, in plain words

The ATO does not calculate your repayment on your taxable income alone. It uses a broader figure called Repayment Income, which adds five items to your taxable income before working out the rate. This is where most of the confusion about HELP repayments originates, because a person can find their rate higher than the salary alone would suggest.

The five add-backs are straightforward once you see them listed. The first is reportable fringe benefits, which is the grossed-up value of perks like a novated car lease or employer-provided accommodation. The second is your total net investment loss, which includes rental property losses from negative gearing. The third is reportable employer superannuation contributions, essentially any salary sacrifice above the statutory guarantee. The fourth is exempt foreign employment income. The fifth is any other amount the tax law specifies, which in practice rarely applies to most people.

The reason the ATO uses this broader figure is that the simpler definition would allow employees on high incomes to reduce their repayment by packaging more of their salary into fringe benefits or by claiming large investment losses. Broadening the base closes that gap. If you have any of these items in your own tax situation, entering them into the advanced fields on the calculator gives a more accurate rate than the basic salary field alone. If none apply, leave those fields empty and the calculation stays on the simple path.

The ATO repayment scale explained

The ATO repayment scale is a set of income brackets, each with its own rate. The rate for a given bracket applies to your whole Repayment Income, not just the portion above the bracket floor. This is the detail that catches people out, because it means a small increase in salary can push you into a higher bracket and lift your repayment by more than the increase itself in a given year.

Below the minimum threshold, no compulsory repayment applies. The current floor is $54,435. Between that floor and the next bracket, the rate is 1 percent. It then steps upward through a series of brackets, reaching 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, and so on, with the top rate of 10 percent applying once Repayment Income reaches $159,664. There are nineteen brackets in total, and the rate moves in half-percentage-point steps in the middle of the range.

The practical consequence is worth stating plainly. If your Repayment Income sits just above a bracket boundary and your employer offers you a raise that keeps you in the same bracket, the repayment grows in proportion. If the raise pushes you into the next bracket, the repayment grows slightly faster than the salary increase for that year. Over the long run you are still better off with the higher salary, because the increase in repayment is a fraction of the increase in income, but the immediate effect can feel uncomfortable. The full bracket table is printed on the ATO website, and the calculator uses it directly.

How indexation works and why the rules changed

Indexation is the annual adjustment applied to any HELP balance that has been unpaid for more than eleven months. It is applied on 1 June each year, using the previous year's published figures. The reason for the eleven-month rule is that student contributions accrued in the current year are not indexed in that same year, which gives recent borrowers a short window before the adjustment starts.

Until the 2024 reforms, indexation tracked the Consumer Price Index. When CPI rose sharply in 2022 and 2023, indexation rates climbed into the 7 percent range and student debt balances grew faster than average wages. Public pressure led to a legislated change, and the indexation rate is now capped at the lower of CPI and the Wage Price Index. In practice, this means the debt can only grow at a rate no faster than average earnings, which keeps the burden from rising in real terms simply because of a temporary inflation spike.

Two other mechanics are worth knowing. First, indexation is applied to the balance as it stood before the year's compulsory repayments have been credited, because repayments are processed when the ATO finalises your tax return, typically in July or August. Second, if you make a voluntary payment in the days before 1 June, it reduces the indexed balance directly, which is a legitimate and commonly used strategy in the weeks before the indexation date.

Why the timing matters. A voluntary payment made on 25 May reduces the balance that will be indexed on 1 June. The same payment made on 5 June will not. If you are considering a voluntary lump sum, do it before the end of May, not after.

A worked example from start to finish

Take an engineer who graduated from a Bachelor of Engineering with an accumulated HECS-HELP balance of $44,000. She is now working in Sydney on a starting salary of $78,000, paid fortnightly, with no fringe benefits, no investment losses, and no reportable super contributions beyond the standard guarantee. Her Repayment Income is therefore $78,000, the same as her gross salary.

On the ATO scale, $78,000 sits in the $74,856 to $79,346 bracket, which carries a rate of 3.5 percent. Her annual compulsory repayment is 3.5 percent of $78,000, which is $2,730. Paid fortnightly, that is $105.00 per fortnight withheld from her paycheck on top of her normal PAYG tax.

Now suppose the calculator projects forward with a salary growth rate of 3 percent a year and an indexation rate of 3.2 percent. In the first year, the balance of $44,000 is indexed by 3.2 percent to $45,408 before the repayment is credited. The $2,730 repayment then brings the balance back to roughly $42,678. In the second year the salary rises to $80,340, the rate steps up to 4 percent because the new income sits in the next bracket, and the repayment rises to $3,213.60. The pattern continues, with the salary and the balance both adjusting each year, until the debt is extinguished somewhere around year twelve or thirteen. The exact year depends on how much the salary rises and how high indexation runs over the period.

What the projection makes visible is the interaction between the two growth rates. When wage growth sits above indexation, the balance falls faster because the repayment grows quicker than the balance does. When indexation briefly exceeds wage growth, which happened during the recent inflation spike, the balance can stall or even rise despite steady repayments. That is the scenario the Universities Accord reforms were designed to prevent, and the current cap makes it unlikely to recur in normal conditions.

Voluntary repayments — when they help, when they do not

The question of whether to pay extra on top of the compulsory rate comes up often, and the honest answer is that it depends on what else the money could be doing. HELP debt is unusual among debts because it carries no interest and because the repayment schedule is income-contingent. That combination makes it a low-priority target for spare cash in most situations.

The clearest case against voluntary payments is when other debts carry higher effective costs. Credit cards, personal loans, and car finance all sit well above the current indexation rate. Paying those down first produces a guaranteed saving that no HELP strategy can match. High-interest savings accounts and mortgage offset accounts also offer returns that exceed indexation, and money parked in an offset is not locked up the way a voluntary HELP payment is.

The clearest case for a voluntary payment is when you are applying for a mortgage. Australian lenders treat the compulsory HELP deduction as an ongoing liability when they calculate your serviceability, and depending on your balance and salary, this can reduce your maximum borrowing amount by a substantial margin. If you are close to clearing the balance and are planning to apply for a home loan within the next six to twelve months, paying off the remainder can lift your borrowing power by more than the payment itself. A licensed mortgage broker can model the exact difference for your situation.

There is also a timing argument for anyone who happens to hold spare cash in late May. A voluntary payment made before 1 June reduces the indexed balance directly, effectively saving the indexation rate on that amount. This is a small, mechanical advantage rather than a strategy in itself, and it only makes sense if you were already planning to make the payment.

Common mistakes and misunderstandings

Several mistakes come up repeatedly when people estimate their own repayments, and each of them is easy to avoid once you know it is there.

Assuming the rate applies only to the portion above the threshold. It applies to the whole Repayment Income. This single misunderstanding accounts for most of the gap between what people expect to pay and what the ATO actually deducts.

Ignoring fringe benefits and investment losses. Reportable fringe benefits and net rental losses are added back before the rate is set. Someone with substantial salary packaging or a negatively geared property can find their rate a bracket or two higher than their salary alone would suggest. The advanced fields on this calculator exist precisely for that.

Expecting the tax withheld to clear the debt immediately. The extra PAYG your employer withholds is held by the ATO as a credit until your annual tax return is processed. The balance is credited only when the Notice of Assessment is issued, usually in July or August. In the meantime, the June balance has already been indexed.

Comparing your rate with a colleague on a different salary. Because the rate depends on the bracket, two people on similar salaries can have visibly different take-home pay if one is just above a bracket boundary and the other just below. That difference is not a mistake in either payroll system. It is how the sliding scale is designed.

Forgetting that overseas income counts. If you live outside Australia for more than 183 days in any twelve-month period, you are required to report your worldwide income to the ATO and make a repayment equivalent. The calculation follows a different process to the domestic one, but the obligation itself does not disappear.

Treating the projection as a forecast. The calculator projects forward using the assumptions you choose. Real wage growth varies year by year, indexation moves with the published CPI and WPI figures, and your career may take a path the model cannot see. Use the projection as a planning aid, not a promise about a specific payoff date.

Where this tool stops

This calculator estimates compulsory repayments using the published ATO brackets and a simplified projection of salary and indexation. It does not replace a tax return, an accountant's advice, or a formal statement from the ATO about your balance.

It also does not model every nuance of the HELP system. It does not handle the special overseas repayment calculation, the transitional rules for people who studied before 2020, or the interaction between HELP and other government benefits. It does not know about the small number of employers who report to the ATO on a non-standard cycle. And it does not adjust for the fact that your salary in any given year is unlikely to grow at a smooth rate, which the year-by-year projection assumes for simplicity.

The tool is also not financial advice. It will not tell you whether to make voluntary payments, whether to prioritise your mortgage over your student loan, or whether to refinance anything. Those are decisions that depend on your whole financial picture, and a licensed adviser or mortgage broker is the right person to help with them. What the calculator can do is give you a clear, honest, defensible estimate of what the ATO will deduct from your pay this year and how the balance is likely to move over the next decade. That is enough to make a decision from, and it is more than most people have before they run the numbers.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between HECS-HELP and FEE-HELP?

HECS-HELP is the loan attached to a Commonwealth Supported Place, where the government subsidises part of the tuition and the student contributes the rest. FEE-HELP is the loan available to domestic students paying full fees for a course that does not offer a Commonwealth Supported Place. Both loans are repaid on the same ATO sliding scale, and both use the same Repayment Income definition. The repayment maths does not change based on which loan type you hold.

Does my HECS debt have interest?

No. HELP debts do not carry commercial interest. Instead, an unpaid balance is indexed on 1 June each year to keep pace with the cost of living. Under the Universities Accord reforms, the indexation rate is now capped at the lower of the Consumer Price Index and the Wage Price Index, so a sudden spike in inflation cannot push student debt past the growth in average wages.

Why does my repayment rate apply to my whole income and not just the portion above the threshold?

That is how the ATO scale is designed. Once your Repayment Income reaches a bracket, the rate for that bracket applies to your entire Repayment Income. Crossing into a higher bracket does not mean you take home less money, because the increase in repayment is smaller than the increase in income that triggered it. It does mean the effective marginal cost of a small pay rise can feel noticeable, which is why the bracket boundaries are worth understanding before you accept a raise.

What counts as Repayment Income for HELP purposes?

Repayment Income is broader than your taxable salary. It adds reportable fringe benefits, total net investment losses (including negative gearing on rental property), reportable employer superannuation contributions above the statutory guarantee, and exempt foreign employment income. The ATO does this so that employees cannot lower their compulsory repayment through salary packaging or investment losses.

I live overseas. Do I still have to repay my HELP debt?

Yes. If you are overseas for more than 183 days in any twelve-month period and your worldwide income is above the minimum repayment threshold, you are required to report that income to the ATO and make an equivalent repayment. The calculation is not identical to the domestic one, but the principle is the same: the loan follows the borrower, not the borrower's tax residency.

Should I make voluntary payments on top of my compulsory repayments?

Usually not while other higher-rate debts exist. Indexation sits at roughly the inflation rate, whereas credit cards, personal loans, and car finance carry far higher effective costs. The clearest exception is mortgage borrowing capacity. Australian lenders treat the compulsory HELP deduction as an ongoing liability when they assess your home loan, and clearing a small remaining balance before applying can raise your maximum borrowing amount by a meaningful margin. A licensed broker can run those numbers for your situation.

What happens if I move jobs partway through the year?

Your new employer picks up the HELP withholding from the tax file declaration you complete. The ATO reconciles the full year at tax return time, because your final repayment rate is based on your total annual Repayment Income, not on what each employer withheld separately. If one employer under-withheld and another over-withheld, the difference is settled in your Notice of Assessment.

Why does the calculator show my debt being indexed before my repayments are credited?

Two timing rules line up awkwardly. Indexation is applied on 1 June to any balance older than eleven months. Compulsory repayments, on the other hand, are calculated and credited only when the ATO processes your annual tax return, usually in July or August. That means the June balance is indexed before the tax year withholding is credited, and then the repayment reduces the indexed figure. New student contributions from the current semester are not indexed in that same year because of the eleven-month rule.

Does a HELP debt affect my credit score or mortgage application?

A HELP debt does not appear as a credit liability on Australian credit bureau files, so it does not directly lower your credit score. It does affect a mortgage application, because lenders subtract your compulsory HELP deductions from the income they count for serviceability. Depending on your salary and balance, that can reduce your maximum borrowing amount by a substantial sum. A broker can show you what the reduction looks like for your specific profile.

Does this tool store my salary or loan balance?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the loan balance, salary, repayment income, applied ATO rate, annual repayment, and years to payoff is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. No name, no tax file number, no email, and no history.
