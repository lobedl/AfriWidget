What this planner does

This planner turns a handful of numbers about your semester into a working picture of your week. You enter the points you are enrolled in, the number of active weeks in the semester, your weekly timetabled contact hours, your paid work hours, and the number of days you plan to study. The tool calculates your total weekly academic demand, your independent self-study hours, your daily study target, and your EFTS standing. It also generates a recommended time-block structure and a milestone checklist, and it can produce a printable PDF you can pin to the wall or hand to a study adviser.

The point of the tool is not to tell you a number you could not work out on your own. It is to make the arithmetic behind a realistic week visible before the semester gets busy. A student who registers for sixty points, sixteen contact hours, and fifteen hours of paid work is committing to roughly sixty productive hours a week. That is above the level at which most people can sustain their marks. The planner makes that trade-off obvious while there is still time to adjust.

Every calculation runs inside your browser. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored, and nothing is tracked. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the numbers on this page is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops.

The New Zealand points and EFTS system

New Zealand universities and polytechnics measure study load in two related units. The first is the point, which describes how much work a single course involves. The second is the EFTS, or Equivalent Full-Time Student, which describes a full-time study load in a way that funding and student support systems can compare across institutions.

The national convention, set out by Universities New Zealand and reflected in the funding rules used by the Tertiary Education Commission, is that one point corresponds to roughly ten hours of total student workload. A standard 15-point course, which is the typical size of a single semester paper at most New Zealand universities, therefore represents about 150 hours of work. Across a 14-week semester that is around 10 to 12 hours per week for that one course, including its scheduled lectures and its independent study.

Four 15-point courses in a semester come to 60 points, which is 0.5 EFTS and about 600 hours of total effort. Two such semesters in a year produce the standard 120-point, 1.0 EFTS full-time year. A full-time semester of 60 points usually works out to around 40 to 50 hours of total academic effort per week, which is why full-time tertiary study is best treated as a full-time commitment rather than something that fits around a busy work week.

The distinction between 45 and 60 points matters because of the StudyLink full-time threshold. A student who drops from 60 points to 45 points is still above the threshold in most cases, but the workload drops by 150 hours across the semester, which is roughly four full working weeks of study time. That reduction is often the difference between managing the semester comfortably and spending the last month catching up.

The academic calendar and how to plan around it

New Zealand universities run on a broadly similar academic calendar. Semester one begins in late February or early March and runs through late June. Semester two begins in mid-July and runs through mid-November. A short, intensive summer school runs through January and early February at most institutions.

A standard teaching semester is twelve weeks long, followed by a two to three week exam period. Somewhere between weeks six and eight, most institutions insert a two-week mid-semester break. That break is not a holiday. It is a non-teaching study period that catches students who treat it as a rest in exactly the way it is designed to catch them.

The practical shape of a semester for a student who wants to stay on top of the workload looks like this. Weeks one to three are for setup: downloading course outlines, entering assessment dates into the calendar, and confirming which papers are worth keeping. Weeks four to six bring the first round of internal tests and early assignments, and they are also the point at which the academic withdrawal deadline approaches at most institutions. The mid-semester break is for the big essays and lab reports, a short rest, and a consolidation of weeks one to six of lecture material. Weeks seven to twelve bring the final internal submissions and the beginning of past-paper practice. The exam period itself is best treated as a fixed block rather than something that can be absorbed into the rest of the semester.

The StudyLink full-time rule and why it matters

StudyLink, the government agency that administers the Student Allowance and the living costs component of the Student Loan, requires students to be enrolled at least 0.8 EFTS over a full academic year to qualify for the full-time rates. In practice that means 96 to 120 points across the year, or roughly 45 to 60 points in a single semester. Anything below that threshold, without an approved Limited Full-Time exception, results in suspended weekly payments.

There is a second condition worth knowing. To keep future StudyLink support, a student has to pass at least half of the courses they were enrolled in during the previous period of study. Failing more than half of a semester's papers, whether by withdrawing too late or by failing them outright, can affect eligibility for the following year. That is why the withdrawal deadline, rather than the exam date, is often the most important date in a student's calendar.

The threshold is a floor, not a target. Enrolling at exactly 0.8 EFTS to preserve StudyLink eligibility is a valid reason to stay above the line, but it is not a reason to take on more work than you can realistically handle. A realistic load of 45 points completed at a good standard will always beat a 60-point load that ends in two failed papers and a suspended allowance.

Balancing study and part-time work

Most New Zealand students work while they study, and the research on the topic is consistent. Paid work of around 10 to 15 hours per week has little measurable effect on grades and can help with the budget. Beyond 15 hours, and particularly beyond 20, the pattern starts to shift: assignment deadlines slip, exam preparation gets compressed, and the marks slide quietly rather than dramatically. The reason is arithmetic rather than attitude. A full-time academic load already consumes 40 to 50 hours per week. Adding 20 hours of paid work takes the total productive week to 60 to 70 hours, which is more than most people can sustain for more than a few weeks without something giving way.

If working more than 15 hours per week is unavoidable, the cleanest option is to reduce points rather than push the weekly total higher. Dropping from 60 points to 45 points frees up roughly 10 hours per week of academic time, which keeps the total productive week in the manageable 50 to 55 hour range even with a 20-hour job. The cost is a slightly longer degree, but the alternative is often a failed paper, a summer resit, or a full additional semester to recover from a paper that was never going to be passed at that pace.

A worked planning example

Take a second-year engineering student at the University of Auckland. She is enrolled in four 15-point papers for a total of 60 points, which is 0.5 EFTS. She has 16 hours of timetabled lectures, laboratories, and tutorials per week. She works 12 hours a week at a part-time software job. She plans to study six days a week and expects the semester to run for 14 weeks including the study break and exams.

The total semester workload is 60 points multiplied by 10 hours per point, which is 600 hours. Divided across 14 active weeks, that is about 42.9 hours per week of total academic effort. Of that, 16 hours are already taken up by timetabled contact, leaving about 26.9 hours per week of independent study. Spread across six study days, that is a daily target of about 7.2 hours, which is a realistic but demanding schedule.

Add the 12-hour work week and the total productive load becomes 54.9 hours. That sits just below the overload line the planner uses, which means the schedule is workable but not generous. If she adds a fifth paper, or picks up more hours at work, or lets a couple of weeks of independent study slip, the load tips into the overload band and the grades are the first thing to feel it.

The useful output of the planner in this case is not the total figure. It is the daily target of 7.2 hours. That number is the one she can act on. If a day falls short, the planner shows exactly how much time needs to be recovered elsewhere in the week to keep the semester on track.

Common planning mistakes

Six mistakes account for most of the semesters that go sideways for New Zealand tertiary students.

Treating the mid-semester break as a holiday. The break is a study period with a different shape. Students who use the first half for major assignments and the second half for lecture review arrive at week seven with a buffer. Students who use it as a rest arrive at week seven behind on both fronts.

Registering for 60 points and a 20-hour job. The combined load is 60 to 70 productive hours per week, which is not sustainable across a full semester. Something has to give, and usually it is either the assignments or the sleep.

Planning around the exam date and ignoring the withdrawal deadline. The withdrawal deadline is the real decision point. Withdrawing before it produces a Withdrawn mark on the transcript rather than a failing grade, which protects the cumulative GPA. Missing it locks the paper into the record regardless of the result.

Assuming contact hours are the workload. Contact hours are the visible part of the week. The self-study hours are the part that actually decides the grade. A student with 16 contact hours who does not budget the 26 hours of independent study will finish the semester unprepared for the exams.

Studying every day with no rest day. Six study days with one proper rest day is the pattern most students sustain across a full semester. Seven days a week works for a few weeks and then collapses, often right when the internal assessments are due.

Reading the planner output as a promise. The plan says what the workload is, not what the grade will be. Students who hit their weekly hours still need to study in an effective way, which means active recall, past-paper practice, and spaced review rather than passive reading.

How to build a weekly timetable that survives the semester

A timetable that survives contact with a real semester has three features: it protects the fixed commitments, it protects at least one rest day, and it makes the study hours visible enough that skipping them is a choice rather than an accident.

The way to build it is to start with the fixed blocks. Lectures, labs, and tutorials go in first because they cannot be moved. Work shifts go in next. Everything left over is available study time, and the planner's daily target tells you how much of it you need to actually use. If the available time is less than the target, the load is too high and something has to change before the semester starts.

The time-block pattern that works best across most New Zealand courses splits the day into three sessions. The morning block, roughly 8:30 to 12:30, is for the highest-focus work: new problem sets, laboratory write-ups, and any reading that requires real concentration. The afternoon block, roughly 1:30 to 5:00, is for scheduled sessions, tutorials, group project meetings, and the more collaborative parts of the work. The evening block, roughly 6:30 to 8:30, is for review, active recall, and planning the next day. This pattern respects the reality that the same subject is easier in the morning for some students and in the evening for others, while still giving the whole day a shape that is easy to defend when other commitments compete for the time.

Where this planner stops

This tool produces a workload estimate and a suggested structure from the numbers you enter. It does not know your institution's specific assessment weighting, the exact dates of your mid-semester break, or the internal deadlines that fall in any given week. It does not know your personal study speed, your commute, your family responsibilities, or the other commitments that shape a real week. The figures it produces are a planning aid, not a contract with the semester.

It also does not provide academic advice. If a paper is going badly, the right people to speak with are the course coordinator, the student learning centre, and the academic adviser in your faculty. The planner can tell you that the weekly load is too high. It cannot tell you which paper to drop, and it should not. Those conversations belong with the people who know your record, your degree requirements, and the specific circumstances of the semester you are in.

What the planner does provide is a fast, transparent, private estimate, together with a printable document you can take to an adviser or pin above your desk. That combination is enough to turn a vague sense that the semester is going to be hard into a specific, actionable picture of what the week actually looks like. From there, the choices are yours, and they are made with the arithmetic in front of you rather than hidden behind it.

Frequently asked questions

What is an EFTS and how many points is a full-time load?

EFTS stands for Equivalent Full-Time Student, and it is the unit New Zealand universities and the Tertiary Education Commission use to measure study load. One EFTS equals one full year of full-time study, which is normally 120 university points. A standard full-time semester is usually 60 points, or 0.5 EFTS. A course worth 15 points carries 0.125 EFTS. StudyLink uses the same framework when it checks whether you are enrolled full-time for the Student Allowance and the Student Loan living costs component.

How many hours should I study per week for a 15-point course?

In the New Zealand university system, one point represents roughly ten hours of total student workload. A standard 15-point course therefore represents about 150 hours of effort across the semester, and across a 14-week semester that works out to about 10 to 12 hours of work per week. That figure includes lectures, tutorials, lab sessions, required readings, internal assignments, and exam revision. Four 15-point courses in a semester add up to roughly 40 to 50 hours per week of total academic effort, which is why full-time study is best treated as a full-time job.

What is the StudyLink full-time threshold for a Student Allowance?

To qualify for the Student Allowance or the Student Loan living costs component, you need to be enrolled in at least 0.8 EFTS over a full academic year. In practice that is 96 to 120 points per year, or roughly 45 to 60 points in a single semester. If your enrolment drops below that threshold without approved Limited Full-Time status, StudyLink will suspend the weekly payments. Always confirm your exact enrolment with StudyLink before withdrawing from or dropping a course.

How do I balance part-time work with full-time study in New Zealand?

Universities New Zealand advises full-time students to keep paid work to around 10 to 15 hours per week. Above that, assignment delays and exam fatigue start to show up in the grades. If you need to work more, dropping to 45 points a semester keeps you above the StudyLink full-time threshold while freeing real hours each week, and it lets you spread the degree over a slightly longer period rather than risk failing papers. Whatever you choose, block your work shifts on the timetable before you block study time, so the study hours fit around the fixed commitments.

When should I use the mid-semester break?

Treat the two-week mid-semester break as a non-teaching study period, not a holiday. The first four to five days are best used for the major essays, lab reports, and internal assessments that fall due in the first weeks back. Take two to three days of proper rest in the middle, then spend the final days consolidating weeks one to six of lecture content and getting ahead of the first round of tests. Students who use the break this way arrive at the second half of the semester with a buffer instead of a backlog.

What is the difference between contact hours and self-study hours?

Contact hours are the timetabled sessions you attend: lectures, tutorials, laboratory practicals, workshops, and wānanga sessions. Self-study hours are the additional reading, problem sets, assignment drafting, lab write-ups, and revision you do outside those sessions. A total weekly academic workload of around 40 to 50 hours for a full-time load is normal, and the split between contact and self-study varies by subject. Science and engineering courses often have more contact hours, while humanities and law courses usually have fewer contact hours and much heavier reading loads.

Can I download my study plan as a PDF?

Yes. After you generate a plan, press the Download PDF button. The browser builds a clean, printable document on your own device and opens the print dialog, from which you can save it as a PDF or send it to a printer. Nothing about your plan is uploaded to any server for the download. The document includes your semester profile, weekly workload figures, the recommended time-block structure, and the semester milestone checklist.

Does this planner store my inputs?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the points, weeks, contact hours, part-time work hours, study days, weekly workload, EFTS, and StudyLink eligibility band is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. Your name, student ID, institution, and course codes are never collected because they are never asked for.
