What this calculator works out

Three numbers come out of this tool. The first is the projected balance at the end of the period you chose. That is what you would have if the account behaved exactly as the assumptions say. The second is your total deposits over the same period — your starting balance plus every monthly amount you added. The third is the growth, which is the difference between the two.

The growth figure is the one that tends to surprise people the most. In the early years of a savings plan, it looks almost insultingly small. In the later years, it becomes the largest single component of the balance. Understanding why that happens is the entire reason tools like this exist.

What the tool does not do is tell you which account to open, how much tax you will pay on the interest, or what inflation will do to the spending power of that final balance. Those are separate questions, and they are answered in the sections below.

How compound growth actually happens

Compound growth is the process of earning interest on interest. Each month, the bank or investment adds a small amount based on the balance that was in the account. Next month, that new interest is part of the balance, so the interest the following month is calculated on a slightly larger number. Run that cycle for many years and the results become surprisingly large.

Here is what the shape looks like in practice. On a plan that deposits 1,500 rand a month at 8 percent a year, over the first two years the balance grows mostly because of your deposits. The interest earned in year one is around 700 rand. By year five, the annual interest is closer to 3,500 rand. By year ten, the interest earned in that single year exceeds the money you deposited across all ten years combined. By year twenty, the interest is earning more each month than the deposit itself is adding.

That shift is what makes saving for the long term feel like it suddenly accelerates. It is not actually accelerating; the maths has been steady all along. It is that the balance has finally grown big enough for the interest on it to matter more than the deposits going in.

The formula, in plain words

Two formulas sit behind the calculator, and they are both simpler than they look.

For the starting balance, which grows on its own without any further deposits: Future Value = Starting Balance × (1 + r)^n. Here r is the monthly rate as a decimal (annual rate divided by 12, then divided by 100), and n is the total number of months.

For the stream of monthly deposits, which is called an annuity in finance: Future Value of Deposits = Monthly Deposit × [(1 + r)^n − 1] ÷ r. The same r and n apply.

Add the two together and you have the projected balance. That is what the calculator does every time you press the button, once per month for however many months your plan runs.

The reason the numbers feel unintuitive is that people's brains handle linear growth well but exponential growth badly. Doubling the time does not double the interest. It multiplies it by much more than two, because the balance that is earning the interest is itself much larger in the later years. A plan that runs for thirty years does not earn twice as much interest as one that runs for fifteen. It earns closer to three or four times as much.

A worked example using rands

Suppose you start with 20,000 rand already saved. You add 1,500 rand every month. Your account pays 8 percent a year, compounded monthly, and you leave it alone for twenty years.

Your total deposits over the period are 20,000 plus (1,500 × 12 × 20), which comes to 380,000 rand. That is the money that came out of your pocket.

The projected balance, using the formulas above, comes to roughly 935,000 rand. The difference between the two, about 555,000 rand, is the compound growth. In other words, more than half of the final balance is money you never deposited. It was earned by the account itself, mostly in the second half of the plan.

The year-by-year table in the calculator shows where that growth comes from. In year one, the interest earned is under 2,000 rand. In year ten, it is around 32,000 rand. In year twenty, it is over 66,000 rand in that year alone. That last year's interest is almost as much as five years of your monthly deposits combined.

Now change one variable. Run the same plan for thirty years instead of twenty. The deposits rise from 380,000 to 560,000 rand. But the projected balance does not go up by roughly a third. It goes up to around 2.36 million rand. The extra ten years added more growth than the first twenty years combined. That is compounding in one sentence.

Where to save in South Africa

South African savers have a reasonable range of options, and the right one depends on what the money is for.

Bank savings accounts

Standard transactional savings accounts at the major banks pay relatively low interest, often between 4 and 6 percent a year depending on the balance tier. They are useful for everyday money you want to access without penalty. For serious long-term savings, they are usually not the best home because the rate rarely keeps up with inflation.

Fixed deposits

You commit a lump sum for a fixed period, typically three, six, twelve, or twenty-four months, in exchange for a higher rate than a regular savings account. Rates vary widely between banks and move with the repo rate. In recent years, twelve-month fixed deposits have typically paid between 8 and 10 percent a year at the higher end of the market, though rates in the 6 to 8 percent range are also common.

Money market funds

Collective investment schemes that hold short-term, low-risk instruments such as Treasury bills and bank deposits. They tend to earn slightly more than a savings account with similar day-to-day accessibility. They are popular for emergency funds because the money is usually available within a day or two.

Tax-Free Savings Accounts

A specific South African product where interest, dividends, and capital gains are not taxed, subject to annual and lifetime contribution limits. You can hold cash, unit trusts, or ETFs inside a TFSA. The tax advantage makes them a strong choice for long-term saving, though the lifetime limit means you cannot treat it as your only savings vehicle forever.

Retail Savings Bonds from National Treasury

A government-backed product sold directly to the public, with terms of two, three, or five years. Historically they have offered rates above comparable bank products, and they are considered very low risk because the issuer is the sovereign. Interest is paid either monthly or on maturity, depending on the option you choose.

Unit trusts, ETFs, and direct equity

For long-term goals, investments that hold shares have historically delivered higher returns than cash, though with real volatility along the way. A diversified equity or balanced fund is a common choice for retirement savings and children's education funds where the money will not be touched for seven years or more.

The calculator does not care which of these you choose. What it does is let you see the effect of each rate so you can compare them fairly. Enter 5 percent, then 8 percent, then 12 percent, and see how far apart the projections land over your chosen horizon.

The tax you might pay on interest

The projection this calculator produces is before tax. In most countries, interest earned is taxable income, which means the balance you actually keep will be lower than the number on the screen. How much lower depends on where you live and what kind of account the money is in.

In South Africa, interest earned by an individual is taxed at the normal income tax rates, but the first portion is exempt. As at recent tax years, the annual exemption is 23,800 rand for people under 65 and 34,500 rand for people 65 and older. Interest above the exemption is added to your taxable income and taxed at your marginal rate. A person on the 26 percent marginal rate who earns 50,000 rand of interest in a year would pay tax on 26,200 rand of it, working out to roughly 6,800 rand.

Money held in a Tax-Free Savings Account is not taxed at all on interest, dividends, or capital gains, which is why that product is so popular for long-term saving. The trade-off is the annual and lifetime contribution limits, and the fact that you cannot claim back any contributions once they have been made.

Other countries handle this differently. The United States taxes interest as ordinary income at the federal level and often at the state level as well. The United Kingdom has a Personal Savings Allowance that allows a certain amount of interest tax-free. Canada taxes interest as regular income but offers registered accounts that shelter it. India has a deduction for interest on certain deposits but taxes the rest. Australia taxes interest as income but offers a low-income offset.

The calculation this tool performs does not include any of that. To approximate the effect of tax, reduce your annual rate of return by the percentage you expect to lose. If you expect to keep 74 percent of the interest earned after tax, reduce an 8 percent rate to roughly 6 percent. That is a rough adjustment, but it is often closer to reality than ignoring tax entirely.

Inflation, the quiet cost

A second reduction sits on top of tax: inflation. The projected balance is a future number of rands, dollars, or pounds. It tells you nothing about what those units will buy at that future date. In countries where inflation runs at 5 or 6 percent a year, the purchasing power of money halves roughly every twelve to fourteen years.

To think about real terms — the balance measured in today's purchasing power — subtract the inflation rate from the interest rate and enter the result. If your account pays 8 percent a year and inflation is running at 6 percent, your real return is closer to 2 percent. A twenty-year projection at 8 percent nominal might turn into something much more modest when you convert it to what it will actually buy.

This is not a reason to avoid saving. It is a reason to make sure that the rate you are getting is comfortably above the inflation rate that applies to you. Money parked in an account that pays 4 percent while inflation runs at 6 percent is quietly losing value even as the balance grows.

A simple rule for real returns. Take the rate your account pays, subtract your country's inflation rate, and that is your approximate real return. If that figure is positive, you are gaining purchasing power. If it is negative, you are losing it, even though the balance on the statement keeps going up.

Common mistakes with savings plans

A few errors show up again and again when people use calculators like this one.

Using an unrealistic rate. Entering 15 percent because it sounds good will produce a big number that has no relationship to reality. The rate you use should reflect the actual product you plan to save into. Savings accounts and fixed deposits do not pay 15 percent; certain equity investments might over very long periods, with plenty of volatility along the way.

Forgetting that returns are not smooth. A stock portfolio that averages 10 percent a year does not pay 10 percent every year. It might return 25 percent in one year and minus 15 percent in the next. The average is a long-run statistical figure, not a yearly guarantee. This calculator uses a constant rate for simplicity, which is fine for planning but worth remembering when you look at the projection.

Overlooking fees. Investment products charge fees. A fund with a 1 percent annual management fee does not sound expensive, but over thirty years it can eat a meaningful slice of the compounding. Where possible, subtract an estimate of the fee from the rate before you enter it, or check the fund's total expense ratio and factor it in mentally.

Stopping and restarting. The last years of a savings plan are the ones that do the most work. A plan interrupted at year eighteen to spend the money has already captured less than half of the eventual compounding. If the goal matters, treat the plan as something you do not touch until the target date.

Assuming the projection is a promise. It is a mathematical outcome of the assumptions you fed in. Change any of them and the projection changes. The value of a tool like this is not in predicting the future; it is in showing you which of the inputs matter most.

Frequently asked questions

How does this savings calculator work?

It takes a starting balance, a monthly deposit, an annual rate of return, and a number of years, then simulates the growth month by month. The result is the projected balance at the end, plus a breakdown showing how much came from your own deposits and how much came from compound growth.

What rate should I use for a South African savings account?

It depends on the product. A standard bank savings account usually pays between 4 and 7 percent a year. A fixed deposit or money market fund might pay 7 to 9 percent. A tax-free savings account often sits in the same range. A retail savings bond from National Treasury has historically paid more than a standard bank account for the same term. For equities, long-run returns have averaged closer to 10 percent before inflation, but they are volatile.

Why does compound interest feel so slow at first and so fast later?

Because the interest earned each month depends on the current balance. In the early years that balance is small, so interest is small. As deposits pile up and interest accumulates, the balance grows, and the interest on it grows too. Roughly half the total growth in a thirty-year plan tends to happen in the last seven or eight years, which is why time in the market matters so much.

Does the calculator account for inflation?

No. The projected balance is a nominal figure. It shows what the account balance will be in rands or dollars at the future date, not what those rands or dollars will buy. In South Africa, where inflation has historically been higher than in many developed markets, this matters a lot. To get a rough sense of real terms, subtract an estimate of inflation from the interest rate before you enter it.

Are interest earnings taxed in South Africa?

Interest earned is generally taxable, but there is an annual exemption for individuals (currently 23,800 rand per year for those under 65 and 34,500 rand for those 65 and older, as at recent tax years). Amounts above the exemption are added to your taxable income. A Tax-Free Savings Account is a separate product where interest, dividends, and capital gains are not taxed at all, subject to an annual and lifetime contribution limit.

Should I save into a bank account or invest in shares or ETFs?

The answer depends on your time horizon and how much volatility you can tolerate. Money you will need within two or three years is usually better off in a low-risk savings vehicle where the value does not swing. Money you will not touch for seven years or more has historically done better in diversified equity investments, though with real short-term swings along the way.

How much emergency savings should I keep?

Most financial planners suggest three to six months of essential living expenses, kept in an accessible savings or money market account. In South Africa, where unemployment is high and income disruption is common, some advisers recommend building towards the upper end of that range if your household depends on a single income.

Is the projected figure guaranteed?

No. It is a mathematical projection based on a constant rate of return, and real accounts do not behave that way. Interest rates move, investments rise and fall, fees are charged, and taxes are paid. Use the projection as a planning tool, not as a promise about what any specific account will return.
