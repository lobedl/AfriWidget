What this calculator actually does

This tool takes the annual income you want to take home and works backwards to the hourly rate your business needs to charge. It does that by adding back three things that sit between revenue and take-home pay: business overhead, tax, and a profit and risk buffer. The result is a target revenue figure. That figure is then divided by the number of hours you can realistically bill in a year, which is your billable hours per week multiplied by the number of weeks you actually work.

The output panel shows the recommended minimum hourly rate, the weekly revenue target at your billable hours, the annual revenue the business needs to generate, and the total billable capacity the calculation is based on. It also shows a small bench panel of what the rate would be at different billable loads, which is usually the most useful part of the whole tool. If a freelancer's rate comes out higher than expected, the bench panel almost always explains why: the assumption about billable hours was too optimistic.

Everything runs inside the browser. No client names, no bank details, and no income figures are sent anywhere. If the AI insight button is pressed, only an anonymous summary of the figures on the page is used, and the response is not stored.

Why a freelance rate is not a salaried rate

The single biggest mistake new freelancers make is comparing their rate to the hourly equivalent of a salaried job. A salaried employee's total cost to the employer is typically one and a third to one and a half times their gross salary once employer taxes, benefits, and paid leave are included. A freelancer carries every one of those costs personally. On top of that, a freelancer has no paid holidays, no paid sick leave, and no employer contribution to pension or health insurance, and their income is spread unevenly across the year.

The consequence is that the hourly rate a freelancer needs in order to match a salaried job is almost always higher, often substantially higher, than the hourly equivalent of that salary. A designer on a forty thousand salary is not simply earning forty thousand divided by two thousand and eighty working hours. They are earning that salary while their employer covers paid leave, pension contributions, insurance, and equipment. Take away all of those supports and the same take-home requires a rate between one and a half and two times higher than a naïve calculation would suggest.

A useful sanity check. If your recommended freelance rate comes out at only a small percentage above the hourly rate of a comparable salaried job, double-check whether you have accounted for enough weeks off, enough overhead, and enough buffer. The gap is usually much wider than most freelancers expect.

The three cost layers: overhead, tax, and buffer

Three layers sit between gross revenue and what you actually take home, and each one is a percentage in the calculator.

Overhead. This is the cost of running the business itself: software subscriptions, equipment, workspace or co-working, internet, phone, banking fees, accounting, insurance, and the tools of your trade. Overhead does not scale with client work the way a fixed salary does, so it has to come out of revenue before anything else. A typical solo freelancer's overhead runs between five and twenty per cent of revenue, though businesses that need expensive equipment or software can sit higher.

Tax. The exact treatment of self-employment income varies enormously by country. Some jurisdictions require provisional tax payments through the year, others tax the freelancer at their marginal rate on profits, and many add self-employment contributions, VAT registration thresholds, and withholding obligations on top. The calculator uses a single tax percentage as a planning allowance, which is useful for building a rate but is not a substitute for advice from a qualified accountant in your own country.

Profit and risk buffer. This is the money the business keeps so that bad months do not cut into personal pay. Freelance income is uneven, and a buffer covers the gap between when a client is invoiced and when they actually pay, the cost of an unexpected equipment failure, and the unpaid weeks when a project is cancelled or delayed. A buffer of five to fifteen per cent of revenue is standard. Without a buffer, the first slow month turns into a personal cash crisis.

Billable hours are the number most freelancers get wrong

The billable hours field is where the calculation usually fails, and it fails in the same direction every time. Freelancers enter the hours they would like to bill rather than the hours they realistically can, and the resulting rate looks comfortable while the actual bank balance tells a very different story.

Consider what a solo freelance week actually contains. Client work is maybe half of it. Proposals, discovery calls, and meetings with people who never convert take an hour or two. Marketing, social posting, and portfolio maintenance take another hour or two. Invoicing, chasing payment, bookkeeping, and general admin take an hour or two more. Learning new software, reading about the industry, and staying current is another hour or two. Equipment maintenance, backups, and IT housekeeping eat into the rest. A freelancer who sits at their desk for forty hours a week is doing well if twenty-five of those hours are billable.

The practical takeaway is that if you are not sure what your true billable percentage is, start with twenty to twenty-five hours a week and treat that as the upper bound. If you find after a month or two that you can genuinely bill more, adjust the number upward and the recommended rate will fall. If you find you are billing less, adjust downward and the recommended rate will rise. Either way, the number should be driven by your actual invoices, not by your intention.

A worked example

Take a designer who wants to take home forty-eight thousand a year. She plans to bill twenty-five hours a week, take four weeks off, allow fifteen per cent for overhead, twenty per cent for tax, and ten per cent for the profit buffer.

The working weeks are fifty-two minus four, which is forty-eight. Billable hours per year are twenty-five times forty-eight, which is one thousand two hundred hours. The three layers multiply together: 0.85 for overhead, 0.80 for tax, and 0.90 for the buffer. That product is 0.612. Dividing the forty-eight thousand take-home by 0.612 gives a required revenue of roughly seventy-eight thousand four hundred and thirty-one.

Dividing that revenue by one thousand two hundred billable hours gives a minimum hourly rate of roughly sixty-five. The weekly revenue target at twenty-five hours is about one thousand six hundred and thirty-four. A twenty-hour project with a hundred in direct costs comes out at roughly one thousand four hundred in the fixed quote, before any contingency for scope changes.

Now look at what happens if the same designer only manages to bill twenty hours a week. The billable hours per year drop to nine hundred and sixty, and the required rate rises to roughly eighty-two per hour. The business has not changed, the income target has not changed, and the overhead has not changed. The only thing that moved is the number of hours she can actually sell, and the effect on the required rate is substantial. That is why the billable hours field is the most sensitive input in the whole calculator.

Pricing a fixed project rather than an hour

Hourly rates are a planning tool, not a commercial strategy. Most clients prefer fixed quotes because they can budget against a number rather than watching a meter, and most freelancers prefer them too once they learn to price them properly. A fixed quote is built from four components.

Estimated hours. Multiply the hours the project will actually take by your minimum hourly rate. The key word is actually. The first estimate is almost always too low, because it counts only the work the client can see: writing the copy, designing the page, building the site. It forgets the time spent reading the brief, asking clarifying questions, waiting for feedback, revising, testing, and delivering the final files.

Direct costs. Anything you have to pay for in order to complete this specific project: printing, travel, licences, stock photography, subcontractors, or specialised equipment rental. These are pass-through costs and they belong in the quote in full, not absorbed into the hourly rate.

Contingency. Add ten to twenty per cent on top of the hours to cover the scope changes that will inevitably arrive. The client will decide halfway through that the colour palette needs to change, or that the third page really needs to become five pages. Without a contingency, every change is either an argument or a personal cost.

Value adjustment. Finally, look at what the project is actually worth to the client. If it is a small internal document, the price the hours produce is probably right. If it is a launch campaign for a product the client expects to sell for six figures, the value of the outcome is much higher than the hours, and a percentage of that value is reasonable. This is the hardest adjustment to make because it requires a confidence that many freelancers do not have at the start, but it is standard practice in consulting and in high-end creative work.

Common mistakes when setting a rate

Six mistakes come up repeatedly when freelancers work out what to charge.

Setting the rate from what the market will accept rather than what the business needs. A rate based on the lowest competitor's price is not a rate, it is a race to the bottom. The correct starting point is the minimum the business needs, and the market conversation happens after that floor is known.

Ignoring time off entirely. Freelancers who plan on working fifty-two weeks a year are planning to work fifty-two weeks a year, and they will burn out by month eight. Building in four to six weeks off is not generous, it is necessary, and the rate must reflect it.

Forgetting the non-billable admin time. Every invoice, every quote, every email to a supplier, every minute spent updating the accounting software is time the business is paying for. If the billable hours assumption does not account for that time, the rate will be too low.

Charging the same rate to every client forever. Rates should rise as experience grows, as the portfolio strengthens, and as the market for the service changes. A rate that was right in year one is almost never right in year four.

Not writing down scope. Most disputes about money in freelance work come from a mismatch between what the freelancer thought the project included and what the client thought. A written scope is boring and it takes an hour to prepare, and it prevents the kind of argument that ends a client relationship.

Treating the buffer as optional. The buffer feels like profit that could just be taken home instead. It is not. It is the reserve that keeps the business solvent during a slow month, and skipping it is exactly what puts a freelancer back on a full-time payroll in year two.

Where this calculator stops

This tool estimates the minimum hourly rate your numbers require. It does not tell you what a specific client will pay, and it does not account for the fact that some markets support much higher rates than others for the same work. It does not model multiple service lines, retainers, productised offerings, or the mix of hourly and fixed-price work that most mature freelancers eventually use.

It also does not model tax correctly for any specific jurisdiction. Tax rates, thresholds, registration rules, VAT or sales tax obligations, and self-employment contributions vary widely, and the calculator's single tax percentage is a rough allowance rather than a precise figure. If your income is significant enough that the difference between twenty per cent and thirty per cent matters, speak with a qualified accountant in your country.

Finally, the tool is not a substitute for the commercial judgement that pricing actually requires. The rate it returns is the floor your business needs. What you charge above that floor is a decision about positioning, client relationships, market conditions, and the value you deliver. None of that is in the calculator, and none of it should be. Use the tool to make sure you are not inadvertently funding your clients' businesses with your own savings, and use your judgement for everything above that line.

Frequently asked questions

How is a freelance hourly rate actually calculated?

You work backwards from the income you want to take home. That figure is grossed up to cover business overhead, tax, and a profit buffer, producing the revenue the business needs to generate. That revenue is then divided by the number of hours you can realistically bill in a year, which is your billable hours per week multiplied by the number of weeks you actually work. The result is the minimum rate you can charge without running at a loss on the year.

Why do I have to include non-billable time in my rate?

Because you cannot bill every hour you work. A freelancer typically spends between thirty and fifty per cent of their week on marketing, proposals, invoicing, admin, learning, and answering enquiries that never convert. That time still costs you money, so the hours you actually bill have to carry the whole cost of the business. A rate built on forty billable hours a week when you can only realistically sell twenty-five produces an unwelcome surprise at the end of the first quarter.

Should I charge the same rate to every client?

No, and you should not feel bad about that. A specialist rate for hard technical work, urgent delivery, or high-value outcomes is fair, and it is standard practice in consulting and creative industries. What matters is that your lowest rate still covers your floor. Use the calculator's recommended minimum as the rate you will never go below, and price up from there for scope, urgency, and value.

How do I price a fixed-price project rather than an hourly rate?

Multiply the number of hours the project will actually take, including research, communication, revisions, testing, and delivery, by your minimum hourly rate. Add any direct project costs such as printing, travel, subcontracted work, or software licences. Then add a contingency of ten to twenty per cent for scope changes, because projects almost always take longer than the first estimate. The result is the number you send as a fixed quote.

What is a profit and risk buffer and why does the calculator ask for one?

The buffer is money the business keeps so it can survive the bad months without cutting your pay. Freelance income is uneven, and a buffer covers the gaps between client payments, the cost of an unexpected equipment failure, and the unpaid weeks when a client cancels. A buffer of five to fifteen per cent of revenue is common, and it is a business reserve rather than a personal saving.

How should I handle tax as a freelancer?

Treat it as a planning estimate rather than a precise figure. Different countries handle self-employment income in very different ways, with income tax, self-employment contributions, provisional tax, VAT or sales tax, and withholding rules all layered on top of each other. The calculator's tax field is a rough allowance so the rate you derive is not wildly under-provisioned. For your actual obligations, speak with a qualified accountant in your jurisdiction, especially in the first year of freelancing.

How do I raise my rates with clients I already have?

Give notice, be clear, and expect to lose one or two accounts. The pattern that works is to send a short, courteous message at least four to six weeks before the change takes effect, explain the reason in one sentence if you want to, and hold the new number. New clients get the new rate immediately. Existing clients get one opportunity to accept or negotiate. If a client pushes back hard, that is information: it usually means they were never a good fit for the rate you needed anyway.

Is this the same as an hourly-to-salary converter?

No. An hourly-to-salary converter takes a rate and translates it into a yearly figure, usually for comparison against a salaried job. This tool works the other direction. It starts from the income you want to take home, accounts for the real costs and non-billable time of running an independent business, and tells you the minimum rate that produces that income. The two tools answer different questions and are useful at different points in a career.

Does the tool work for small studios and agencies as well as solo freelancers?

It works for any small service business that bills for time. A studio with two or three people can enter the combined billable hours of the team and the combined annual income target, and the calculator returns a blended minimum rate the business needs to charge. It does not model individual salaries or profit distribution inside the business, but it gives a useful floor for the pricing conversations the team needs to have.

Does the tool store my income figures?

No. Every calculation runs inside your browser tab and nothing is stored. If you press the AI insight button, only an anonymous summary of the currency, rounded income target, billable hours, weeks off, overhead, tax, profit buffer, and the computed hourly rate is sent to a small serverless function that runs once and then stops. No name, no client information, no account numbers, and no history.

Why does my recommended rate look higher than the rate I have been charging?

Because most freelancers, especially in their first two years, start from a rate they think the market will accept rather than a rate the business needs. The calculator does not care what the market will accept. It tells you the minimum rate your income target, tax, overhead, and profit buffer require. If the market for your specific service genuinely cannot bear that number, the honest answer is that the current business model does not support the lifestyle it is meant to fund, and either costs need to come down, hours need to go up, or the service itself needs to change.
